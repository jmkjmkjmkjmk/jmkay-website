The Approved Version

a novel · Jonathan Michael Kay

Thank you for reading some of this. It is a novel I have been working on for a while, called The Approved Version.

It follows Daniel Reiss, an American who arrives in Singapore in 2012 on a work contract he keeps meaning to leave, and the three years he ends up staying. His job is a kind of translation, for companies. He sits in the room where something real gets said, then he hands back the approved version that’s safe to say out loud. The book is about what a life like that does to a person, and about the one relationship he could never find the words for, and lost.

What you have here is a sample, not the whole book: a few chapters, enough to get a feel for the voice and what it is doing. They run in the book’s order, so there are gaps between them. That is normal for a sample.

If anything lands, or doesn’t, I would love to hear it. There is a box at the end — say as much or as little as you like. There are no wrong reactions.

— Jonathan

Prologue

Doha, 2019

Doha, 2019

My life had a man in it. His name was Arjun. We didn’t call it dating but that’s what we were doing and it was thoughtful and loving and ended too early, by what I called circumstance for years, when he was sent with his firm to London.

Partway through those years in Singapore, a television crew flew in to film my life, and when it came time to commit this story to the camera, they kept my apartment and the morning light that shone through the oversized windows; the pool at my complex and the shophouses that dotted older neighborhoods and the hawker centers that punctuated my daily life in the few moments I wasn’t working.

They took the man out, and that is the version that plays, cleanly, to this day.

It plays because the premise was simple: a man, newly arrived from New York, choosing between three apartments. Newly arrived in the broadcast’s version; in the other one, I had lived in the Fortuna Court flat for over a year already.

The morning they arrived, like every morning, a bird in the rain tree outside my window woke me before they did: a low, round, unhurried koo that climbed into a run of bright rising repeats, then dropped and started over from the bottom. I would hear it for three years and never once be able to name it. Never the bird, never the line in the field guide, never the word a local would have had for it without thinking. I knew the call and not the bird. That day I may even have hummed along with it.

Under the call was a lower sound, a moving truck pulling in some floors below Apartment #1 (mine). It had come for my furniture: in the version that could be aired, he was to live alone. Most people would call the difference reality. I can only call it another version.

Mine I am writing from a desk in a third country, one I had said yes to before the offer was finished, under a hum you stop hearing inside a week. I have already told you what Arjun was to me. It took me until the first sentence of this account, and even then I did not use the word. There is a word. It is short, and people use it across dinner tables without lowering their voices, and I have written longer and more dangerous words into documents that lawyers signed. This one goes the way a foot goes off a curb you did not see in the dark. I could never set it down next to his name.

The country I met him in kept a law about men like us in a drawer it liked to say nobody opened; the country this desk is in keeps the same law. I looked it up before I signed, because looking it up is the job. So what I am writing is, in the place where it is being written, evidence. I write it anyway, at night, with the door locked, and the hum holds still while I do. When I stop for the night the pages go into the case with the client work, under the printed decks. A man who found them would have to read a while before he understood what he was holding. Nobody decided that; by the second night my hands were already doing it, and I watched them do it, and did not correct them.

The law is a true explanation. It is not a whole one. For more than a decade it was my one skill to find the word a room could use: the version that cleared legal, that a regulator would read and find nothing in, that a camera could air. I found it for hospitals, for a drug company’s lawyers, for my own mother. I could not find it for him.

He waited a while for me to. The waiting was quiet; it was a pause he left after other people’s sentences about us, long enough for me to step into, and I never stepped. Then he stopped waiting, and went, on a departure that had a date on it long before either of us would say a date was coming. For years I called that circumstance.

Two things are still in this room from that city. One is a phone that holds nine seconds of Arjun’s uncle talking, with his head tipped my way, in a language I do not have, kept whole these five years, never translated, though the same phone holds the thing that would translate them, and the whole transaction would cost one tap.

And this, the pages under my hands, is the other. I have written one account of a man before: a single entry, in the third person, mailed to a casting department. That is what this is: the one account I could never produce.

One

Production Week

Production week was filmed, which means there is a version of it you could still watch. This is the other one.

What wakes me is the call in the rain tree outside the window, the room already bright at the seams of the blinds. It starts low and climbs, each pass higher and more insistent than the last, until the bird is no longer making a sound but making sure of me. Then it stops. Then, from the bottom, it starts again. It has woken me most mornings since my first week in the flat. I have never once been glad of it, and I could not tell you what it is. Under it, when I hold still, is a lower sound — a vehicle the size of a small truck arriving in the lot below, exactly where, days ago, the production said it would. The truck is early, or I am late; either way the machine that has come to film me is already on its mark, idling, while I am still horizontal.

The telling goes back one night here. The morning does not stand up without it.

The night before the crew arrives there are drinks, and I have agreed, mostly reluctantly, to be the reason for them. Graham insists on Neil Road, on the smallest bar on that strip of shophouses, over every roomier and better-cooled option, because his fondness for a place runs inverse to its square footage. He was the first person at the firm to put a hand out to me, at the security desk on my first morning, and he has been putting things in front of me ever since: bars, opinions, people I have known longer than he has. British, boat-tanned year-round, and built on the assumption that no room has ever wanted him to leave it. Drinks and shots and the kind of room where the bar is the back wall and the crowd is one set of men who have eaten at the hawker center beside another set who will stumble there later to soak up the drinks they have already forgotten. The room runs hot. The ceiling fan turns at a speed that is purely ceremonial, and by ten everyone has rolled their sleeves back or done away with sleeves, and shirts, entirely.

By eleven the occasion has been thoroughly observed. A sentence strikes like a bell off the too-large disco ball and comes back to me: You’re going to be on TV!

“Daniel! What are your opening lines gonna be?” I have a line ready, of course; I am paid to have lines ready. But I am not yet drunk enough to give this room a performance the night before I owe one to a camera. “Well?” It is Graham, one hand flat on the bar, the weight of his jaw jutting into your space, enjoying the attention his handsomeness conjures. “So House Hunters International, mate,” he says, his voice pitched to unnerve me. “How’s our newest American celebrity going to introduce himself to the world tomorrow?”

“I don’t have a speech prepared. I’m reacting to apartments I’m seeing for the first time —”

“— the first time?! Are you getting new retinas before the filming?” Graham waits for the appropriate level of laughter to arrive.

I stumble. “— well, it’s – it’s the character’s first time seeing the apartments. I describe what the rooms look like, I voice my reactions — it’s really just a glorified apartment tour.”

Graham reaches for his glass at the stem and twirls it with surprising felicity. The movement pulls the shirt across his shoulders, emphasizing his well-defined chest. His eyebrows go up. A beat the length of a swallow. Then they furrow, slow, a whole brief conversation held with someone just beyond me, no words exchanged but the meaning couldn’t be clearer.

“Oh, honey,” Graham says. “They did not fly a crew to Singapore to film you describing a room.”

“They want the rooms described. There are cameras. People can see what it looks like.”

“They want the room sold. To you, by you, while you pretend the selling is news to you.” He is delighted. “You’re going to be on TV.“

“I produce things for a living,” I say. “I know how the sausage gets made.”

“Darling, nobody is questioning the sausage.” He sets his glass down with the care of a man laying flowers. “I’m questioning whether you’ve grasped that you are the sausage. They flew economy-plus across nine time zones for the look on your face when you walk into your own kitchen and feel something.” A finger up. “Authentically.”

“I can do authentic.”

“You can do produced authentic, which is worse, which is why they’ll love you.” He leans back and considers me. “I want you to picture it. Tuesday afternoon. A woman in Ohio, let’s call her Doris, has the iron going and the TV at near-maximum volume. Suddenly you pop up on her screen, walking through a flat you already live in, and marveling at the sheer height of the windows.” He gives marveling a small filthy laugh on its way past, entirely for himself. “Doris wholeheartedly believes your performance; that is the tragedy. She will believe you and you will know, in the moment, exactly how you made yourself believable. You will probably be disgusted at what you were part of, but Doris is a fan, and she’ll go on the forums and defend your decision to complete strangers.” Graham turns to the table at large. “He’s going to be insufferable about this. He’s going to come back Thursday saying things like eyeline, I’m calling it now. Mark it; somebody mark it!”

Shots arrive in a configuration no one will later admit to assembling. Arjun — nearly two years of him by now, the sleeves of the office shirt turned back the way they were the first night I clocked him across a room and turned back again tonight, the same decision made about the same cuffs — leans in close, his mouth at the side of my head, and says something warm and a little damp into my ear that I could not have repeated ten seconds after he said it. Some of it was not in English, and that part I could not have repeated at all.

His hand is flat on my back the whole time, between the shoulder blades, the heel of it warm through the wet cotton and the squash callus at the base of his thumb catching once on the weave, a steady pressure I lean back into without deciding to, and nobody at the table looks, because everybody at the table has long since stopped looking.

I have a clear and isolated memory of agreeing that I am going to be on TV, only now realizing the expectation that I play a character, convincingly, and this must register on my face, as the room’s attention returns to me with interest.

The night, already considerably loosened, seems on the precipice of becoming right and properly messy.

Somehow, a taxi arrives and Arjun and I hurl ourselves into the back. The driver eyes us in the rearview mirror for longer than is comfortable, but then refocuses on the road, suddenly determining that speed limits are optional after midnight. We pay the comically low fare and then tiptoe up the stairs, elaborately quiet and not quiet at all, Arjun’s hand pasted to the small of my back for balance, his or mine I can’t remember.

There is, somewhere in my head, the knowledge that a crew is coming at eight and that the responsible version of this night ended hours ago. I was supposed to use this evening to assiduously clean up before the cameras arrived. “This place is a mess,” I garble, to Arjun but also to the refrigerator.

He gets as far as the couch, the couch he has slept on exactly twice in two years, both times this drunk, and is out just as he slips his shoes off. With just enough dexterity to remove my sweat-filled clothes, I hop into bed in my briefs and leave what sweat is left to the sheets. In the morning we will each blame the other for the sleeping arrangement and neither of us will mean it. Nothing about it gets discussed, because there is nothing in it that wants discussing, and we are, by now, fluent in that. Nothing gets tidied.

My head takes the morning in installments. The apartment has the composition of a room several people used and none closed out. A glass on the floor stands upright and full, set down by a man who fully meant to carry it to the kitchen — which is somehow worse than broken. On the couch Arjun is asleep sitting up, more or less, one arm still in its jacket sleeve, the sit clearly meant to have been temporary. Through the window, the pool is a flat pale rectangle, no one in it yet. My phone, when the search for it concludes, carries the time, and beneath the time a message from the production company confirming what I have been told twice: they will be at the flat at eight. To shoot the apartment. They need it empty, and then, very quickly, full again, and they need me in it looking as though I have never seen it.

Twenty minutes. I run it again and it is still twenty minutes.

Down in the lot, a man in a cap is backing the truck toward the service entrance. The sky is the color it is at seven-forty in July, which is to say it is already fully day, the equatorial sun not arcing up from anywhere but simply present, committed, a fact since well before I was awake. The room smells of last night — spilled beer, the bar’s cigarette smoke that two of us must have carried home in our shirts — and under that, the specific sealed-apartment smell of recycled air and the small daily heat the building generates by being occupied. I am aware, with the sudden awareness of a person who has not slept well, of how my body sits in this heat: the shirt slightly damp already at the back, the headache a pressure behind both eyes rather than a pain, the dry-mouth that is part hangover. I drink the glass of water still standing full on the floor, and it is warm.

Arjun surfaces in stages, against his will, with a brief facial tell that a complaint will be filed. He takes in the ceiling, then me, upright on the bed in last night’s clothes, doing arithmetic at a wall, and then, with fresh dismay, the jacket he never finished taking off. He arrives at the morning in roughly the order I did — the wreckage, the hour, the arithmetic — and adds to it. “There’s a truck,” he says. There is a truck. He finds one shoe at once and approaches the second as a separate project. When he has both, he carries the glass of water from the night before to the tap, refills it, and puts it into my hands without my asking, the way you reach for a thing without looking.

“You’re a terrible influence,” he says, into the side of my neck. “I’m going to tell people.”

“You’re the one who ended up on the couch.”

“I’m the exhibit,” he says. “The couch is where they put the exhibit.”

“And you were doing so well unlabeled.”

“You’re the curator. One good piece and no idea what to write on the card.”

“I know what I’d write.”

“You don’t,” he says, pleasantly. “Remind me how many days they get you for.” It is not really a question; he has heard the schedule enough times to recite it. It is a way of making me say it out loud, a defined commitment.

“Three. A fourth if their rig won’t behave.” I run it against the ceiling. “Today’s the apartment. Walk in, marvel at the windows, say something appreciative about the light, pretend I’ve never seen the place. Tomorrow I ‘discover’ a neighborhood I’ve lived in for two years. Then a day of touring two units I’ll never rent and choosing this one, on camera, as if I hadn’t been asleep in it last night.”

“You’ll view your own apartment and be surprised by it.”

“And choose it in the end. That’s the format.”

“Playing yourself.” He is not quite smiling. “Really.”

“It’s not a big deal.”

“They’ve got me down for a piece of it, you know. Not the apartment. One of the outside things. A coffee somewhere, and maybe the gallery; I forget what they settled on. They sent an email with a lot of capital letters. RELEASE FORM. TALENT. CALL TIME, which is apparently seven, which is a thing they invented to ruin a Saturday.” He stands, phone already out. “It gets better. I am, it says, your FRIEND WHO KNOWS THE CITY. Capital letters, all five words.”

“You do know the city,” I say.

“Twelve years. I have a tailor. I have a regular order at two hawker centers and a feud at a third.”

“Then the caps are just true.”

“It’s a defined term. I checked. Somewhere under TALENT there is a box, and in the box there is me, warranting and representing that I know the city.”

“That’s what forms are for.”

“I’m going to use it at dinner parties. Someone asks what I do. FRIEND WHO KNOWS THE CITY. Licensed. Ask me anything.” Then he reads the line once more off the phone, flat, in the show’s own house voice, and for the length of the line his face forgets to do the joke. Then his mouth finds it again. “My one line is about a flat white. They will have flown a crew across the world to capture it. You have to try the flat white here.” He gives me the line twice, once warm, once confidential, and waits for notes.

He scrolls the email again. “Who writes these, do you think. Somebody wrote this. Somebody sat in a room and decided what I am.” “A committee,” I say. “It reads like a committee.” It reads like me. “No, but think about it,” and he is enjoying it now, building it the way he builds a bit, “somewhere there is a man who read about us. Some file, some form. He read the whole thing, and friend is where he landed.” And then he stops. Not the way a bit lands; the way a floor is not where the foot expected it. The flat is quiet for one beat, two. He looks at the phone as if it has said something further. I could put a sentence into the silence; I have the sentence; it is my job to have the sentence. I let the silence do its own work instead, and after a moment he sets the phone down and asks what we are doing about breakfast.

“You drink kopi,” I say.

“I drink kopi in my real life. On camera I am a flat white person. The kopitiam did not sign a location release. The café has good light, and that is where Singapore drinks its coffee, according to Ohio.”

“They’ll want it more than once.”

“They’ll want it nine times, and somewhere around the sixth I will stop recommending a coffee and start narrating a perfume ad.” He composes his face and gives me take six: “The flat white here is a revelation. Silken. A little poem of steamed milk. The beans are Ethiopian and the milk is practically a lullaby.” He drops it. “By the fourth the foam will be dead, someone whose entire job is the tray will bring a fresh one, and I will recommend the drink again, with my whole heart, to a man I have watched drink coffee for two years.” A small dry beat. “It’s Singapore. An American, on camera, even on the dumbest show on the dial — that’s a thing here. People will watch. People you know.”

I don’t have an answer shorter than the conversation, so I don’t open it. And the label is not false; that is the part I do not look at. He is my friend. He does know the city. The form is competently drafted, is the thing. Two true facts and a role in capital letters, and nothing that would slow the review chain: medical would initial it, legal would initial it under medical, regulatory would not even reach for the pen. I draft that sentence all day for other people. Accurate, cleared, incomplete by design; I could tell you the job number it bills to. It is the first time I let their version of him stand without a word from me.

At the door he takes my hand. The grip begins as a handshake and then does not proceed as one and does not release, holding for a count longer than a handshake needs and shorter than anything that would have asked to be named — our own thing, the one we keep for doorways and crowded rooms, that looks like nothing and that we both know is not nothing, his thumb pressed once into the soft of my palm where no one can see it.

Right then I go looking for the word that belongs under the touch, and nothing comes up, and nothing ever has. Below us, in the lot, the truck is reversing toward the building with the patient beeping of a vehicle that has all morning. He lets go on the sound of it. My palm stays warm; the word stays missing; and we are both, by now, easy with leaving it that way.

I stand at the closed door. His footsteps go down two flights and then I lose them. Outside the window a myna works the lip of the pool, stepping along the edge in its deliberate way. The myna I can name; I just did. The bird in the rain tree woke me again this morning, and a year of those mornings has not taught me its name, and the not-knowing itches now, a word held at the tip of the tongue that never comes the rest of the way. A blue rectangle of sky sits above the building opposite, a clean solid blue, the kind that means no rain before noon. The apartment is still and warm and mine, and in twenty minutes it will not be mine in the particular sense of looking like it: the furniture will be on a truck, and strangers will come and fill the rooms with their version of what a person who lives here might want. But right now it smells of last night and Arjun’s jacket and the rain-tree outside the open window, all of it still mixed, not yet sorted.

The heat comes up the stairwell behind him and lands on my face, wet, the held heat of the stairwell, and for a second I am only a man in a hot room who has not yet been asked to be anything or anyone in particular.

I shower in water that never quite runs cold. By the time I have found a shirt that is not the shirt from the night before, the two men from the truck are on the landing.

They come in without much greeting and count my furniture with their eyes; there is not a great deal of it to count. All this time in the apartment and I have a bed, a desk, a bookshelf with two of its shelves in, the other two leaning behind it, waiting on an errand to the lighting-and-hardware row on Balestier Road, an errand I keep scheduling and the office keeps eating, and a few things on the walls. They have all of it on the truck in under twenty minutes. What they do next I have not been told about. They begin bringing things in.

Not my things. A rug I have never owned. Two lamps. A low table, and on the table a stack of books arranged at an angle: I pick up the top one and it is a glossy survey of Peranakan shophouses, and beneath it a history of the city written in the warm, managing tone the city uses about itself. A bowl is set on the floor by the kitchen, the kind of bowl a dog eats from. I do not have a dog. A folded plaid throw appears on the back of the couch where none has been, slightly the wrong colors for it, arranged with the kind of precision the ones people actually use never get.

The kitchen they clear to the walls. On the counter there is a steel bowl with a plate over it, two days into its slow ferment, batter for a Sunday that is not on the schedule, and a mover lifts the plate, looks under it, puts the plate back, and carries the bowl down to the truck with the lamps. It sits in the truck all morning, in the dark, while upstairs I am filmed meeting my own kitchen. The schedule the production assistant carries has the whole day on one page, and on paper the day is a year: the morning block is called FIRST VIEWING, the block after lunch is called REVEAL, and in the white space between the two lines the show intends three months to pass. So at half past one the movers climb the stairs and reverse themselves. Down go the rug and the lamps and the bowl of the dog I do not have; up come the bed, the desk, the bookshelf. The two of them carried my bed down in pieces at eight and they carry it back up in pieces at two and rebuild it, and neither of them finds this remarkable, because neither of them is being asked to sleep in it tonight and believe he has been sleeping in it for months. They rebuild the bookshelf from a photo one of them took on his phone that morning, two shelves in, two leaning behind it at my exact angle, my unfinished errand to Balestier restored to the frame like evidence.

By three my life is back where it stood at eight, and at four I am filmed living in it: settled now, months in, easy in rooms I left that morning, admiring a ceiling I am supposed to have stopped noticing. The steel bowl comes up with the last load and takes its old place on the counter. I set the plate straight and do not lift it, that day or after. By Sunday it has stopped. Arjun lifts the plate off it and looks for a while at a thing that is not doing what it should. “It died,” he says, conversationally, and tips it out and washes the bowl and sets the next one soaking, and that is all that is ever said. The clipboard had listed it, I assume, as a bowl.

I sit on the arm of the couch and look at the room. Not a long look. The lamp they’ve set by the window throws the wrong quality of light: softer, warmer, a light a person would choose for reading in the evening, which is not what you do at seven-forty in the morning. The rug covers the scuff where the desk leg dragged on the laminate the first week, when I moved the desk to the wall. The Peranakan book lies at the angle you’d arrange it for a coffee table you meant to show someone, not a coffee table you used. I know all of this. I recognize every substitution. But the room I live in is gone past finding now, every true thing in it covered or moved or restyled for a viewer who will never arrive.

No one explains any of it, and I do not, in the end, ask, because by then a man has come in from the corridor with a clipboard.

He is American, mid-forties. The clipboard is not for holding information. He steps over his own crew’s cable and takes the apartment in slowly, already lighting it in his head. The ceilings go up two stories. The canal throws its light at the far wall as it has every late morning I have lived here, and I watch him find that light and decide what it is for. He reads the apartment with his eyes before he sets the clipboard down, and sorts it the way they sort any room: this part useful, this part furniture. The windows are useful. The canal-light is useful. My life in the place is furniture, and by the time the lamps are lit the useful things have cables running to them and the furniture gets carried down to the truck.

He says Beautiful. Then he sets the shoot — not by explaining it but by walking it, stopping where he wants me stopped, sweeping a hand at the kitchen to mean all of it.

You come through the front door, he says, behind your lovely realtor, and you stop here, in the kitchen. Fresh eyes. First time you’ve seen the place. The lavalier goes on under the shirt, the wire cold against the skin over the sternum — a thin cold line where the sweat has not yet started, the apparatus reaching your chest before anything else of it reaches you. The director has been saying you since the kitchen; from here it is the only name the shot has for me — for the length of the take, the only one I have. You have not seen this place. You have slept in it. You did arithmetic in it ninety minutes ago. But you have been hired to keep not-recognizing your own apartment, and through a low and even headache you find that you can.

The first walk-through takes eleven minutes. You say the things you have been given to say — oh, wow, look at this space; so this is the living area — and then, on cue, the question they want.

“And the rent here?” you ask the room.

“Two thousand even,” Stephanie says, without a flicker. She is the realtor the production has hired to be my realtor, small and composed and in her fifties, and she sits like a closer, very still, hands folded. It is a price for an apartment that is not this one, delivered at the cost of nothing. The figure on the lease is $2,850 a month, steep for one person on a mid-tier comms salary, which is what you are; the production has decided the man it is building lives on less, and so that man pays two even. It is the first number she invents for you.

You ask after the figure you signed for two years ago as though the answer might surprise you. It does not. The bed is up a short flight of stairs to the mezzanine the double height leaves room for; the single bathroom sits tucked beneath those stairs. You went looking for exactly that and nothing larger — one bedroom, one bathroom, a rent a single income could carry, a building with a name you could hand a taxi driver — and you found this, and signed for it, and were asleep in it that morning while a man on a phone arranged for the truck. The show means to spend two days proposing that you have not yet decided. The man the show is assembling does not seem to know he decided already.

He asks for the ceiling, the look up, and then more of it, and you give him more: the double height you have looked up at every morning you have lived here, more air than a person needs in a city that meters its air by the cubic meter. The sweat has started under the mic tape now and the lavalier pulls at the chest whenever you draw breath too sharply; a single bead runs from the hairline down behind the ear and you cannot lift a hand to it without breaking the shot. They want the face of someone meeting a room. They do not want the evidence, in the chest, in the breath, in the wet at the temple, of how long you have already been in it.

By the eighth take it begins to register, not as a complaint about him but as a noticing. He works by removing the words that would have told me what I was doing. He has not said act, and he is not going to. He never says again; he says one more, just to have it, as though the take were a thing already mine that he is helping me collect. When a take fails he never tells me it failed; he tells me I very nearly let it happen and then, at the last instant, went and did it on purpose instead.

The sound technician, between takes, lowers the boom and lets out a single short breath through his nose. Not a sigh. The breath you let out when a thing you have seen before is happening again. The director sees it land. “Don’t help him,” he says to the technician, mild, not looking up from the monitor. “Let him get there.” The technician had not appeared to be helping anyone. He catches my eye for the length of a frame, wipes the mic clean of me with a cloth from his back pocket, and does not appear to decide to.

“We watched your island tape last night,” the director says, to the monitor. “You were easy, on the island. Be easy.” Island tape. For half a second the phrase locates nothing — we are, after all, on an island, the whole country is one — and then it slides under before I can hold it, and we go again.

“You’re confirming it,” he says, a few takes on, in the voice of a man reporting the temperature. “You go straight up to the ceiling — you already know it’s there. What I want is the half-second before you know it, the look arriving on your face before you’ve decided to wear it.”

The heat is in my collar and the headache is behind both eyes and the only answer I have is the produced one.

“I’m finding it,” I say.

“You’re letting me watch you decide to find it. Find it before you decide to. Ideally, find it before there’s a you who could. The camera only wants the difference.” He nods once at his own sentence, a man who has heard himself say something. I spend the next take trying to locate the difference between finding a thing and deciding to find it, and fail, and the failure is, he says, the closest yet. Then he moves a light a few inches and goes on without telling me what was wrong with the last.

The phrase, when I let it land, is Pulau Ubin. Not Singapore, or Singapore the way an attic is the house: a smaller island off the northeast coast, a ten-minute bumboat from a jetty at the end of the bus network, old granite quarries gone to flat green water, the last of the kampong houses still standing in red dirt under the trees. The country has kept it mostly unbuilt, the way a gut renovation keeps one wall of original brick: something to point at, proof of everything it knocked down. A long Saturday last February, the production asked each candidate for a self-tape — film yourself, alone, say who you are and why you want this — and I carried a camera out there to make mine, because it seemed like the kind of place a person with a larger life would think to go. It has since become a tape.

Late in the day they turn the monitor toward me. It had been facing the director; now it faces me, and on it is Pulau Ubin in a wide gray light — the jetty, the tide low, the strait behind it the flat brown of weak tea — and a man sitting at the end of the planks in a shirt I have since given away.

The man is you. You set the camera down yourself, that February, and walked to your mark and sat, and did it four or five times until the framing held. There was a prompt, emailed: Tell us who you are and why you want this. You answer it into the empty air over the water. You say you are a person taking a chance. You say you wanted a larger life and went out and got one. You say you are glad, on the whole, to be exactly where you are — and as you say it you open one hand a few degrees toward the strait behind you, as though the strait were a thing you could offer in evidence. No one gave you the gesture. You found it alone, on a jetty, with no one there to be eased by, and it was good, and it was entirely yours. You say all of it well. You believe a workable amount of it.

The room is quiet for the length of the playback’s click.

You had it on the island, the director says, by way of context, and steps back so I can see.

They play it through, and the room watches it with me — the director, the cameraman, the sound technician, all of them angled at the small bright screen where a man on a jetty is being easy about his own life. He has it without reaching — that February, no one stood behind a monitor to tell him it was a thing a person could be asked for. The sentences are mine, every one of them mine, written by me, said by me, into a camera I carried out and set down myself. I watch him say them. Then the playback stops, and the room turns from the screen to me and waits for me to produce the description again, live, and matching. On the screen the man on the jetty opens his hand toward the strait without being told to. I can see the gesture. I cannot make my own hand do it, not now, not with four men watching the monitor to see whether it will.

We go again. The director does not say beautiful, and by now the absence of the word is itself a direction; I have learned to hear the silence where it should be faster than I have learned anything else today. He says Reset, and we reset. When the look still has not come he tells me to take my time, there is no clock, in the voice that said Beautiful and Fresh eyes and Be easy, the voice of a man handing me, as a kindness, a thing I cannot use. There has been a clock all morning and everyone in the room can hear it running. Somewhere in the high teens of takes a thing arrives in my face that I can neither name nor send back — the monitor catches it, low, around the mouth — and the director watches it arrive and says, not unkindly, that we have plenty, that the rest is the edit’s problem.

The island tape is not even the first draft of me. The July before I moved — before I had a Singapore address, before I had signed the thing that would get me one — I found the casting team’s email and wrote to them unasked, describing myself in the third person: a man about to take a big chance, a man about to accept a transfer to his company’s Singapore office. I wrote that man a full year before I would have to be him, and then I went and was him, and the year filled the sentences in.

I understand the motive now, though I could not have named it then. I have spent my working life sending sentences up for approval, and somewhere in those years approval stopped being a step and became a property of the sentence: an uncleared thing did not feel false, exactly. It felt pending. I had lost the part of a person that can mean a thing before anyone has signed off on it, and I did not notice the loss. The part that would have noticed was the part that was gone. So I could not take the chance until a version of the man taking it had been submitted somewhere and not rejected. A casting department’s silence is not permission, but it files the same way. I cleared my own life with the only reviewer I could find.

That is the pathology, and it is true, and I would rather leave it there, because the pathology at least is interesting. The other motive is not. The reply came, and I felt picked. Some part of me wanted the forums Graham promised, the strangers defending my decisions to strangers, the version of me with an audience attached. Wanted Doris. I can offer you the wound with some dignity. The appetite I can only report.

Making it up was the easy part.

A paragraph I typed at my own kitchen table has become a Saturday with a van in it. The cups go down. The steam is wrong for the shot inside a minute, and the assistant replaces them, and that is the job of the tray.

They shoot it fast, from the one angle, the camera at the curb with the street behind us. There is no lavalier for him; the boom hangs over the table like the question it is. The director wants one beat: the new arrival and his local friend, the friend recommends what the neighborhood drinks, the arrival is charmed. The director says, one more, just to have it. We have it, he says, after the third.

And then, because the crew is at the van sorting a battery, and the cups have been replaced again, and no one has said anything to us in a minute or two, the scene stops being one. He looks at the flat white in front of me and starts in on it, low, the real opinion, the one his line was the cleared version of: the drink that everyone orders, the drink for people who want the idea of coffee without any of its consequences, and it is teasing, and then somewhere inside the same sentence it is not teasing, or it is teasing about a bigger thing than the coffee, and his voice drops into the register with no one else in it, and I turn my head to him, mid-beat, and say something back. He answers. The camera at the curb was rolling; the battery was a different battery; nobody was watching the frame. I am not going to pretend the account kept the words. It kept everything around them: the light, the wet ring the first cup left, the exact half-second his face took the turn. The words themselves it filed where it files what it cannot clear, and the door of that file has never opened for me, and I have stood in front of it for years.

After, the director wants safeties of me alone. Singles: the arrival at the café table, settling into his neighborhood, no friend in the brief for this part. But a man at a table looks at something, and the eyeline has to land where the scene needs it, so the production assistant sits in Arjun’s chair. The word arrives in Graham’s voice, collecting. She is given his cup. She sits the way the frame needs a person to sit, which turns out to be roughly the way he sits, one forearm on the table, the weight back, and I am asked to play the beat again, the charm, the appreciation, at a stranger holding his coffee in the shape of him. The beat comes out cleaner than it has come out all morning. Nothing in her is at stake for me, and the take shows it: charm with nothing to guard. The part of me that grades takes files this one as usable and is pleased. The pleased arrives on the spot. The fright I can only date to later. Arjun stands behind the camera with his arms folded and watches this being manufactured, and his face decides something about the room and keeps the result. The singles take eleven minutes. She gives him back the cup. It has gone cold, and he drinks it anyway, looking at me over the rim, and whatever the look is, it is not for the account either.

That night, running the day back the way I run every day back, I get to the curb and stop. The camera was up. Whatever it kept, it kept. Somewhere tonight there is a card in a padded case with the two of us on it, the register with no one else in it, filed under a working title, and this week a stranger will watch it at speed with the sound down, deciding what the episode needs. There is an email that would fix this. Three lines. I write versions of it for clients before lunch, and the show would grant it; asks like that are granted all the time. I never write it. Not that night, not any of the nights after, and for years I told myself I had simply not gotten to it. Here is the truer version. I wanted the take to exist. I wanted someone, even a stranger, even at speed, even with the sound down, to have seen what we were when nobody was watching the frame. If nobody could ever see it, I wanted it seeable. That is the wanting. I am not going to dress it as anything smaller.

He gets the produced me, the stop and the tilt and the held beat. I get the real him, at one remove, and all afternoon that is the trade. The glass is not in the brief. We wrap by one. The problem with the other two apartments is that they are both two-bedrooms. Two-bedroom, two-bathroom, each of them, and the apartment the show has me hunting for is a one-bedroom. That is the brief: a one-bedroom, on a budget, for a single man. It is also, as briefs go, true. A one-bedroom is what I earn, and what I looked for, and what — two years back, before any of this — I found. The two comparison apartments were chosen to lose the contest, and they would have, except that they are too much apartment in the one way a camera cannot quietly correct: a whole extra room, with a door on it. So the production spends the better part of two days deciding, out loud, what to do about a wall.

COMPARISON 1

This is already plain by the second day, the schedule having flipped overnight, when the crew loads us into a van — Stephanie, two cameras, the sound technician, and me — and drives northeast to film the first comparison. The first apartment of my own life is here at Fortuna Court, in Balestier, ten minutes from the city by a road I could walk; the show, having filmed it, now sends “Daniel” to the literal edge of the island to look at where else he might live. The van goes and goes. Balestier gives way to the expressway, and the expressway to the long northeastward haul of it — the off-ramps counting down toward the coast, the interchanges thinning, the road running low past the reservoirs until the blocks give out and the green netting of new construction starts to outnumber the finished towers — all the way out to Pasir Ris, the far northeast corner, where the island stops being city and starts being the place the city keeps for later. Off the expressway, the last stretch takes us past provision shops with awnings sagging in the heat and a row of shophouses whose pastel plaster — pink, mint, the yellow of a bruise in its second week — has the patience of facades repainted every decade until the paint itself becomes the architecture. At a red light the cameraman has his window down and the lens cap off, filming the intersection in a way that will never reach the show but that he does, I have noticed, at every stop — the street as raw footage, uncommissioned, a city he is collecting for some private inventory.

Stephanie, beside me, watches him do it and then begins, unprompted, to narrate the window. She prices a passing block the way other people read a street sign, automatically, aloud, before she has decided to. “Those are conserved,” she says, of the shophouses. “The fronts can’t change. Inside can become anything.” The squat blocks behind them are HDB, public housing, and the older HDB, she says, holds its value in a way the property textbooks keep failing to predict. The green netting two streets on is an en-bloc: a whole development sold off at once, by its owners, in concert, standing empty now and waiting to be knocked down. “Everybody’s hoping for the en-bloc,” she says. “You buy the flat to live in it, and then you pray the building gets bought out from under you.” She says it without bitterness, as a fact about the city, as she says all of it, easily, to fill a van, and the camera is up front with its cap on, pointed at a street.

“And yours?” I ask. “Are you hoping?”

She considers the question the way she considers a number. “I tell people I’m hoping,” she says. “It’s the polite thing to hope.” The camera stays pointed at the street. “Don’t put that on the show.”

“There’s no microphone in the van.”

“There’s always a microphone,” she says, pleasantly, and goes back to the window.

And I check. I can’t help it. The lavalier they clipped to me is off, coiled in my bag, and I watch it not be on. She does not look at it. “I never assume off,” she says. “Twenty years of other people’s living rooms. The owners step out, very gracious, take your time. But the helper is in the kitchen. DECISION

The pool is real. The pool at Fortuna Court, the building whose name I say to taxi drivers without translating, is real, and it is, that afternoon, empty of swimmers and bleached silver-flat under the equatorial midday, the sun holding its angle from before noon until five. The crew films me walking past it, a man passing an amenity. What the pool is, is where my body goes at six-forty most mornings: thirty laps before the office, goggles and chlorine, the building standing over the water while I count, the one thing I do in this country that nobody asked for. They do not film that. Tomorrow at six-forty I will swim it again, for no one, and then come back up to the apartment where my furniture has been returned to approximately, but not exactly, the positions it occupied before.

I keep noticing the approximately. The moving crew, the day before, put everything back in under thirty minutes, less time than it takes to eat a bowl of laksa at the hawker center down the road, and the double-height apartment at two in the afternoon is virtually identical to the same apartment at eleven in the morning when they filmed the “discovery” scenes, because the sun in a fourth-floor Singapore apartment in July does not arc the way it arcs in Brooklyn. The angle is the same. It sits.

I do not mention this to the director. I do not mention it to Stephanie. I mention it to no one. I am mentioning it now.

That night I go to Neil Road.

The crew wrapped at five. The director shook my hand and said “Great day” in the tone of a person who means it professionally and does not mean it in any other way, and the sound technician peeled the mic tape off my chest. The adhesive lifts a thin line of skin-warmth with it, a small downward tug at the chest hair, his eyes already on the next cable. By the second day my body has stopped reading the hands at my collar as anything but the hands that do this. Then I am out of it, and the apartment is mine again, the furniture in its approximate positions and the impression of the mattress back where it was, and through the window the clouds above the canal are working through orange to mauve. One degree north of the equator, dusk is a fifteen-minute event, the same fifteen minutes in December as in June, and it does not wait: at the first hint of burnt yellow you run to the window or onto the rooftop. Otherwise you are stuck with mauve.

I shower. I put on a shirt that is not the shirt I was wearing on camera, a distinction that matters to me in a way I could not explain to the director and do not need to explain to anyone else. On the way down to the lobby I pass Aunty Lim’s open door on the second floor; she is at her kitchen table with the Lianhe Zaobao and a cup of barley water. She looks up at me over the paper.

“Two days, so many people,” she says. “All for what?”

“They’re filming the building.”

“Filming the building.” She turns a page. “So many years I ask management fix the lift. Nobody come. Now got people, got lights, all for the building.” A small sound that is not quite a laugh. “The big one, with the clipboard. He stand in the lobby this morning, look up, look down, write something. I think, wah, finally, somebody from management. I go ask him about the lift.” She turns another page. “He tell me he is from America. He is filming. The lift is not in the shot.” The paper goes flat on the table. “Not in the shot. Aiyoh. Forty years the lift is not in the shot.” A pause. “I tell him the lift is the shot. He write that down also. So at least somebody write it down.” The paper comes back up. “Good. About time somebody look at it nice.” She looks back down, which is the end of it, and somehow it is me she nods to on the way past, not the crew who have been on her corridor for two days.

I take the MRT to Outram Park. The platform is the familiar cold, the train cooled to the same temperature as the office, as every enclosed space on the island — a communal decision about what indoor air should feel like, one that holds from the most expensive building on Orchard Road to the hawker center off Tanjong Pagar. I get out at Outram Park into the evening air, the cold-train smell of recirculated air and someone’s dinner giving way to the heat, the city’s own body pressing at every surface of mine. Not unpleasant, exactly. More like permission. I walk the six minutes there without hurrying, and the shirt is soft at the back before I reach the first corner. A cart outside the HDB block sells pong piah — tiny wrapped pastries arranged in rows, the vendor’s whole attention on the waxed paper — and something else is frying at the end of the block, the char smell carrying the whole length of the street. I don’t stop. By the time I reach it the heat lies over the whole front of me, and I am, walking into it, entirely outside the frame.

The Neil Road shophouses are a row of two-story buildings that look, from the outside, like any other shophouses on any other Chinatown street: white stucco fronts, terracotta tile above, the canopy of a single old rain tree leaning over the corner. The city preserves the facades and guts the interiors. The bar I am going to is on the second floor of one of these. It has a name I no longer remember and a staircase I do — narrow, steep, the wood worn to a soft luster at the center of each tread where the foot lands, the handrail slick with the humidity coming up from the street through the propped-open door at the bottom.

The room at the top is small: low ceiling, the bar against the back wall, four tables pushed together in configurations that change by the hour. The bartender is Malay, early twenties, unhurried, the shift a thing that would end without his help. The music is something ambient and Western, low enough to be architecture, a condition of being inside, like the air conditioning. A ceiling fan turns overhead; the work is being done by a window unit mounted above the stairwell whose condensation drips, every eight seconds, into a plastic bucket someone has positioned with the precision of long practice. The crowd is the late crowd: later arrivals, softer clothing, the particular unhurried energy of people who have chosen this, who have not come from work but come from the decision to come.

Arjun is there. When I come through the door at the top of the stairs he is mid-sentence at a table near the back, his hands making a shape in the air — the gesture he makes when he is building an argument and hasn’t decided yet whether it holds. He sees me before he finishes. The hands drop. He does not stop talking, but one corner of the mouth moves in the way it moves when he is placing a person in the room and deciding something about them, and what it decides about me, arriving damp-shirted and slightly raw from the day, is something warm and a little private that he keeps for his own account. I find myself standing straighter than I was at the top of the stairs.

He is there with four or five people, mostly expats and one who is not, a mix I recognize from the circuit of dinner parties and Sunday brunches and somebody’s-friend’s-birthday-drinks that constitutes, in those years, the gay social infrastructure of a city where the infrastructure is not officially supposed to exist. He is wearing a shirt I would not be able to describe to you the next morning and that I can describe to you now because I have thought about it since — a dark short-sleeve button-down, untucked, the kind of shirt that is slightly too big and that on him looks like a decision rather than an accident.

I have known Arjun for not quite two years; Singapore has had him for a dozen. He is also the person my eyes go to first when I arrive; I have noticed the habit and left it alone.

“Daniel,” he says. He has a way of saying my name I have not heard from anyone else. The emphasis falls faintly on the second syllable, as if the name were a question he has already answered.

“How was your thing? Your filming thing?”

“It was — ” I pick up someone’s vodka-soda and take a sip and put it down before the person whose drink it is can object, a gesture I stole from someone in college and perform now without thinking. “It was a thing.”

“You’re going to be on television,” says someone at the table, a British man, blond, whose name is either James or Julian and who works in finance and whom I will not see again after March.

“So yes.”

“My cousin was on it,” Kelvin says, from the far end, not turning. Kelvin is the only Singaporean among us, and the one whose flat we end up in when nobody wants a bar. The first thing he has said about the television all night. “The Penang one. They made her flat look like it had a view.” He lifts two fingers off his glass. “It does not have a view. It has a wall, and beyond the wall a car park, and beyond the car park, if you put your face on the glass and look hard left, the sea, technically. They lit that one window for forty minutes. She keeps the blinds down on it the rest of the year, because the afternoon sun cooks the room to a heat she described to me, on the phone, as homicidal.”

“Forty minutes for a wall,” the British man says.

“Forty minutes for a promise of a wall,” Kelvin says. “The wall they cut. They kept the bit where she stands at the glass and says, and I just love the light in the mornings. She works mornings. She has never been in that flat in the morning in her life.” He turns the glass a quarter inch. “They also gave her a husband.”

“They what?”

“A man on the sofa. For the establishing shot. They needed a someone, and the budget did not run to casting a someone, so the someone was the gaffer, in a shirt, holding a coffee. He is in the frame for six seconds. Three relatives have asked her, seriously, who he was.”

“Did she mind?”

“She framed the screenshot,” he says, and lets that be the whole verdict. Then, after a moment, to his glass: “The screenshot of the wall. Not the husband.” A beat. “She is particular.”

The British man is delighted. “They can just do that? Invent a husband?”

“They can do anything the schedule allows,” Kelvin says. “A husband is cheap. The gaffer was already on payroll.”

Under the table’s laugh, Arjun turns his head my way. “You should ask for one. They have the shirt.”

I laugh where the table laughs. Six seconds, a shirt, a coffee: a husband, issued like a prop, because a woman alone in a flat reads to a broadcast as a question that needs answering. Under the laugh I do the other sum without deciding to.

Her flat was short a man, so the show added one. Mine has one too many.

“Bargain, as husbands go,” the British man says.

“And returnable,” Kelvin says. “Shirt back to wardrobe, man back to the lights.” And then, to his glass, at a volume built to be missable: “Subtraction’s cheaper, of course. No wardrobe involved.”

The table moves on. The sum does not. Nobody would even have to cast anything for my flat; the man is already in it most Saturdays. Where her production added, mine subtracts: no shirt, no sofa, just one word on a form, FRIEND, and the flat comes out of the edit with the right number of people in it, which is one.

Kelvin wears the television the way he wears the rest of it, lightly: a polo gone soft from washing, the weekend version of a man whose weekday hours bill at a rate you can read in how he holds his glass. He has been coming to this bar since before it had the name it has now, since before most of the men at the table had a reason to know the city was a place a person could be from. The bartender sets down a drink he has not ordered; Kelvin lifts two fingers off the glass and lets it sit. He checks his phone once, thumbs a short reply, turns it face-down, and whatever is at home does not need more than the answer he has given it. Then he goes back to listening. He listens to expats narrate the city to itself, patiently, without correcting, a man who long ago did the arithmetic on how long any of us will stay. When my eyes go to Arjun and hold a half-second past what the room allows, Kelvin catches it, and shifts his knee, and opens a space at the table that does not need opening, and says nothing about that either.

Arjun leans in. “What were you doing?” he says, quieter.

“Saying things I’d already said. They had a tape of me from February. They’d been playing it back.” I drink. “And two years ago I wrote them a scene I didn’t have yet. Me, three months in, with friends I hadn’t made.”

He looks at me with a patience I cannot quite place, except that his own work runs on the same machinery as mine: he spends his days turning what a room wants into the version that can leave the room. “Did they shoot it?”

“They thanked me for the suggestion.”

He turns that over before he answers. He has a way of holding a word up to the light first, checking it for whether it is the real one or the near-thing standing in for it. “They’ll use the worse take,” he says. “They always use the worse take. The worse one tests better.”

“That’s a grim thing to know about your industry,” I say.

“It’s a grim thing to know about people.” He says it warmly, which is how he says the grim things. “You give them the true one and the near-one, side by side, and they reach for the near-one every time. It photographs better. It asks less of them.” A beat. “You should put that on your tape.”

“They have my tape.”

“The February one. The one they kept playing back.” He turns the words over. “Was it the worse take of you, or the true one?”

“The true one. That was the problem.”

“Of course it was. You can’t compete with the version of you that didn’t know it was being.” He stops on the missing word. Two fingers and a thumb at the rim of his glass, he turns it a quarter turn where it stands, then another, unhurried, while he goes and gets the word. “Watched. Graded. Whichever.” He lets go of the glass without having drunk from it. “That’s the con, then. They find the one tape where you forgot to perform, and they spend three days asking you to perform it.”

“Then they already know,” I say.

“Then they already know,” he agrees, and looks pleased about something that is not the tape.

He is leaning back with one forearm along the rail of the booth, in no hurry, a man who has decided the night can take as long as it wants. I spend my days making the version of a thing a client is allowed to be shown — the sayable cut, the line Legal will clear, the sentence with the weather taken out of it. He has just done it to my own evening, better than I would have.

I sit down. Someone orders a round: vodka-sodas, the cheapest thing at the bar, the drink you order when the bar is the point and not the drink. The booth is a green tufted vinyl banquette that has absorbed a decade of someone else’s perfumes; the air conditioning is fighting the July heat and winning by a narrow margin and the fluorescent panel above the bar is humming the single flat note every Singapore commercial fluorescent holds, a note pitched just under the aircon, the one your ear stops hearing and your skull keeps. Arjun is talking to someone across the table about a work thing — a client, a deadline — and I am not listening to the words. I am listening to the sound of him, which I have no file for: lower than his work voice and quicker, sentences left unfinished where the other man has already caught them, nothing in it framed for anyone. He laughs in the middle of his own point and does not go back for it.

The production sent a questionnaire in the spring, before any of this — the company’s logo at the top, a column of mild questions down the side. Several of them were about my life in Singapore: who I spent my time with, who knew the city, who might be worth meeting on camera. I answered the way I answer things for a living. I gave them a paragraph, and the people in the paragraph were rendered as the questionnaire wanted them rendered. Arjun was in the paragraph. I typed a friend who knows the city and then pared it down to the cleaner thing the form preferred — a friend. It should have gone quick, and it didn’t. The cursor sat in the field with his name in it, in the blank where the word in front of the name goes, and my hands, which fill these fields all day without looking, went still on the keys. Then the pause passed and I typed the true-enough thing and moved on, and I put the stall down to the form.

The future I offered them sits on a list somewhere, available to any other contributor in any other city. And here I am at the bar with a vodka-soda and a table of people who know my name and my job and my orientation and who do not need me to pretend any of these things are different from what they are, in a city where the law says that what we are doing is the thing the camera cannot see. Not the drinking, not the sitting, but the being, the ambient fact of men attracted to men, sitting closely together at a table on a street, some hugging, some singing, all absurdly sweaty. The law is still on the books: 377A, colonial, a crime the state keeps and does not enforce. When I say so, Arjun does not look up from his drink. “They left the light on in a room nobody uses,” he says. “Very Singapore. We don’t throw anything away; we just don’t switch it on.” The room nobody uses has a switch in me all the same. A hand at the back of my neck in a bar like this one, and my eyes run their small circuit before anything in me has decided to. Door, bar, street window.

I drink my vodka-soda. Arjun catches my eye across the table. “And here. After.”

“After,” I say.

He does something with his mouth that is not a smile and not a question. Not here, not yet.

Outside, two men share a cigarette in the shophouse doorway opposite, one of them watching me through the smoke. I will be back on camera in the morning. The director will want one more walk past the pool. The light will be the same. It was. The light, at least, I can vouch for.

Six

Bugis

I meet Arjun because we reach for the same mochi. In anything I produced for pay I would have cut that for being too neat. It is what happened.

This is at a convenience store in Tanjong Pagar, the kind that stays open past midnight in the lobby of an office tower. I have been at a work dinner three streets over that became drinks, and I have left early because I can feel the evening becoming a night, and a night is not what I want. The store is on the way to the train. I go in for water and for something sweet, and the something sweet is a mochi — three to a package, soft rice-flour shell, red bean filling, cold from the case.

His hand arrives a half-second before mine and stops, because my hand is already there, and for a moment we are both reaching and neither of us is picking it up. He is wearing a collared shirt with the sleeves turned back twice, not once, the second fold a half-centimeter under the first, which by my second month I had stopped reading as carelessness.

I know the shirt before I place the man. I have seen him before. Three times, now that he is close enough that I have to admit it: a party in a crowded flat in Tiong Bahru, a birthday at a bar off Club Street, Sonja’s Sunday brunch where I stayed past the point of having any reason to. Each time the same sleeves, the same listening that keeps the other person’s pace, and each time the same failure to get him into a sentence the way I get everyone else. I could have asked someone his name at any of them; the rooms were small and the city is smaller. I did not ask. Walking home from each one I told myself it was the heat — the flush up the back of the neck, the pulse in the wrists, the shirt damp between the shoulder blades by the lift. Singapore gives a body a hundred reasons. None of them was the reason. Now he is an arm’s length away under the white light, the dark hair on his forearm, the stilled weight of his hand over the same cold package as mine, and the wanting comes back with a number on it.

“Go ahead,” he says.

“There’s another one behind it.”

He looks, and there is, and he takes the one behind it, and we stand in the refrigerator aisle holding identical packages while the condensation beads on the wrappers and runs down our fingers, because this is Singapore, and everything from a case begins to melt the moment it enters the air.

“You’re going to lose that race,” he says, nodding at the package in my hand. “It sweats faster than you can walk home.”

“I’m three blocks.”

“Then you’ll lose by a little.” He looks at me. The face open and still, the size of the smile not yet decided on. “You’re not from here,” he says. Not a question.

“No.”

“I could tell. Not the accent.” He lets that sit, deciding something, and I watch him decide it. “Neither am I. Originally.” He smiles. “Let me buy you a coffee sometime. Before the mochi situation repeats itself.”

This is how, on a Wednesday in October, in a convenience store in Tanjong Pagar, I am asked out by a man who reached for the same mochi, in a country where what he has just done — asked another man for coffee in a tone we both understand — points, in the most technical reading of the law, toward a thing the law still keeps a number for.

He does not seem concerned, and I am not, in the moment, concerned either. The mochi is already sweating through the wrapper.

Arjun. He gives me the name on the way out of the store, in the doorway, the way you hand someone a thing they will need later — Arjun, and then, because I have given him nothing back, he waits, and I give him mine, and the trade is the first thing we do that we both keep. The name I can write. I have just written it, and nothing happened. It was the other word that failed me, the one that goes beside a name, and it failed me in the doorway with his name still warm in my mouth, and it fails me now, at this desk, the hum on, the ink wet, the man gone.

The desk can wait. We have coffee the following Saturday at a place on Purvis Street that he chooses. A broken ceiling fan, an Italian espresso machine, a barista who knows his order without asking, which tells me how he moves through the city: rooted, unhurried, a man with routes. He is a year or two older than me and has been in Singapore the better part of a decade, and Tamil, though that last fact I get not from him but from the barista, who greets him in it and gets it back easily, his two words to the barista’s three, a laugh splitting the difference, before either of them has decided what language the morning is in. I order a flat white, which is new to me and which I have started ordering because it is what people order.

He asks me what I do. I tell him the version I have arrived at over two months of the same conversation. He says, “So, medical,” and sets the word down lightly, half a question, the way he sets down any word he is testing for whether it is the right one.

“Medical-adjacent. The communications side. I work with hospitals and device companies. I produce things for them.”

“What kind of things?”

“Videos. Print. Events. The version of what they do that other people are supposed to see.”

“The version,” he says, setting the word down the way he set down medical. “So the hospital does the thing. The surgery, the trial, whatever the thing is. And then you make, what would you call it, the telling. The one with the good lighting.”

“The one with the good lighting,” I say. “Nothing in it is false. Most of the job is keeping it not false.”

“But it’s not the thing.”

“It’s not the thing. The thing already happened. Nobody was filming.”

He nods and drinks his coffee, black, no sugar. His hands around the cup have long fingers and clean nails, the left thumb against the ceramic in a way that looks practiced until I see it isn’t, it is just how he holds things. I am aware of his hands.

“You’re staring at my coffee,” he says.

“I’m not.”

“You are. It’s all right. People stare at it. It’s a very serious coffee. It has thoughts about itself.” He pushes the cup across the table. “You can try it if you want. Mind it. It bites.”

I try his coffee. It is better than mine.

“It’s better because it’s his,” Arjun says, tilting his head at the barista, who is wiping the wand of the machine with a cloth tucked into his apron and not listening, or giving it the half-ear you save for a song you have heard too many times. “Mine, yours, the same beans. He just doesn’t burn it. You burn it. I’ve watched you order. You let them leave the milk on the steam too long because you’re being polite and you don’t want to be the one who says stop.”

“I don’t say stop.”

“You never say stop. It’s going to be a problem.” He says it lightly, into the cup, and it is the first time he has told me a thing about myself, and the thing is true, and I have known him for two coffees. “You’ve been here how long?”

“Two months.”

“Two months. And you’ve found this place, and the bar three blocks from your flat, and the minimart, and nothing in between. You go out in a straight line and come back on the same line. Like a man on a leash who has measured the leash.” He says it without unkindness, the way you point out a stain a person can’t see. “There’s a kopitiam one street over that’s better than this and half the price. The coffee’s worse but the people are the point. There’s an uncle there who has been wrong about the same horse for forty years and tells you about it whether you sit or not. You’d hate it for a month and then you wouldn’t leave.”

“You’re managing my whole map.”

“I’m not managing. I’m offering. There’s a difference, and you of all people should know it.” A small look over the rim of the cup. “You can keep the straight line. Some people like a straight line. They sleep better.” But he is already drawing the other one, the one with the turns in it, the one I will spend the next two years being walked down. He finishes his coffee, black, and looks at the bottom of the cup as though it had something to say, and sets it down. “Saturday after this one I’m getting a dosa. The good one, near my place — not the one near here, the one near here is for tourists and people who have given up. You should come. You’ll do it wrong, and it’ll be educational for both of us. I get to watch, and you get to learn that there is a wrong way to eat a pancake.”

By the third Saturday he has taken me to a dosa stall near his flat in Holland Village. A counter and four steel tables, the dosa on a plate the size of a manhole cover, the dosa itself bigger than the plate, hanging off both sides. I look at it.

“You eat it with your hands,” he says.

“All of it.”

“All of it. With a fork, the whole thing collapses and you’re just pushing a wet flag around a plate. The right hand does the work. Tearing with the thumb and the first two fingers, then you scoop the sambar with the torn edge.” He demonstrates, fast, a thing he stopped thinking about twenty years ago. “Not the left. The left hand does nothing. The left hand is on holiday.”

My fingers are too slow and the dosa tears wrong and the sambar runs down my wrist toward my elbow. Arjun watches this happen with the flat attention of a man with all morning.

“You’re attacking it,” he says. “It’s not going to leave, no? It’s a pancake. It has nowhere to be. It has no flight to catch. You’re the only one in a hurry, and you’re losing.”

I laugh, the real kind, the kind that arrives before you decide to let it, and a piece of dosa goes the wrong way and I put the plate down. He is already laughing, low, pleased with himself, pleased with the situation, and for a second we are two men at a steel table losing a slow fight with breakfast, the sambar gone everywhere a sambar can go.

“Stop,” he says, and I stop, and he reaches across and takes my wrong hand by the wrist and turns it over, palm up. His thumb presses once at the base of mine, where a hard ridge of skin sits — a callus, racquet-shaped, his and not mine — that I will learn the origin of months from now and am only aware of now as a roughness on a hand that is warm and dry and on mine in a food center at nine in the morning. “Two fingers and the thumb. Not the whole hand. You’re not catching a fish — you’re not strangling it either.” His hand stays on the back of mine after the lesson is done, and we both notice it stay, and when he takes it back he takes it back slowly, and the place his thumb was stays warm. “Now tear with the side, not the point. The point fights you. The side gives.” I tear with the side. It gives. “There,” he says, and the there has more in it than the tearing of a dosa warrants, and we both let it have the extra.

The man at the griddle slides a second dosa onto a fresh plate and sets it down between us without being asked, and Arjun pushes it half an inch toward me, the host’s gesture, and tells me in the same breath that the trick with the sambar is to wait for the first dosa’s edges to go soft in it before you start, which I have just failed to do, which is why mine fought back. An auntie at the next table is watching my hands with the frank attention old women give a grown man learning to eat, and when I finally get a clean tear and a clean scoop she makes a small sound of approval and goes back to her own plate, and Arjun catches it and does not let it pass. “See. You’ve got the gallery on your side now. You can’t quit. She’s invested. If you go back to the fork she’ll take it personally — she’ll tell her husband a foreigner gave up at her stall, and he’ll have an opinion, and the opinion will outlive all three of us.” The coffee here comes in the same green-rimmed cups as everywhere, and he drinks his black, and I drink mine with the milk because I have not yet learned to want it the other way, and the ceiling fan turns its slow turn over both of us, and for the length of a Saturday breakfast there is nowhere either of us has to be.

Outside the stall the heat has not yet arrived in full, and there is a half-hour, this early, of something almost like cool — the night’s wet still on the five-foot way, a coffee-shop uncle hosing down the tiles in front of his unit, the smell of the hose-water hitting warm concrete coming up sharp and mineral and gone. A column of schoolchildren in white and blue goes past the open front of the stall, two by two, a teacher at each end, and the auntie who approved my tear watches them with the unsurprised eyes of forty years of the same blue-and-white columns at the same hour. The steel of the table has gone warm under my forearms. The fan turns. Arjun eats without once looking at his food, tearing and folding and lifting it up through the sambar in a single motion his hands settled the rules of decades before he could remember, and I eat with the whole of my attention.

That night, in my own kitchen, I tear a slice of bread with my right hand the way he showed me, and get it wrong, and tear another, and get it wrong in a different way. I do not mention this to him. It will be the better part of a year before my hands can do it without my watching them, and by then I will have learned not to say how much that pleases me.

There was a thing I was not learning. A few mornings on, he takes me to a Murugan Idli Shop on Syed Alwi Road, steel tumblers and a steam-clouded counter and idlis three to a plate, soft as something not quite set. The man at the till knows him, or knows his family’s name, which in this kind of place comes to the same thing, and says something to him in Tamil with my plate still in his hands, and whatever it is, it moves across Arjun’s face, and I watch it happen and cannot name it. This is the one face in the city I cannot run. I read the others for a living, the half-beat where a man decides what he will let me see, and I see Arjun decide nothing. The till-man’s sentence lands and his jaw does not set, his eyes do not cut to the door, the mouth does not arrange itself toward the easier version of whatever he is feeling. Something passes under the surface of the face the way a fish moves under a still pond, a darkening and a shift of weight, there and gone, and the surface closes over it before I can name the shape that made it. I have watched a thousand men hand me the read; he hands me the closed face and the thing kept behind it, both at once. I sit with an idli going cold in a sentence I have been shut out of, in a language I am a few months and nowhere into, and the not-knowing is not unpleasant; it is only total.

By the new year Arjun has begun to fold me into the rest of his life. He does it without announcing it, so that the folding has happened before I understand it is a thing I might have declined. The order is his: first the barista who knows his order, then the man at the idli counter who knows his family’s name, and then, when he has decided I am ready for it, Kelvin’s table.

Kelvin has us to lunch on a Sunday. He keeps a flat in one of the older estates, three rooms he could long ago have traded up out of and has not, with the settled order of a place two men have furnished slowly and lived in a long time and that neither of them, to me or to anyone, calls anything but Kelvin’s. Two armchairs worn to two different shapes. A second pair of reading glasses on the shelf by the door that nobody mentions. Kelvin meets us with a dish towel over one shoulder and moves back into the flat the way a man moves through rooms he has had a long time, a hand going flat to the back of a chair as he passes it, not for balance. He takes the wine Arjun brought, reads the label the length of a courtesy, and sets it on a shelf where I suspect it stays.

Graham is already in the good chair, at a volume the room is too small for and maintains on principle. He has the regional press’s entire week in him and means to spend all of it.

“He’s gone to Hong Kong,” Graham says, to no one, of someone. “Rupert. You don’t know him. Again, honestly. The one with the boat. He goes to Hong Kong the way other people go to a clinic, and comes back thinner and won’t say what for.”

“The boat’s the part that gets me,” Graham goes on, warming. “He doesn’t sail it. He’s never sailed it. He had the hull repainted last year, sight unseen, picked the blue from three photographs by email. It sits in a marina in Aberdeen accruing barnacles and dock fees and he flies up to look at it, like visiting a relative you’re hoping will remember you in the will.”

“He’ll come back,” Kelvin says from the kitchen, not turning. “They always come back. To tell you Hong Kong is finished.” He says it as if reading an arrivals board.

“Finished,” Graham agrees, savoring it. “They all come back to tell you the place they just spent three days in is finished. Hong Kong is finished, Bangkok is finished, Bali was finished the moment they personally discovered it. The whole region is a wake they keep flying in for.” He raises his glass to no one. “And yet here we all are, drinking the deceased’s wine.”

“Everyone’s leaving or rehearsing it. Half my address book is a departure lounge. There’s a man I’ve had to dinner four times, Duncan, lovely, useless, who’s been about to move to Berlin since 2009 — he’s furnished the imaginary flat, he’s got opinions about the U-Bahn. He’s picked the neighborhood twice. It gentrified out from under him both times while he was deciding about the taps. He’ll die here. They all die here, the ones who talk about leaving.” He drinks. “The quiet ones are the ones who actually go. I hear about it afterward, from their landlords. I resent it enormously.”

“You went quiet once,” Kelvin says from the kitchen.

“I went to Bali for a long weekend and you’ve never let me forget it.” Graham looks at me for the first time, openly, already enjoying it. “Except you, mate. You washed up. The American who came the wrong way down the escalator while the rest of us were sprinting up it.”

“I’m on a contract,” I say.

“They’re all on a contract, to be fair. The contract is the thing they leave.” He turns the stem of his glass without drinking. “And how long have you got this one for?” He tips his head at Arjun, fond, proprietary, a half-second past the line, which is where Graham does his living.

“As long as the firm has me,” Arjun says, before I can find a register for it. He is on the arm of the sofa, loose, a plate balanced on one knee, and he answers Graham and not me, which is a kindness I clock and could not explain. “They post us about. You know how it goes.” He rights the plate on his knee without looking. The firm posts its people about; he has just told me the whole of it, and it lands on me as one more line said over drinks, and I let Graham’s next question carry it off.

“I do know how it goes. I’ve watched it happen to better men than him.” Graham is delighted with himself. Then, to me, lighter, fonder, sharper: “Does your boyfriend know you make that face when he speaks Tamil? You’re making it now. It’s indecent.”

“Arjun’s not —” I say, and the sentence comes up in my mouth with no end on it, because the end is a word I do not own and have not, in three months, gone looking for. All day I have the next word ready for other people. This once the line runs out and the belt keeps moving and carries nothing. “Well. Arjun is —”

Kelvin comes in from the kitchen with a dish in both hands and sets it down in the place where the rest of my sentence should have been. “Eat,” he says, to the room, to me, to Graham, to all of it at once. “Before Graham talks it cold.” He does not look at me. He opens a space at the table that does not need opening, and says nothing about that either.

Arjun has heard the unfinished sentence. He hears everything. He reaches over and takes a piece of whatever Kelvin brought off my plate, and eats it, and says to Graham, “His face is like that for everyone. It’s a condition. You should see him with a spreadsheet. There is a column he loves more than he will ever love any of us. I’ve seen the column. It’s a good column.”

“A man with a favorite column,” Graham says, delighted, to the ceiling. “You’ve found yourself a romantic, Arjun. Hold on to him. The accountants make the best ones. Still waters, deep tables.”

Kelvin, from the kitchen, without turning: “He’s not wrong about the spreadsheet. Arjun reorganized my entire pantry the second time he came here. Alphabetical. I found the cumin under C. I’ve lived here twenty years; the cumin was never under anything.”

“It’s a system,” Arjun says.

“It’s a sickness,” says Graham, happy. “It’s a beautiful sickness. I want it. Come do mine. My spices are a refugee camp.”

“Twenty years,” Kelvin says, “I knew where everything was by not knowing where anything was. I could find the cumin in the dark. Now I have to read.“

“That’s the gift,” Graham says. “He’s taught you to read your own kitchen, darling. Mine is still illiterate. I intend to raise it with him at dinner.”

“He’s given me a kitchen I have to look at.” Kelvin sets down the dish. “I liked not looking.”

“It’s a system that means you can find the cumin.” Arjun says it to the room, but he says the next part to me, lower, while Graham, refilling glasses, is already turning Kelvin’s accusation into a story of his own: “He moved it all back the week after. He just wanted me to know he’d noticed it was done.” He says it, and goes back to his plate.

The room laughs, and the laugh carries it off, and I laugh too, a moment late, because for that moment I was somewhere else. I had reached, in a room of the only men in the city who would have taken the true sentence without a flicker, for the smoother thing instead. The racquet will be standing in my entryway before the lease renews.

I look, once, at the second pair of reading glasses on the shelf, and at Kelvin moving between the kitchen and a table in a flat he has never once called anything but his. He has been not-saying a thing since long before me. I let it stay where it is, and I eat Kelvin’s food, and Graham, satisfied, starts in on someone else.

Some weeks later my phone lights in the back of a cab on the way home from a shoot. Arjun wants to know when I am done and whether I feel like dosa at the usual place. There is a usual place now. I cannot point to the week it became one; no one proposed it, and nothing was agreed. I have spent the day building the version of a man other people are meant to see, out of takes and release forms and a brand’s quiet preferences about which words may be said on camera, and somewhere across the city there is a dosa, and a person who wants nothing from me but my arrival. I write back that I will be done by seven. I do not write the rest of it down anywhere, including in my own head, where it would have needed a name.

What I want to say about Bugis is this. The street the sailors came to, for the trans women and the drag shows, is gone — cleaned up and built over, a mall, a covered market, the version that survived, the one with that part left out. New tile, bright signage, a cool mall breathing air-conditioning out onto the street where the bars used to be; walking it, you would never know a thing had been taken. I walk it and admire the work and do not think, once, that it is my work too, or that a street is not the only thing you can do this to. What happens to me there is a night in February, and the streets at ten p.m. hold a heat I have no setting for: not the boil of noon, a lower rung of it, steam more than fire, the day’s heat let down off the shophouse walls and standing in the street, and for the first time since I arrived I register it as a temperature, a particular one, and not just as the weather.

The underpass lets us out onto Victoria Street at ten. I will start at ten and work back to the hour behind it, because that is the order the night comes back in: ten o’clock arrives on its own, and the hour before it I have to go and get.

Twenty-eight degrees, eighty-two Fahrenheit; I do the conversion. But the air outside Bugis Junction is not a number. It is a texture. It reaches the skin a half-second before anything else: a warmth with weight in it, a wet that does not bead but settles, carrying drain water and frangipani and the diesel of the idling buses.

Arjun is beside me, close enough that I hear his breathing change when he laughs at something I said about the underpass.

An hour earlier, then. We are at Albert Centre, the hawker stalls off Queen Street, the food center at the loud middle of its evening. Under the low ceiling the heat from forty woks has nowhere to go; it sits on the tables and the heads of the people at them. Each stall runs its own light — white tube, warmer bulb, the orange of the satay coals being fanned at the front — and the smells layer beneath it: charred chili, and the deep animal note of a soup that has been on since morning.

Arjun walks the stalls slower than I would, reading the queues. He stops at the Indian-Muslim stall at the back, the one he says makes the best murtabak in Singapore, a claim I have no standing to evaluate and no desire to contest. The uncle at the griddle has a stack of dough balls under a damp cloth and a paddle worn smooth at the grip. Arjun orders for both of us in Tamil, switching to English to ask me whether I want it spicy, then switching back before I finish answering, so that what I want and what he orders are not necessarily the same thing. The uncle says something short, no smile, a sentence that ends in a word I will later learn is the way you ask after someone’s mother. Arjun answers with a sentence that ends the same way. While the man works — flattening the dough with the heel of one hand, slapping in the egg and the mince and folding the edges into a square that hisses when it meets the oil — the two of them keep talking, and I stand a half-step out of it, holding a tray, watching Arjun be a person in a place he is from in a city he came to, both at once.

“He asked if you eat chili,” Arjun says, not turning. “I told him you’ve been practicing.”

“Have I.”

“You will have, by the time he asks again. I’ve committed you. Don’t make me a liar in front of this man. He’s known me since I had less of a face.” The uncle says something else, longer, flipping the murtabak with a flat turn of the paddle and not looking up. Arjun’s mouth goes up at one corner. “He says the last ang moh I brought asked for it without chili and he made it anyway, out of respect, and then watched the man drink three teh tariks and call it brave.”

“There was a last one.”

“There’s always a last one. You’re just the current one.” A beat. “Don’t drink three teh tariks. He keeps count. He’ll have a number for you by Deepavali, and the number will not be flattering, and I will have to hear it for years.”

The last ang moh. So there have been others: walked in under this awning, shown the stall, measured in chili and remembered by it. I look for the sting in that and cannot find one. The last one asked for it mild, called the tea brave, and is a story now, told at the griddle while the current one stands listening. But Arjun has committed me to the chili in front of a man who has known him since he had less of a face, and put his own name behind my mouth, and the uncle has not once looked up to check. Whatever the last ang moh was, he was not that.

The murtabak comes onto two plates, cut into squares with the side of the paddle, and a small steel dish of dark curry beside it, and a wedge of cucumber. Arjun carries both plates. “You carry the drinks,” he says, and tips his head at the chai-wallah two stalls over, and I understand that the division of the tray has already been settled between us without a sentence, the way it gets settled between people who have eaten together enough times.

We find a table a young family is leaving, the parents stacking the kids’ bowls with the speed of people who have done it a thousand times, and Arjun puts the plates down and wipes the laminate with a paper napkin out of the steel cup before either of us sits, a small automatic courtesy I have started to anticipate. The stool is bolted to the table and too low, and we sit at the angle that bolted stools make you sit, knees turned out, leaning in.

I tear the murtabak with my right hand. I get it almost right. Four months of the dosa stall in Holland Village live in the motion now — the thumb and the first two fingers, the left hand on holiday — and most nights it is no longer a thing I watch myself do. Tonight a corner of the square is too thick to tear clean and folds instead of separating, and curry runs down the side of my hand toward the wrist.

Arjun does not look up from his own plate. “Still attacking it.”

“It folded.”

“It surrendered. There’s a difference.” He tears his own cleanly, scoops, eats, and only then looks at the mess at my wrist, and the look is the one from the first morning at the dosa stall, except slower now, the eyes going from my hand to my face and staying on the face a half-second past anything the spilled curry asks for, the corner of the mouth set in the way that means he has already decided I will not get this right tonight and has decided he does not mind, that he would in fact be a little sorry to watch me get it right, because there is a version of these evenings that ends with me competent and needing nothing, and the look says he is in no hurry to reach it. There is a thing under it that was not there in October, a fondness he is not bothering to keep off his face because the food center is loud and nobody is watching and there is no door to do it behind. He hands me a napkin without my asking. “You’ll get there. You’re slower than you think and faster than you were.”

The murtabak is dense and rich and faintly sweet under the heat, the egg setting the dough into something between a pancake and a pie, the mince spiced in a way that arrives a full second after the first bite and then keeps arriving. I drag a corner through the dark curry. The chili lands at the back of the throat and stays. I drink water. Arjun watches this without comment, with the satisfaction of a man whose order has been vindicated, and I understand that he ordered it spicy on purpose, and that the asking-me-about-the-chili had been a formality, and that I do not mind.

“Good?” he says.

“Better than yours will admit.”

“Mine is fine. Mine doesn’t have anything to prove. Mine is a settled man. Yours is the one with the chili, doing the most, asking to be talked about.” He tears another corner. “You always order the loud one. The flat white when everyone has a flat white. The spicy one because the uncle dared you with his face. You think you’re being quiet, and you keep choosing the dish that comes to the table announcing itself.” But he is pleased, and he eats the rest of his looking out at the food center, at the satay coals being fanned, at a table of taxi drivers on their break, at the gray-rag man working the returns, and once, for no reason I can find, at me, and then back out at the room.

The teh tarik comes once the plates are mostly gone, in two thick glasses already sweating in the air. The chai-wallah pulls it for us, kettle held at chest height, the tea falling a foot and a half into a metal jug at his knee, a long ribbon of pale-brown liquid that aerates as it falls, then catches and re-pours, twice, three times, until the jug holds a half-inch of foam he scrapes into our glasses with the side of a spoon. The drink is sweeter than I expect and milkier and the temperature of the inside of someone’s mouth.

Arjun watches me try it. “My father drinks it hotter than this.”

“How much hotter?”

“Throat-cancer hotter.” He says it in the same tone he used to order the murtabak. “It’s a thing among the men of his generation. Tea so hot you can’t sip it for ten minutes, and they don’t wait, and the rates of esophageal cancer for Indian men over sixty are higher than for any other men their age in the country. Nobody talks about it. They drink it as hot as their fathers drank it.”

“And the women?”

“The women drink it warm.”

He takes a sip of his own and makes a small face I cannot read.

“That’s encouraging,” I say.

“What is.”

“The Indian Men of His Generation. The esophageal cancer rates. The whole received tradition rendered as table chat.”

He sets his glass down with the care of a man laying evidence on a table. “Daniel. One doesn’t just say these things out loud. There are entire weddings built on not saying things like that. Centuries of careful nobody-mentioning-it. And you, here, with a fifty-cent tea —”

“You just said them.”

“Aiyo, but I’m allowed. My father, my generation, my problem. You — you’re a guest. Guests don’t get to name the cancers. There’s an order to it.”

“Can lah,” I say. The particle comes out half-laughing, with the wrong stress; the can is longer than the lah. He looks at me.

“You’re learning.”

“Slowly.”

“No, that was bad. That was a man who has heard the word said by other men.” He says it again, fast, throwaway, the stress landing somewhere I cannot find. “Short. You’re putting a whole sentence into two words you don’t need a sentence for. The lah does the work. You don’t have to help it.” He drinks. “But you said it. The accent will catch up. It always does, the year after you stop trying.”

The stall uncle is already wiping down the next table. Across the aisle a hand-painted board reads VEG & NON-VEG CUISINE.

When the food is gone Arjun is up before I am, taking both plates and the empty glasses to the return point without being asked, the way you clean up a place you intend to come back to. I reach for my wallet and he says, over his shoulder, “You got the underpass coffee. I’ve got this.” There was no underpass coffee. It is the form the thing takes between us; no one is keeping track, and no one has ever come up short. He pays the drinks man in a folded note and waves off the change with two fingers, and the man nods, and we go.

Outside the food center the night reaches us all at once. The covered hall had its own weather, the trapped heat of the woks; the open street has the other weather, the wet-season air that sits on Singapore in February like a held breath, warm and thick and faintly green with rain that has not started and may not, the sky a low orange where the city light hits the cloud. A drain along the curb is running with the afternoon’s water still, and somewhere close a frog is going, one note, patient, and stops when we pass and starts again behind us. Arjun loosens the second turn of his sleeve and turns it up once more against the heat, and I watch him do it, the small private grooming of a man who has decided about his sleeves again, and he catches me watching and does not say anything, and we step off the curb together.

We walk south along Victoria Street toward the river. He walks at a pace that seems slower than mine until I see I am the one adjusting. His shoulder is close to mine, not touching, close enough that I feel the warmth of it through the air between his sleeve and my sleeve. A space of about an inch. I have not paid attention to an inch of air since my first weeks here, when every proximity was a question and every question had a body attached to it.

Halfway down the block he steps without warning into a mama shop, the kind of narrow corner shop that sells everything and admits it in no order — newspapers stacked on the step, a cooler of drinks humming against the back wall, packets of nuts and biscuits and dried cuttlefish clipped to a grid that runs floor to ceiling, a row of plastic toys faded to the same dust color by the doorway sun they get all day. He buys a small bag of sour plums and pays the auntie behind the counter in coins he counts out exactly so she does not have to make change, and she says something to him and he laughs. Outside he opens the plums and holds the bag out to me. The plum is salted and sour enough to pull the whole inside of my mouth toward one point, and dusted with something — licorice, maybe, or five-spice — that arrives after the sour and stays, and I make a face, and he watches me make it with open enjoyment.

“You don’t have to like it.”

“I don’t dislike it. My face is making a decision without me.”

“That happens to you a lot. Your face is the only honest thing about you. The rest of you is in PR.” He says it lightly, a man tucking a coin somewhere he can find it later. “It’s an acquired thing. Everyone’s auntie has a jar of these. You suck on it for an hour, you ruin one tooth, and somewhere around the second one you stop being able to remember not liking it. Everything here works like that. It’s awful, it’s awful, it’s still awful, and then one day, don’t ask me how, it’s yours, and you’ll fight a stranger over it.”

“That’s a sales pitch.”

“It’s the only one that works here. The good things hide behind one bad bite. The expats never get past the bite.” He takes one himself, unbothered, and sets the stone in his palm, folds the bag closed, looks, briefly, for a bin, and finding none, pockets the stone.

We pass a five-foot way where an old man has set up a folding stool and a transistor radio and is listening to what sounds like a Rediffusion opera, his eyes closed, one slipper off; a temple’s side wall, the incense from inside reaching the street in a thread that crosses the diesel and the drains and is gone. Arjun nods at the temple — not a bow, just an acknowledgment, a thing his hand does at chest height almost too small to see — and keeps walking, and does not explain it, and I do not ask.

“So tell me about Brooklyn,” he says.

I tell him about Brooklyn selectively. The apartment I gave up. The job at the firm’s New York office. The transfer, which I describe as a career move because that is one of the things it was. I say, “I needed a change.”

“Everyone says that.”

I am quiet.

“It got tiring,” I say. “Holding it up. The version of me I was being there.”

He looks at me a moment longer than he has at any earlier point in the evening.

“Performing,” he says, in a tone that is not a guess. “Yeah. I know it. I knew it longer in India than I’ve known it here. But I know it here too, some weeks.”

“It’s the same thing?”

“It’s the same shape. Different room.”

“What did you say, when you came?”

“I said I came for a job. Which was true. And then I stayed because —” He pauses. We are at a corner, waiting for the signal, and the pause is long enough that I look at him. His face in the streetlight is the specific yellow-sodium color the older lamps make, a color I have not noticed until now because I have not looked at anyone’s face in that light from this angle: the jaw, the line of the neck where the collar meets skin. My attention lands.

“Because what?”

“Because I hadn’t finished what I came to do. And then because I had, and by then it was too late to leave without it being a statement.”

The signal changes. We cross.

Bugis, the neighborhood and not the mall, has by 2013 been demolished and rebuilt as a version of itself. The nearest the old street comes to surviving is on television: the year after I arrived, a drama called Serangoon Road, set in the Singapore of fifty years before, rebuilt the old Bugis on a soundstage and ran on Sunday nights — the street lit and dressed and walked through again, available in the one form the city had decided it could keep. Arjun had watched two episodes when it aired and given up. He watched a third because I said I should see it, on the couch at Fortuna Court, and gave it ten minutes of silence, which from Arjun is a review. Then: “That sign is right. The sign painter they found, whoever he is, he’s right.” A while later: “They built the whole street and forgot to put anyone on it who’d actually lived there. It’s very clean. Everybody’s teeth are wrong.” He watched to the end anyway, angled forward, reading the set the way he reads a queue. What he was looking for on that street I could not have told you. The credits ran and he said, “They’ll do the whole country like this eventually. Beautifully. You’ll visit,” and turned it off before I had decided whether I was in the joke or in the country.

Just north on North Bridge Road, the back of Parkview Square cuts a different shape against the night: bronze Art Deco from 2002, all Manhattan nineteen-thirties, dropped without apology into a part of Bugis that is otherwise post-war shophouses and the heritage market. Arjun glances up at it as we pass. “Batman building,” he says, not slowing. “They wanted Gotham. What they built is a very expensive way to say we have money.” He does not look up again, which is, I will learn, the local response.

We walk past the glass-roof market without going in. The street market that runs along its flank is still open, the late stalls under their own bare bulbs, racks of phone cases and printed scarves and rubber sandals graded by size, a man frying you tiao in a wok at the curb, a woman selling cut fruit from a cart of crushed ice — pineapple in long spears, watermelon, green guava with sour-plum powder in a paper twist beside it. The aisle between the stalls is barely two people wide and the crowd moves through it at the crowd’s own speed, slower than either of us would walk alone, and Arjun lets a family with a stroller go first, and a teenager pushing past with a phone held flat in front of his face, and a woman carrying a rice cooker still in its box. For a second the crowd closes between us and I lose him, and then the aisle opens and he is there again, a half-step ahead, glancing back to find me without making a thing of finding me. His hand comes up at my elbow — not a hold, a touch, a steering, there and gone — to bring me around a puddle I have not seen, and I feel the place it was for longer than it was there.

A vendor calls something at Arjun in Hokkien and Arjun answers in the few words of it he has, badly, and the vendor laughs, delighted, and says it again slower, and Arjun says one more word and the vendor throws up both hands and waves us off. “I have about eight words,” Arjun says, low, only to me. “Six of them are food. One of them I just used wrong. He’s going to tell that stall over there about it before we reach the corner, and by Sunday it’ll be a story where I said something much worse than what I said.” He says it without rancor, almost fondly. “All these years and I have eight words. The Chinese aunties have more Tamil than I have Hokkien, and they only learned it to scold the kitchen boys.” We pass the fruit cart and he buys nothing and looks at all of it, and I understand that this is a way he likes to spend an evening, walking through a market he is not shopping in, missing nothing.

Arjun says, “My mother would have loved this. Not the new part. The older one, before they put the roof on.” He says it without nostalgia, in the register of a fact filed a long time ago that has not changed.

I say, “Did you grow up here?”

He says, “No. Chennai. Mylapore: three streets, a temple, and a market my mother knew better than her own kitchen. She bought our fabric from a man who swore every bolt was the last of its kind. It never was. I came over at twenty-three.”

A small dry-goods shop on the corner has a sign over the door in Tamil. Arjun points at it.

He says, “Read it.”

I say, “I don’t know any of those.”

He says, “Try.”

I make a sound that approximates what I think it is. It is wrong. He laughs once, smaller than the laugh he gives when I have said something he could have said better. He is pleased to be teaching the wrong sound to someone willing to make it. He says, “That’s po, not pa. Your mouth is open too wide. Close it about half a millimeter. The vowel is rounder.”

I try again. Wrong again, in a different way.

He says, “It will take you longer than you think. The pa in your mouth is not the po in Tamil. The tongue is in a place yours doesn’t go without help.”

He is watching my mouth while he says it. I am aware of the watching the way you are aware of a hand that has not yet touched you, a heat with a location and no contact, and aware, separately, of being a man on a dark street having the shape of his own mouth corrected by another man standing close enough to correct it, and the two awarenesses will not sit in the same part of me, and I change the subject.

I say, “What does the sign say?”

He says, “Pongal-um Kadai. The Pongal Shop. They sell the rice and the lentils and the jaggery for the Tamil New Year.” He points at one of the characters. “This one is po. This one is ngal. This one is kadai, which is shop. You’ll see kadai on a hundred signs in Little India. By the time you can read kadai, you can read about a tenth of what Little India is telling you, which is more than ninety percent of the expats who live a kilometer from it ever bother to read.”

I try po one more time. He doesn’t correct me. He has decided we have spent enough time at this lesson.

We turn down a side street. A row of shophouses with painted shutters, a cat on a concrete post, three motorcycles in sequence on the five-foot way. The street has the particular hush of a Singapore back lane after ten, which is not silence — there is the drip of an aircon unit somewhere above, the tick of a cooling engine, a television two floors up running a drama — a Korean one, dubbed into Mandarin — at the volume of a household that has stopped hearing it — but a hush all the same, the day’s traffic gone out of it, the shophouses leaning their stored heat back into the lane. The five-foot way is uneven underfoot, the tiles laid by a dozen different owners across a hundred years, a lip here, a patch of newer cement there, and I watch my feet without meaning to and then stop watching them because Arjun does not watch his; he knows the lane the way he knows the queue and the cup and the order of a meal, in his feet, without looking. The air between his shoulder and mine has gone to less than the inch. A woman on the second floor is hanging laundry on a bamboo pole extended from her window, and the pole wobbles with each shirt she clips, and I watch it wobble. Six months earlier I would not have seen her at all.

I say the heat doesn’t let up, even now. Arjun shrugs. “In New York hot is an event,” he says. “Here it’s infrastructure.” The way he says infrastructure, softer at the second syllable than I would, makes me look at him a half-second longer than I have been. He notices. He keeps walking.

The side street takes the inch between us and makes it the only thing there is. On the main road I had been measuring it. Here I stop.

I can feel the warmth coming off the side of him, the heat of a body that has been walking in the night air for an hour, and under the warmth something that was, this morning, whatever he put on, and is now mostly just him. My own body has gone somewhere ahead of me.

Arjun takes my hand.

He does it without preamble. We are on the side street, between two rows of shophouses, under a lamp that is not the sodium-yellow of the main road but a warmer, weaker bulb that makes the shadows longer, and he reaches across the inch of air and takes my hand the way a person takes a glass from a table — with no announcement, and no permission needed. There is a version of this gesture I already know. It lives in doorways: a grip that begins as a handshake and then declines to finish as one, that holds a beat past anything a handshake needs and stops short of anything that would ask to be named, indoors, deniable, between just the two of us. This is that gesture with the door taken away. It does not stop short. It does not let go. We are outside, and the street is not empty, and his hand stays.

His palm is dry and warm, and the squash-racquet callus I learned by the dosa is there under my fingers, and I find that I have been waiting to feel it in this register and not the other, the hand as a hand and not a lesson. It is a hand holding a hand on a public street; it is not, by any measure I could write down, more than that. It goes through me as though it were more than that. The warmth does not stay where the skin meets. It travels — the inside of the wrist, the forearm, a slow heat arriving in places a hand cannot reach and has no business reaching from four inches of contact. What it delivers, when it arrives, is the plain fact that I have wanted exactly this, the specific weight of his specific hand, for longer than I have been willing to hold the wanting where I could see it.

His thumb moves once along the inside of my wrist where the skin is thinnest, and finds the place where the pulse is, and stays on it, and my pulse is not a thing I have ever been able to produce a version of.

No one photographed the street; the one photograph there is of his hand and mine comes later, from an afternoon that thought it was ordinary.

At the same time my chest draws in by a half-inch, not quite a flinch, the shoulders squaring a degree, the tightening that happens when a queer man is touched by another man in a public space and his body, before his mind can intervene, runs the scan. Where are we. Who can see us. What is the legal situation, what is the practical situation. In Singapore, in 2013, they are not the same. My body has run the arithmetic before my mind reaches it.

And I do not pull my hand away.

I could. The side street is not empty — a man locking up a shop three doors down, across the road a couple walking a small dog, behind us a motorbike idling. Any of them could look. I do not know what they would see — two men holding hands, one South Asian, one white, both in the collared shirts and leather shoes of a certain kind of Singapore professional — and I do not know what that picture means in this neighborhood, on this street, under that lamp. I have seen men hold hands in Little India dozens of times, easily, without code. This is not that. I know what it means to me. For a second the word for it is right there, behind my teeth, easy. Then the old habit swallows it and gives up the wide, safe question in its place: what it means to the city, which I can’t say, and which the city has never once asked.

Arjun’s hand is still in mine. He has not tightened his grip, has not looked at me, is walking at the same pace and looking ahead as if the hand-holding were the least remarkable thing about the evening, an ordinary fact about two people on a side street who happen to be walking in the same direction. His breath is at the same rate as mine, or mine has adjusted; I could not say which.

He is not being brave. He is being himself in a space he has decided, for reasons I do not yet understand and will not ask about for another three months, is safe enough. He has lived here for years. He knows the scan. He has run it longer than I have. He has, at some point in those years, arrived at a place in the calculation where the answer is: here, now, on this street, yes.

I have not arrived there. I am arriving.

And so we walk, and I hold his hand, and the holding is not enough and is also, tonight, all there is going to be. There is a whole further set of things a body wants when it has been handed this much of another body and told — by the street, the hour, the country — that this much is the evening’s allowance, and I want them, in an order I have not arranged, with a clarity I have not let myself have about anything since I arrived. We are not going to do any of them. He knows it and I know it and neither of us slows down. The wanting does not go anywhere. It stays where it is, in the hand and the arm and the warm dark between his shoulder and mine, held at the one pitch the evening will carry.

We walk like that for about four minutes, which is the length of the side street and the turn onto the next one and about half a block past a kopitiam closing up for the night, the chairs being stacked inside with the clatter of metal on tile that means eleven p.m. in a hawker neighborhood. Somewhere in those four minutes I reached across an inch of air for a hand that will not always be in Singapore to reach back. I know that and do not say it. I hold it anyway. Then the street opens onto a larger road, and the light changes, and there are more people, and the space between our hands opens by an inch, and then by another, and then we are walking side by side again, not touching, at a distance that is ordinary and that feels, for the first time tonight, like a decision rather than a default.

Neither of us says anything about it.

We do not go straight to a taxi. There is a coffeeshop on the corner where the road meets the canal, one of the ones that does not close. A corner unit with its shutters up and half its lights off and a man behind the drinks counter counting a night with three customers in it. Arjun goes in without asking whether I want to, and I follow him, which is its own small piece of information about the evening.

He orders two kopi siew-dais. The man sets them down in the cups that are the same cups in every coffeeshop in Singapore, thick-walled, a green band around the rim, and we take them to a marble-topped table near the open side where the canal is a darker stripe under the dark, and the air off the water is the air it was on the side street, minus the inch.

“You drink it local now,” Arjun says.

“I’m trying it.”

“You’re trying everything.” He says it the way he said everyone says that an hour ago, which is not unkindly. “The flat white. The murtabak. The —” He tips his head at the kopi.

“You’re keeping a list.”

“I notice things. It’s a condition — your word, not mine. You’d know.”

I would. I drink the kopi, bitter at the top and sweet at the bottom where the condensed milk has not been stirred all the way, which is not a flaw but the design.

“On the street, back there,” I say, and do not finish it.

“I know. You did the thing your face does, the checking.” He is not unkind about it. “It’s fair. The law’s still on the books. Three seventy-seven A. They keep it and don’t use it, a rule left in a drawer so everyone knows the drawer is there.” He turns the cup a quarter-turn. “India has the same one. Older. Almost the same number, three seventy-seven, no letter. Same Englishman wrote both. We struck ours down a few years back, in the Delhi court. Now the big court’s sitting on whether to put it back.” He drinks. “They’ll surprise you. They like to.”

He makes it sound like housekeeping. It is the best copy I have heard in this country: a rule kept so everyone knows it is kept. The checking my face does on its own, the thing he was kind about a minute ago, is the drawer, working.

The man behind the counter has begun the long closing of a place that does not exactly close: he lifts the steel trays out of the display and stacks them, runs water over the kopi sock and hangs it on its hook, turns off the bank of lights over the cold drinks so that the corner where we sit is the brightest thing left. He does not look at us. We are not in his way and we have paid. A cat comes in off the five-foot way, sits in the doorway with the proprietary calm of a cat who is fed here, looks at the two of us, and decides we are not the development of the evening it had hoped for. Arjun watches it go.

“That’s the boss,” he says.

“The cat.”

“The cat runs four of these. He franchises.” He says it flatly, in the murtabak tone, and waits, and I laugh, and he is pleased.

“Four of them,” I say. “All this one cat.”

“He’s got a guy at each, and every one of them thinks he runs his own shop, which the cat allows, because a man who believes he is the boss works harder than one who knows he isn’t.” He drinks the last of the kopi and tips the cup to get the sweet sludge of condensed milk off the bottom, unhurried, a man finishing a thing he has finished ten thousand times. “My father did the markets like this — not the coffee, he never sat for that, but the walk. He’d take me through the whole row and buy nothing, and the men would tell him things, and we’d come home and my mother would say what did you get and he’d say news, and she’d say I can’t cook news, and he’d say you’ve been cooking it for thirty years.” He sets the cup down. “He thought he was very funny. He was a little funny.”

“You’re a little funny.”

“I’m the funniest man you know. You just got here so the bar is low.” The teaching smile. “Give it a year. You’ll meet funnier people and I’ll have to start trying, and I don’t want to start trying. I’ve got it very easy with you right now.”

He says it into the cup, pleased. He looks out at the man closing up the trays, the cat gone, the canal a dark stripe, and for a moment he is somewhere a long way east of this table, and then he is back, and he does not tell me where he went, and I am learning not to ask.

Outside, a delivery truck idles at the lights with no one behind it, and a man on a bicycle goes past with two crates of empty bottles strapped over the back wheel, the bottles ringing softly against each other at every seam in the road, a sound that arrives, crosses, and does not come back, and the road goes back to its after-eleven quiet. Arjun says nothing, and the saying nothing is comfortable, is in fact the most comfortable I have been in a room with another person since I got off the plane in August.

His hand is flat on the marble, the same hand, and I am aware of it the way I was aware of his hands at the coffee place on the first Saturday, except that the awareness has a fact under it now that it did not have then. The marble is not wide. His hand, the cup, my hand make a short triangle on it. He does not move his hand toward mine. I do not move mine toward his. There is a version of the next minute in which one of us closes the small distance; we both know it is available; and by the same unspoken arithmetic that has governed the inch of air all evening, we both leave it on the table. A fluorescent tube buzzes above the counter and a radio somewhere plays a Chinese ballad below conversation, and we sit in it, and the not-moving is not a refusal. It is the four minutes on the side street again, in a different room. He knows the room. I am learning it.

He says, “You’re quiet.”

“I’m tired.”

“You’re not tired. You’re —” He stops. Declines to finish a sentence whose ending he has already shown me he has. He drinks his kopi. “It’s fine, Daniel. You don’t have to have a face for it. That’s the —” He gestures at the table, the two cups, the canal, the hour. “That’s what the late one is for. Nobody’s awake to need a face.”

“Is that why you come to these.”

“I come to these because the kopi is a dollar.” But he is smiling. The smaller smile, the teaching one.

We finish the cups. Outside, the main road has the specific emptiness of Singapore after eleven, swept and lit and waiting for nothing. He is going east. I am going to find a taxi. We stand for a second at the point where the standing has to become two directions.

“Saturday,” he says. Not a question — the dosa, the stall near his flat. I say Saturday. He goes east, at the pace that is not slower, and I watch him to the corner before I go to find the cab.

I take a taxi home because the MRT has stopped running and because the taxi is seven SGD and because I am not, at eleven-forty on a Tuesday night in February, in a condition to negotiate the night bus. The driver pronounces the destination, Moulmein Rise, before I finish saying it, picking the words up where I started them. “Moulmein Rise,” he says again, satisfied — a man somewhere past sixty, a small brass deity zip-tied to the dash and a thermos wedged in the gap beside it, the radio turned low to a call-in show he answers under his breath. Some caller has said something that does not sit right with him; he tells the windshield cannot like that one, mild, final, and settles it. The host says something back to the caller, the caller protests, and the driver shakes his head at both of them. “Two of them also wrong,” he says, in the tone of a man who has been right about this particular thing since before I was born. He pulls out without checking the empty road, because the road is his at this hour, and he knows which lights will hold for him all the way to the river. I sit in the back with my hands in my lap.

My left hand is warm. My right hand is cool. It is February; both hands are the same temperature. I turn my left hand over in my lap anyway, palm up, and look at it under the passing streetlights. The same hand it was all evening, the same lines, the same knuckles. It does not look different.

The taxi passes Balestier Market, shuttered at this hour, the metal pulled down over the curry-puff station where the auntie knows my order. The taxi passes a minimart on the corner, its fluorescent light still on, and I cannot pass a lit minimart at this hour now without the mochi and the condensation and his hand arriving a half-second before mine. The taxi pulls into the covered drop-off at Fortuna Court, and the security guard looks up from his small television, and I pay the driver and walk through the lobby. The building asleep around me, Aunty Lim’s downstairs unit gone dark by ten as it always is, the mailbox alcove’s small fluorescent humming alone. I take the elevator and press the button for the fourth floor and stand in the corridor that smells of laundry from one end and something indeterminate from the other.

I let myself into the apartment. The aircon is running on the night setting it always runs on, the hum the apartment’s resting pulse. By February I have stopped registering it as a sound. The compressor catches once with a small shudder and steadies, and the room holds its temperature.

I stand in the living room with the lights off. I have not been to the gym in eight days, and I can feel my body in the dark. Through the window, the canal reflects the lights of the building across it, not the building, just its lights, broken into long vertical smears the water keeps lengthening and folding and starting again, a window’s worth of yellow pulled down into a streak and then let go, nothing in it holding its shape long enough to be the thing it is a reflection of.

Arjun had told me, once, what his firm was. Not the desk or the title. The shape of it. He had said it three weeks earlier, at the coffee place on Purvis Street, in the space between ordering and the coffee arriving, in the tone of a person describing the weather of a country he did not live in. There were people, in his world, who got chosen. Singled out, moved up a level and across an ocean, the firm deciding a man was worth the expense of relocating. London, he said, for the ones it wanted most. He had said it the way you say a thing that happens to other people, and I had received it the same way, and we had both, I think, been performing the same small untruth. It concerned us entirely. I would be the one who made the case for him, and I would make it well. The coffee had arrived. We had talked about something else.

What I did not notice, that winter, was that I had already started to describe Arjun to people, to colleagues, at dinners, at the client events, and I described him well, the way I described everything well. It was the only place I could have him: out loud, at dinners, in the third person. Nobody at those tables could ask me anything I could not field, and he came out perfect every time, and I liked doing it, and I went back and did it again. I made him sound like a man worth moving. He was.

I had filed the sentence about the firm where I filed the things that were not yet real, and it was not yet real. He had a flat and a routine and ten years of Singapore in him, and a firm reaching across an ocean for a man was, in February, a sentence and not a date. And what I was doing with Arjun I had not, at any point, decided to do. I had not decided. I had continued — the coffee, the dosa, the hand on the side street — and would continue a long time still.

The compressor caught and steadied. The apartment was quiet. I went to bed, and in the morning the wanting was still there, and the next morning, and the one after that, the way a thing settles in and stops being news.

Seven

Sunday

This is what the staying looks like.

The racquet has been by my door since March.

It is Arjun’s. It leans in the corner of the entryway where a person would keep an umbrella, head down, the grip wrapped in a tape gone gray along the edges, and it has been there long enough that I have stopped seeing it. Arjun does not live in the apartment. He has a flat in Holland Village with his own bed and his own kitchen and a closet of the suits he wears to an office I have been inside exactly once. Lately the office has been circling him: a bigger version of the job, a floor higher and a continent over, floated and not yet real, that he brings up with his mouth full and his eyes on the television and then does not bring up again. On the nights he stays at Fortuna Court he brings a bag, and the bag goes home with him, and the racquet does not. Neither of us has ever said anything about the racquet. It arrived one Sunday and then it was a fact of the apartment, the way the aircon is a fact of the apartment, and by this summer I have learned not to ask a question whose answer I already have and do not want said aloud.

It rained in the night. I know it before I am properly awake, from the smell that comes in under the door of the balcony — the smell of wet concrete and soaked leaf the building gives off after a Sunday rain — green, with the iron tang of the drains running somewhere below and the rain trees letting go of what they caught all at once when the wind moves them. The light is not yet light, only the gray that comes before it, and the aircon has been off all night because Arjun cannot sleep in it and I have learned to sleep without it, in the thick warm dark with the window open a hand’s width and the city breathing in. He is on his back, one arm off the side of the bed, all the way under, breathing in the slow way I read before I learned anything else about how he sleeps. The sheet is pushed down. There is the heat of him along one side of me and the cooler air from the window along the other, and for a while I do nothing about either, because there is nowhere I have to be and no version of the morning I have to produce, and the balcony rail outside is beaded with rain going slowly to nothing in the first of the warmth.

Before he goes, there is the milk. He cannot start a Sunday without it warm — not heated in the microwave, which he holds in something close to moral contempt, but stood over in the small steel pan he brought up from his own kitchen one weekend and never took home, the heat low, the surface watched for the first crawl of a skin. He drinks it plain, no coffee in it, no sugar, a thing his grandmother did and her mother before her, he told me once and only once, in the offhand way he gives me the load-bearing facts so I will not make an occasion of them. I have learned to leave the pan to him. The one time I tried to do it for him I let it climb too fast and a skin came up gray and wrinkled at the edge.

“It’s fine,” I said. “It’s milk.”

He looked into the pan, and then at me, with enormous gentleness. “It is not fine. You boiled it. One doesn’t boil it, one stands with it. There is a difference, and the milk knows it.” He took the spoon out of my hand without any drama at all. “You can do many things, Daniel. You will not be doing this.” And he scraped the skin off the edge and started again, patient with the milk in a way he was patient with almost nothing else, and let me keep the toast.

This morning he has it going at quarter to eight, one hand on the pan handle, the other turning the spoon in slow figure-eights, while the radio he keeps tuned to a station I cannot find again when he is gone plays something with a flute in it under a man reading the shipping notices. There is a steel bowl on the counter under a plate; he soaked rice and dal in it the night before last and ground it yesterday, and the batter is fermenting toward the middle of the week, when he says the sourness will be right, and the fact of it sitting there working on the counter — a thing started days before the meal it is for — is the most domestic object in the apartment, more than the racquet, more than the key.

He has set a second bowl to soak before he leaves, smaller, the rice and dal for next Sunday’s, a week ahead of when anyone could need it. The dal settles and ticks against the steel as the water takes it; the counter still smells of the ghee and the cardamom he cracked under the flat of the knife. He does it without telling me he is doing it, and when I ask him why two he says, “So there is always one going,” and does not look up from the soak, and I understand that there is a version of this kitchen, running on a clock I cannot see, that he is keeping for a reason that is his and that I am not, this morning, going to be given. He wants the batter fermenting on my counter, plainly, for himself, and I go to read the want and the read comes back with the want and nothing under it. He pours the milk into the one glass he uses, rinses the pan, sets it upside down on the rack, and drinks standing at the window in the flat early light, and I sit at the table and watch him drink milk and do not say anything, because there is nothing in the moment that wants saying and we are both, by now, fluent in that.

Before he leaves he lifts the upturned plate off the bowl and looks at the batter underneath — the pale, faintly sour slurry of ground rice and lentil he set to ferment the day before — and stirs it once with the back of a spoon, down to the bottom and up, reading it the way he reads the milk and the price and the cricket, for whether it is doing what it should. “Coming,” he says, to the bowl, not to me. “Slow. It’s cold for it, your apartment. In Chennai this is done by lunch.” He bends and smells it, frowns, not displeased, the frown of a man taking a reading he trusts more than the calendar. “Another day. Maybe two. It tells you. You don’t tell it.” He settles the plate back. Under his breath, once, a word in Tamil, to the batter, not offered for translation. “My mother would have it on the stone by now. She did not measure anything. You ask her how much and she shows you the hand.” He cups his palm, holds it out, a measure that is no measure. “This much. And it was always right, and if you used the same hand it came out wrong, because it was not the hand, it was her.” He says it lightly, the grandmother and now the mother handed across in one Sunday, and then he washes the spoon and sets it on the rack and goes to find his shoes, and the bowl sits on the counter working at its own speed, slow, in the cold of my apartment, toward the middle of the week.

He has gone to the courts at eight. He plays squash on Sunday mornings at a club near his flat, with a rotation of men he has been playing with for years: a corporate lawyer, an Australian whose name I never retain, a Tamil uncle who, Arjun says, does not run for a single ball and still wins. He comes back from it unhurried, his hair wet from the club shower, as though the morning had been the morning’s own idea and he had merely agreed to it.

In the kitchen, in the slow hour I have to myself after he leaves, I notice the salt-and-pepper grinder is on the side it was on the night before, the side I set it down on after dinner — the width of its own base to the right of where it lives. He moved it back yesterday afternoon, while I was in the shower; I know the correction because the body has run the route too many times not to clock the gap. Counter, stove, grinder, press. He moved it without saying he had moved it, the way he moves a book on the nightstand back into the order of the books beside it, straightening the towel on the rack without straightening it pointedly. The kitchen has a settled grammar I did not put there. I pick up the grinder and set it down on the side the kitchen has assigned it, and then I stand there a moment longer than the adjustment requires.

There are other corrections. The dish towel hangs folded in thirds over the oven handle, which is not how I hang a dish towel; I bunch it through the handle and let it find its own shape, and at some point in the spring it stopped being bunched and started being folded in thirds, and I never saw it happen and have never once seen him do it, only the result, every morning, the towel in thirds. The mugs face the same way on the shelf now, handles all to the right. The bag of his particular tea, the one with the cardamom already in it that he buys at the Mustafa Centre at one in the morning because that is when he ends up at the Mustafa Centre, lives in the cupboard behind my coffee, where I would never have put it and where it is, I have come to understand, exactly correct.

None of it was negotiated. The apartment was mine and then, by a slow accretion of small unspoken rightnesses, it became a place that a second person also lived inside the logic of, and I find I can stand in my own kitchen and read, in the position of every object, the hand of a man who is at this hour on a squash court three kilometers away, missing a ball on purpose to keep a point alive. I can still walk that kitchen from here, object by object, the grinder on its side, the towel in its thirds.

Once, early, I caught him at it. He had the grinder in his hand and the guilty stillness of a man who has been seen moving a thing he had decided to move quietly.

“You moved the grinder,” I said.

“I moved the grinder.”

“There’s a right side.”

“There is a right side. You keep putting it on the side where the cloth gets caught when you wipe down. I am not saying anything about it. I am only correcting it.” He set it down, an inch to the left, where it has lived ever since. “When you are not here I leave it on your side. So it doesn’t get used to me.”

He said it lightly, and went to find his shoes, and I never moved the grinder back.

There had been a first morning, a year and some months back, before the racquet had a corner and the milk had a pan, when he stayed over for the first time.

We had come up late, and it had been the good kind of late, and then he was asleep, on his back, one arm off the side of the bed, all the way under, and I was not asleep at all. Not from worry. From the plain animal fact of another body in the bed, taking up room I had gotten used to having, breathing on a rhythm that was not mine and would not adjust. The window was open a hand’s width and the night came in at intervals, the drains, the frangipani, once a motorbike going up through its gears somewhere past the canal. At some point he had shifted in his sleep, and where his arm had been against mine there was now a gap the width of a hand, and the skin of my forearm was keeping the warmth of him and losing it, and I wanted the arm back. That arm, at that stretch of skin. I lay in the gray-before-light wanting it and not moving, because a man you have slept beside once is not yet a man you can reach for in his sleep.

I could have slept. I did not want the morning to start, because starting it would spend it, and I did not yet know there would be another one.

When he woke he did not reach for his phone. He turned his head, found me already awake, and looked at me for a while, unhurried, wanting nothing I could detect except to be looking. “You’ve been up,” he said. Not a question. “A while,” I said. “You watch people sleep. Of course you do.” He did not seem to mind being watched. He did not offer a sentence about the night or ask for mine. He pulled me in by the back of the neck, dry-palmed, the callus at the base of the thumb, and we did not get up for the milk for a long time, and he did not make anything of it, and neither did I.

I have a list. I have kept it.

I want to be exact about the list, because the day turns on it. It is a Sunday, and Sundays do not need lists.

The night before, while Arjun slept in the manner I had already learned to read, I took a Calder Rowe meeting agenda from my bag, turned it over, and wrote on the blank side a sequence. Dosa. The pool. The gallery at Gillman Barracks, the old army blocks the city gave to art, with the show that is closing. A table at seven at a place in Tiong Bahru that takes bookings. I wrote them in order, and I circled the table, because the table is the part I am least sure of and most want.

He lets himself in a little after ten.

He has a key. He has had a key since the spring, since around the time the racquet appeared, and the key arrived the way the racquet arrived, which is to say it did not arrive so much as turn out, one day, to exist. I had a copy cut at the hardware shop on Balestier Road on an errand I folded into other errands, and set it on the counter without ceremony, and he picked it up and put it on his ring without ceremony, and that was the whole transaction.

I hear the door, and then the specific sequence of Arjun arriving: the bag set down, the shoes off and stood against the wall rather than left where they fall, the racquet returned to its corner with a small wooden knock that is the same knock every Sunday. He comes into the kitchen carrying the heat of the walk from the taxi, his shirt dark down the spine, and he looks at me, and then at the agenda on the counter, turned over, my handwriting on the back of it.

“What’s that,” he says.

“Nothing. A list.”

“A list.” He picks it up and reads it top to bottom, unhurried, and something moves in his face that I do not get to name before he speaks. “We have a list.”

“It’s options.”

“You’ve circled one of the options.”

“That’s not a circle. That’s where the pen — it’s where I rested the pen.”

“You rested the pen,” he says, “in a perfect ring, around the dinner.” He sets the agenda down, face up, so that it is a Calder Rowe agenda again, and not the back of one. “Daniel. It’s Sunday. You’ve project-managed a Sunday.”

“I’ve made some suggestions about a Sunday.”

“There’s a dependency.” He taps the list. “Look. The pool is gated to the dosa. The gallery is gated to the pool. The dinner is gated to the gallery. If the dosa runs long, the whole afternoon slips. You’ve built a critical path, Daniel. On a Sunday you have built a critical path.” He laughs. Not the wide one, the other one, the short exhale through the nose he gives a thing that is true and that he is going to let me keep anyway. He goes to the sink and drinks a glass of water standing up, and the conversation, for the moment, is a thing we have set down rather than a thing we have finished, and neither of us says so.

We go for the dosa.

The stall is in Holland Village, near his flat, the same stall he took me to on the third Saturday, a year and some months back, when my fingers were too slow and the dosa tore wrong and the sambar ran down my wrist and he reached over and adjusted my grip, without looking up, the way you adjust someone’s hold on a racquet. I thought about that, at the time, as a thing that had happened to me. I understand it now as the first lesson in a curriculum neither of us ever called one. It went on, Saturdays and Sundays, at this stall and at others, the places off Serangoon Road where the menu hangs on the wall in two scripts and he orders in the one I cannot read, and the proof of it is that my hands have learned.

This is a small thing to be proud of. I am proud of it anyway. I tear the dosa with the right hand, thumb and the first two fingers, and scoop the sambar with the torn edge, and I do it without watching my own hand do it, and Arjun, across the steel table, watches me not watch my hand, and says nothing, and eats his own.

The radio over the drinks counter is carrying the cricket, and the man at the griddle has it turned up for himself and for the two old men at the corner table — regulars, their kopi long gone cold in front of them, their whole attention on the over — and against the rest of us. Arjun listens to a passage of it with his head tilted, all the way to the end of the over, and then makes a small disgusted sound. “He’s left it. Look at him, he’s left a full ball outside off, this fellow, he will leave and leave and leave and then one day they will write that he was patient.” He tears his dosa. “Patient. He is not patient. He is frightened. There is a difference, and only the batsman knows it, and he will take it to his grave and they will put patient on the stone.”

The man at the griddle says something to the corner table without turning from the iron, and both uncles answer him at once, and the boy laughs. The one word I catch is cannot, and it isn’t even Tamil.

“What happened.”

“He left another one.” Arjun does not look up. “The whole stall is now against him. The man cooking is against him. It is a national project.” He catches my face, the face of a man holding a fork in the middle of a sport he cannot see and a language he does not have. “You don’t follow it.”

“I follow that everyone is upset with a man on the radio.”

“That is the whole of it, Daniel. You have understood cricket. The rest is detail.” He goes back to the over.

“You sound like you’ve met him.”

“I have met a hundred of him. My uncle is him. My uncle played for the district — district, Daniel, this is a serious thing, you would not understand, it is more serious than the Test side — and he left every ball that frightened him for forty years and they called it technique.” He says it with enormous fondness, the way he says all the things about home, an item handed across the table and not explained. “He kept the scorebook from a match in 1971 in a tin with his medals. Eleven not out. He batted four hours for eleven, Daniel. Four hours. The other side went home. The umpires went home. And in the tin it says eleven, not out, and beside it, in his own hand, unbeaten.” He shakes his head, half in awe. “The man at my club is the same. He does not run for one ball. Not one. And he wins. You know why he wins?”

“Tell me why he wins.”

“Because the rest of us are running,” he says, delighted with it. “Because he has made a study of the one ball he cannot lose to, which is the ball he does not play.” Arjun points the torn dosa at me like a man making a closing argument. “You would love him, Daniel. He is you. He stands in the middle of the court doing nothing with total conviction and lets the game come apart around him, and at the end he has the most points and the cleanest shirt, and everyone hates him, and he goes home and warms his milk.”

“I run for balls.”

“You run for the idea of balls. You run for balls that have not been hit yet.” He perks up entirely now. “You are running for Tuesday. You are running for a table at seven o’clock that does not exist.”

He goes back to his dosa, and the over ends, and the uncle at the next table says something that makes both uncles laugh, and Arjun catches a word of it and laughs too, late, on their behalf.

The stall is four steel tables under a tin awning, a man at a flat black griddle the size of a manhole cover, a boy who is probably the man’s nephew running plates. The griddle gives off a smell I would not have been able to name two years ago and could now find blindfolded across a city — the toasted-rice smell of the batter hitting the hot iron, the ghee going at the edges, the curry-leaf and mustard-seed crackle from the pot of sambar kept at a low boil all morning. Arjun sits with his back very straight, which he does everywhere, and eats fast and neatly, and between mouthfuls he rolls his right thumb absently against the side of his first finger — the squash hand, working without his knowing it, a small private friction, the body keeping itself company. I have stopped pointing it out to him. I just watch the thumb work. The uncle who runs the stall knows Arjun’s order and has come, over the year, to know mine, which is Arjun’s order, because at some point I stopped ordering and started arriving. He sets the two dosa down and says something to Arjun in Tamil that ends in the word I now know is the way you ask after a person’s mother, and Arjun answers, and then the uncle says something else, shorter, with a tip of his head toward me, and Arjun’s face does a thing, and he does not translate it.

“What,” I say.

“He says you eat properly now.”

“That’s the whole sentence.”

“That’s the whole sentence.”

“He talked for nine seconds, Arjun. I counted.”

“You counted.”

“I count. It’s a thing I do.”

“He said you eat properly now.” Arjun folds a piece of dosa with the kind of attention the dosa does not require. “And that for an American it is a small miracle, and that the first time I brought you, you held it like a man defusing it. He did an impression. It was not flattering. I am protecting you.” He eats the folded piece. “You should be grateful. The man does not waste material on people he does not like. The lawyer who comes Thursdays, he has been eating here eleven years. There is no impression of the lawyer. The lawyer is furniture. You are material.”

It is not the whole sentence, even now. I have been in Singapore long enough to know when a sentence has been edited on its way across a table, and the uncle said more than Arjun gave me, and what he said more of made Arjun’s face do the thing, and Arjun kept it on his side of the table. The sound itself I kept: all nine seconds of it, whole, the pitch and the run of it exact, next to the two notes of the morning bird. I think to reach over, and wait too long, and the moment passes. I tear another piece of dosa. The uncle, refilling a water jug two tables down, is not looking at us, and is, in the specific way of a man not looking at a thing, entirely aware of us, and has been for a year. Two men who arrive together on weekends and order one order and leave a tip calculated by the one who is not from here. He worked us out long ago, and it has not once changed the warmth with which he sets the plates down or the small flourish with which he tops up the chutney we did not ask for. I have stopped, mostly, noticing the noticing.

The trouble, when it comes, comes in the lift.

We take a taxi back to Fortuna Court for the pool, and come up through the lobby, and the lift is on the ground floor with its doors open as though it had been waiting, and Aunty Lim is beside it, back from the market, and the three of us get in. I press the 4; she presses the 2. The 12 is Tal’s floor. On other nights my thumb has found it without instructions, the button lit before the doors finished closing. Today my hand stays at my side, and the 12 stays a plastic circle with nothing to announce.

Aunty Lim lives on the second floor and has lived on the second floor since before the building was called Fortuna Court, since it was called something else, and she conducts her relationship with the rest of the building from a position of total information and total economy: she knows everything and spends almost none of it. She is carrying a cloth bag of vegetables and a folded Chinese newspaper, a small woman in her seventies whose hands on the bag’s handles have the dry, precise grip of a person who has carried things the same way for longer than I have been alive, and she looks at the two of us, and I begin to produce.

This used to come with a tell. There used to be a thing that happened in my chest before it, a contraction, a squaring, the body reporting the cost before I paid it. The body has stopped doing that. I notice that it has stopped, the way you notice your own hands only when they have gone still. There is no contraction. There is only the smoothness arriving on schedule, the throat finding the right syllable, the breath measured out, and no charge on it anywhere.

“Aunty Lim,” I say. “This is my friend Arjun.”

It costs nothing from the outside: four words and a name, the most ordinary sentence a person could say in a lift. But I say it at office temperature, the temperature of the agenda the dosa list is written on the back of, of every room in which I have ever made a true thing presentable. I introduce the man whose racquet has stood by my door since March as my friend Arjun, and I do it smoothly, consulting nothing.

Aunty Lim looks at me. Then she looks at Arjun. And she does not look at him the way you look at a stranger introduced to you in a lift. She looks at him the way you look at a person you have a standing arrangement with.

“Your friend,” she says, to me, without inflection, banking the phrase rather than spending it.

And then, to Arjun, in the same breath, as though I have finished being a participant in the conversation: “Pool closed Tuesday. They put something in the water, the cleaning. You tell him.” She tips her head a quarter-inch at me, on him. It takes me half a second: the tilt is about Arjun, and it has come to me because, somewhere in her map of the building, I am the one who is responsible for him. Nobody has told her this. She has not asked. It is simply where she has put us, and I stand in the lift and let her put us there. “He never reads the notice.”

“I’ll tell him,” Arjun says.

She looks at the bag of vegetables and then at Arjun, as if the two were related items. “Last time also you tell him. He still went down Tuesday. Stood there.” She does not look at me. “Toweled.”

“I read part of the notice,” I say.

“Part.” She says it to Arjun. She shifts the bag to the other hand, a small redistribution of weight that is also, somehow, a verdict. “The notice is one paragraph. Big letters. They put a picture even. A man, water, a cross over it.” A pause, the kind she leaves before the line she has been saving. “He has a degree, is it.”

“Two,” Arjun says.

“Two.” She lets that sit with the vegetables.

“He never reads the notice,” she says again, to Arjun, settling it between the two of them, two adults discussing the maintenance of a household and the habits of the third person in it, and the lift reaches the second floor, and the doors open, and Aunty Lim gets off with her vegetables, having spent, on the entire exchange, few enough words of her own, and relieved me, with total economy, of the use of mine, and my friend Arjun and I ride on to the fourth.

In the corridor Arjun is quiet for three steps. Then he says, “Your friend Arjun.”

“Don’t.”

“I’m only repeating it. It’s a good line. I want to learn it.”

“Arjun.”

“My friend Daniel,” he says, trying it in the air, turning it over, finding it wanting. “No. It doesn’t —” He makes a small balancing gesture with one hand. “It doesn’t carry the weight. You have a gift. I would never have thought to call me that.” He unlocks the door and holds it. “You produced it at the temperature of a lift notice. Four words and a name. She filed it before the doors opened.” He is enjoying this far too much. “Twelve years I have lived in this country and no one has ever introduced me so completely. My friend Arjun. It is a masterpiece. I want it on a card.” He is smiling when he says it. Under the smile, for a second, something else, there and gone, and he lets it go, because the corridor is where we let things go.

The smile is the wide one, the one I did not see in Singapore until I had known him some months and then could not stop seeing, the one that opens the whole face, and he is not, I understand, angry. I introduce him as my friend in a lift and then spend the walk down a corridor being teased about it by the person I did it to.

The pool is not closed on Sunday. We have it nearly to ourselves.

I have a plan for the pool, too. I have not written this one down, which I tell myself makes it not a plan. The idea is that we swim first, properly, a few lengths, and then we do the part where we stand at the edge and do nothing, and then, with the doing-nothing banked, we have the gallery in the afternoon with the light right. There is an order to it.

So when Arjun puts the towel down and walks straight to the edge and lowers himself in and stands there, immediately, in the chest-deep water, doing the nothing, the plan stubs its toe.

“We could do a few lengths first,” I say.

“We could.” He does not move toward doing a few lengths.

“While we’re fresh.”

“Fresh for the lengths.” He tips his head back and lets the water take the weight of it. “Or fresh for the standing here. The standing here also rewards being fresh. People underrate it.”

“It’s just that if we do the lengths after, we’ll be hot for the gallery.”

He opens one eye at me. The one eye sees everything; it always has.

“Tell me,” he says, “the order. I want to hear the order.”

“There’s no order.”

“There’s an order. You have done the pool in your head already. You’re at the gallery right now, checking the light. That’s the trouble with you, Daniel. You’ve already had this day. I’m still in it.”

“The show closes today,” I say.

“It is good,” he says, delighted, settling it. “The light in your head is very good. You have lit the gallery.” He pushes off the wall and floats onto his back, arms out, addressing this to the sky. “I am in love with a man who has lit a gallery he is not standing in.”

The short laugh first. And the worst of it is that he is right, exactly and without margin: I have spent the lengths before swimming them. I have a clear, false memory of a swim I have not had.

“I’ll do the lengths now,” I say, with what I believe is dignity, and push off the wall, and get through perhaps four strokes of a front crawl before I understand I am swimming lengths to prove a point about not having a plan, which is its own kind of plan, and I stop in the middle of the pool, standing, the water at my chest, and look back at him floating there reading the sky, and that is when the wide one comes, the whole face, because he has watched me work it out in real time and waited for me to arrive at it.

“There he is,” Arjun says, to the sky, not to me. “He’s found it.”

A family at the shallow end, the parents under the one umbrella, and otherwise the long blue rectangle and the heat coming straight down and the towers of the building across the canal standing in the water upside down — the nearer tower holding its shape, more or less, the farther one already coming apart, its edges handed over to whatever the surface is doing, a window here and there surviving as a bright fleck with nothing around it, the whole inverted block kept from being a building by the smallest movement of the surface, which never stops and never resolves into a picture.

Arjun swims a slow front crawl to the far wall and back. I watch him do it and go looking, the way I always end up looking with him, for the seam, the place where the ease is being managed, and I cannot find it, the same way I could not find it on the squash court or across the dosa table, and the not-finding is not a conclusion I reach so much as a wall I walk into. It is a face that stays itself when it is looked at. I spend my days with faces that change the instant they know they are being watched, that arrange themselves toward whomever is doing the watching, and his does not arrange. The eyes find me, hold, let go, and none of it seems to cost him anything I can see. He stops and stands in the chest-deep water with his forearms on the tiled edge and his back to the sun, and I come and stand next to him, our forearms on the edge, the surface holding us at the same height, and we do not say anything for a while. The family at the shallow end packs up and leaves — the father carrying the smaller child upside down and shrieking under one arm, the wet footprints going out the gate and drying behind them before they reach it — and then it is the two of us and the long blue rectangle and the heat coming straight down. The afternoon does not pass so much as deepen, the light getting heavier rather than lower, the way it does here in the hour before the rain that may or may not come.

Somewhere in there I notice that my breathing and his breathing have arrived at the same rate, the rise and the settle of two ribcages against the same tiled edge, and I do not know which of us matched the other and I do not test it by changing mine. He turns his head and rests his cheek on his own forearm, facing me, eyes shut against the sun, and says nothing, and the wet at his hairline catches the light, and a drop runs down past his ear and off his jaw and back into the pool without his moving to stop it. We breathe at one rate and watch the broken towers stand on their heads. It is the most married I have ever felt.

His phone goes, on the towel, at the pool’s edge.

He climbs out. I watch him do it, the water coming off him. He stands dripping on the tile and reads the screen and then takes the call, two fingers pressed to his free ear, and speaks for the length of a short call, and every second of it is in the other Arjun, the office one, the voice with the corners squared, and I catch some of it. A name, a number, the word Tuesday, the word London said once, flatly. Then he says a thing that makes whoever it is laugh, audibly, across the connection, and he hangs up and comes back to the edge and lowers himself into the water and his breathing takes a few seconds to find the rate again, and I let it.

“Work,” he says. “On a Sunday.”

“What did they want.”

“Nothing. They want me to know they’re thinking of me.” He says it lightly, the way he reads a world he watches and is never quite caught by. “It’s a whole register, Daniel. Being thought of. They make you feel it’s a gift.” He wipes water from his eyes with the heel of his hand. “A man rings on a Sunday to tell you that you were in a room you were not in, that your name came up and was set down again gently. And you are meant to spend the rest of the day a little warmer for it. Which I will. That’s the annoying part, I will.”

“So what did he give you,” I say.

“Name one thing. No work. No decision. Not even a question. Ten minutes of a partner’s Sunday, and partners cost, Daniel, partners cost by the quarter-hour even when they are being warm. All that money, spent, to hand a man nothing. It is the most expensive nothing on the market.”

“Is it.”

“It’s a gift the way a list is a list.” He bumps his shoulder against mine, once, under the surface, where it doesn’t count, where no one can see it. “Don’t worry about London. It’s just where the men on the phone like to imagine themselves. Everybody in finance is always slightly in London. It’s a condition.”

“You said London like it was a meeting.”

“It is a meeting. There’s a panel. They fly you somewhere, they look at you, they decide whether to look at you more.” A cloud crosses the sun; the water cools half a degree against my arms, and I let the word panel sink down into it. He floats it off the way he floats everything, on its back, arms out. “It’s nothing, Daniel. It’s a thing firms do. You make a man feel chosen once, when he is thirty and hungry, and after that he does the rest himself. He keeps himself worth choosing. You never have to do anything again, maybe ring him on a Sunday. I’ve watched it done to better men than me.”

And he ducks under the surface and comes up with his hair flat and that is the end of it, and I take the word as he hands it to me, lightly, as a thing that happens to other people, in a country he does not live in, and I go back to watching the towers in the water, and I do not think about it again, because he has said it, and I have heard it, and we have both, in the warm water, on a Sunday, agreed it is nothing.

We come in from the pool in the early afternoon and the apartment is cool and dim with the blinds half down against the west light, and for a while nothing happens, which is the part of a Sunday I have had to be taught to let happen. He showers first and comes out in a pair of my drawstring shorts that have become, without discussion, his, and lies down on the sofa with one of the books that have started living on my shelves at the end nearest the door — paperbacks with cracked spines, a history of the partition, a novel in a language I cannot read that he is rereading for the third time — and he reads with the particular stillness of a man who can disappear into a page so completely that the page becomes the more real of the two things in the room. I make coffee for myself, two scoops, the machine he tolerates and does not use, and I bring the weekend paper to the armchair, and I read the business pages and then the obituaries, which I read everywhere I live as a way of learning what a place considers a life, and the aircon runs its even hum and a delivery bike whines past on the road below and somewhere down the corridor a child is being told, at length and without effect, to put on shoes.

He reads with his knees up and the book against them, and twice he laughs at something on the page, the short laugh, to himself, not for me — and the second time, he reads me the line without lifting his eyes from the page — low and unhurried, the words handed over rather than performed, his voice dropping the half-step it only drops for me. It is a sentence about a railway minister in 1955 and a bridge that was opened a year before it was finished. “They cut the ribbon,” he says, “on a bridge you could not yet cross. And everyone clapped. And then they all went home the long way.” He lets it sit. “This is the whole country, Daniel. The whole century. A ribbon and the long way home.” He looks up to see if it landed, and finding that it did, lets me have the small unguarded smile he gives no one in an office.

A few pages on he goes still in a different way, the way he goes still over the milk. “Here,” he says. “Listen. The minister built a guest house at the bridge for the dignitaries to stay in for the opening. Forty rooms. And then the bridge was late, so the opening was late, so the guest house sat empty two years with a cook kept on, full salary, in case the dignitaries came early.” He shakes his head, delighted and scandalized in equal measure. “Two years a man cooked, every day, for guests who were not coming, so that the day they came it would look as though he had always been cooking. That is the most Indian thing I have read all year.” He turns the book face-down on his chest, one thumb still holding his place, and looks at the blinds a moment, and I would bet the flat he is watching the cook lay dinner for nobody. “You should put the cook in one of your decks.” Then he goes back into the page and is gone again, and I do not say anything, because the thing did not want a reply, only a witness, and I am the witness, in the chair, with the coffee going to the temperature of the room.

Earlier, still reading, he had reached a foot across the small distance between the sofa and the chair and set it against my ankle, the cool sole of his foot on the side of my foot, and left it there, and gone on reading. He turns a page. I turn a page. The foot stays. Twenty minutes go by like that, two men reading in separate languages with one point of contact at the ankle, the coffee going from hot to warm to the temperature of the room in the cup, and I do not, for those twenty minutes, want anything I do not already have, which is a sentence I could not have written about any twenty minutes of my life before this country.

Later, when he comes up out of the book, I tell him a deck is the wrong word for what had been on my desk all week. I had a monk. A real one, a Buddhist monk with a condition in his hip that the device people wanted as their case, and so we had built him a courtyard that photographed better than the one he prayed in, and lit it, and staged a doctor’s appointment he had already had, twice, so a camera could have it a third time. I tell him about the consent forms the monk signed with great patience, and the day we lost to the legal team over whether the man could say, on camera, the actual name of the thing in his spine.

Arjun puts the book face-down on his chest to give it his whole attention. “They made a monk audition his own pain,” he says, pleased and appalled in the exact proportions he keeps for things from my world. “For a word he was not allowed to say.” He thinks about it. “Your job is worse than mine, Daniel. I only move the cumin.”

He does not pick the book back up. He comes across the small distance between the sofa and the chair instead and takes the coffee out of my hand and sets it on the floor and gets me up out of the chair by the wrist, not gently and not roughly, the way you move a thing you have decided you want where you are. “The page can wait,” he says, which is the only explanation offered and is not an explanation. There is nothing teacherly in it. He is not correcting my grip or my map or the side the grinder goes on. He wants me, plainly, in the middle of a Sunday afternoon with the blinds half down, and the wanting has come up off the page and crossed the room before I can get a read on it forming. I reach, after, for the tell — the inch of air that would have warned me, the shift in the breath, the thing his face does before it does a thing. There is nothing to find. He wanted me and I did not see it coming, and I spend my days seeing everything coming, and I file the not-seeing where I file the rest of him, which is nowhere I can find again.

We do go to the gallery, in the middle of the afternoon, because it is the one on the list with a clock on it. Gillman Barracks is the old army camp the city handed to art, low colonial blocks the color of weak tea set among rain trees with their canopies grown into one another, the lawns kept and the whole place quiet on a Sunday in the way a place built for soldiers is quiet once the soldiers are gone. We walk in from the road under the trees, and the heat sits in the shade differently than it sits in the sun, heavier and stiller, and a security guard in a plastic chair nods us through without getting up.

The closing show is in one of the back blocks: large photographs, a Malaysian artist, palm plantations shot so close and so flat that the rows of trees stop being trees and become a pattern, a green going to black at the edges of the frame. The room is cold with the gallery aircon turned down to the temperature art is kept at, and we are the only two in it but for a girl at a desk by the door reading something on her lap. Arjun goes slowly. He stands a long time in front of one print, hands clasped behind him, and I stand a little behind him and watch him look, which I would rather do than look at the photograph. After a while he says, not turning, “My grandfather managed an estate like this. Not this one. One like it.” He does not say more, and I do not ask, and we move to the next print, and the fact of the grandfather hangs in the cold room with the green going to black, given to me and not explained, and I carry it out into the heat with the rest of the things he has handed me sideways today.

In the gift area by the exit there is a rack of postcards of the show, and I watch him almost buy one and then not — pick it up, turn it over, read the price on the back, set it down with the small economy of a man who grew up watching every rupee and has more than enough money now and will still never quite spend it on a postcard. Outside, the five-o’clock gold has come in low across the lawns. The rain trees fold their leaves for dusk. We walk back through it without talking, and it is exactly what I had wanted, exactly what I had lit a gallery for in my head at the pool, and I have it, and Arjun is in it, and neither of us says a word about the light.

On the way back there is a provision shop he likes, a narrow one off the main road with the goods stacked to the ceiling and a fan turning over the till, and he stops without asking whether I want to stop, because he needs curry leaf and the small green chilies and one particular tin he never has to read the label of — a sharp yellow powder whose name I keep failing to retain — that the bigger places stock and then stop stocking the moment he relies on them. The man knows him. Of course the man knows him. He weighs the chilies by feel, pours them off the pan into a thin pink bag, twists it, and says a number. Arjun says a smaller number. The man says the first number again, gently, and Arjun puts two of the chilies back as a matter of principle, and they settle a cent above where they started, both satisfied.

“You talked him down one cent,” I say.

“I talked him down nothing. I gave him back two chilies he did not want returned and we both got to feel like men. That is the whole transaction. The money was never the point.” He lifts the tin of yellow powder, checks the seal with his thumb, sets it in the basket. “My father did this every Sunday of my childhood. The same shop, the same man, the same forty paise that changed nothing. I thought it was about the forty paise. I was twelve before I understood it was the only conversation the two of them had ever had, and they had been having it, happily, for thirty years.” He hands the basket to the man. “You take that away from a man, the small fight he is allowed to win, and what is left. A receipt.”

“You don’t need all that,” I say, of the curry leaf, which is a whole branch, far more than a man cooks with in a week.

“You always need all that. You take the leaves off the stem and you keep them in the cold and they last. The stem you throw.” He hands me the bag to hold, which is its own small thing, the bag passed to me without a word, my hands enlisted. “My grandmother kept a plant. On the back step. You took what you needed in the morning and the plant did not mind.” He pays, and pockets the coins the man gives back without counting them, and we go out into the heat with the curry leaf giving off its sharp green smell from the bag in my hand, and somewhere in the holding of the bag the gallery and the gold are already going behind us and the day is moving toward its evening.

The fight is about the table.

It starts at five, in the apartment, the two of us showered and the aircon on and the light going amber on the canal, when I say we should think about leaving by six-thirty, and Arjun, on the sofa with the squash racquet across his knees doing the small maintenance to the grip tape that he does on Sundays, says, “Leaving for what.”

“The table. Seven.”

“I thought the table was an option.”

“It was on the list.”

“Everything circled is an option,” he says. “You told me that. In the kitchen. The pen rested itself.”

“I’d like to go to the dinner.”

“Then call them and move it to a Saturday. I want —” He sets the racquet down, flat, on the cushion, which is a thing he does when he is about to be more direct than the racquet permits. “I’ve had a good day. I had the dosa and I had the pool and I got teased in a lift. I would rather not put on a collared shirt and sit in a restaurant being served by a person who has to perform that he’s glad to see us. I would like the day to stay the shape it is.”

“It’s a forty-minute dinner.”

“It’s not the dinner. It’s the getting ready for the dinner. It’s the —” He moves a hand. “It’s that the day has been a day, and you want to put a frame around it before it’s over, no? And I want to find out what the day does on its own.”

“So I’ll cancel it.”

“Cancel it, then.”

“I can’t cancel it at five-thirty. It’s a twelve-table place. If I cancel now they can’t fill it.”

He looks at me.

“They can’t fill it,” he says.

“It’s a small restaurant. They hold a table from the time you book it. If I call at five-thirty and say I’m not coming, that table sits empty at seven, and they’ve turned away —”

“Daniel. You are now defending the restaurant.”

“I’m not defending the restaurant. I’m describing how a reservation works.”

“You’re describing how a reservation works to me.” He is not raising his voice. Arjun does not raise his voice; that is not where Arjun keeps the pressure. The pressure is in the evenness, in the way he can make a sentence completely flat and let the flatness do the work a louder man’s volume does. “We are sitting in your apartment, on a Sunday, and we are having an argument about the moral standing of a restaurant’s empty table. Do you hear it? Do you hear what we’re —”

“I hear it.”

“Then why are we —”

“Because I made a plan,” I say, “and you’ve spent the last hour treating the plan like a symptom, and it’s a table, Arjun, it’s a table at a restaurant, people book them, it is the most ordinary thing a person can —”

“It is the most ordinary thing a person can do,” he says. “Yes. I agree.” And then the edge comes up under the flat, for the first time all afternoon. “You don’t even like these places. You like that you can afford them. Except you can’t, quite, and I am the only one at this table who can do the arithmetic.”

I go looking for what is under it — contempt, or worry, or some piece of my own week he has reached across and picked up — and I find the words and nothing beneath them. I always do, with him.

“So why does it feel,” he says, “in this room, right now, like it is not about the table.”

And there it is, set down on the cushion next to the racquet, the sentence neither of us is going to pick up, and we both look at it, and then we both, by a kind of silent treaty, look away from it, because picking it up would require a vocabulary I do not own and Arjun, generously, catastrophically, will not use ahead of me.

We do not talk for a while. He goes back to the grip tape. I sit in the chair by the window and look at the canal, and the aircon runs, and the apartment holds its single even temperature, and the silence is the bad kind, the kind with a meter running. Somewhere in it a sentence assembles itself, whole, the way they do when I am paid to make them. It is about the arithmetic. It is accurate, which is the reason it would work, and it would take four seconds to say, and I know what it would do to him at the exact resolution I know what a headline does. I hold it, available, priced, not spent. It goes on long enough that the light changes.

I take my phone out to the corridor.

The corridor smells of someone’s dinner from the far end and laundry from the near end. I call the restaurant. A young man answers, and I say the name the booking is under and that something has come up and we won’t make it tonight, and I brace for I don’t know what; for the table, for the cost of the table, and the young man says, no problem sir, thank you for letting us know, we hope to see you another time, warmly, with no weight on it at all, and hangs up, and I stand in the corridor with the phone in my hand having been forgiven, instantly and completely, for a thing the man on the other end did not even register as a thing. I stand there a moment longer than the call requires. The table was a hundred and sixty Singapore dollars a head before wine. I budgeted for it on Thursday by skipping the lunch I usually buy at the hawker and the one I usually buy on Friday. The skipping has been a quiet routine all spring. $2,850 in rent and a credit-card minimum I am meeting, meeting the way a person meets a deadline at five p.m. on a Friday, not the way a person clears a thing.

When I come back in, Arjun does not ask. He heard the shape of it through the door. The corridor is not soundproof, nothing in that building is soundproof, the building conducts sound as it conducts everything, freely, without discretion. He knows I called and knows what I said, and he does not make me narrate it, and he does not say thank you, because thank you would make it a transaction and it is not a transaction, it is a man putting down a thing he has been holding too hard. He looks up from the racquet. He says, “We could get the dosa again.”

“We had the dosa.”

“We could have it again. The uncle will think we have a condition.”

“We do have a condition.”

“We do,” he says, and that is the dissolve, not an apology from either of us, not a single word of repair, just the two of us agreeing, out loud, that there is a condition, and that the condition can be fed dosa twice in a day — and the day, which had been clenched for an hour, opens its hand.

We ride over for it after dark and walk the last stretch from the taxi. The day’s heat has come off the pavement and what is left is the night air, the drains breathing their faint green smell, the frangipani in the planters along the road letting go of the day’s perfume in the way they do only after the sun is down. The shophouses have their grilles half drawn; a man is hosing the apron in front of a closed bakery; a cat the color of dust crosses ahead of us, unhurried, and does not look at us. Arjun walks with his hands in his pockets and his head a little back, looking at the upper stories as he always does, the old plasterwork and the bottle-green louvers and the one window with a birdcage hung outside it, and he says, of no building in particular, “I would have liked to live up there. When I first came. Before I could afford to want things sensibly.” He says it without weight, an offcut of a man. “Up there,” he says again, of the window with the birdcage, “with the bird and the window propped open on a chopstick. I had a room like it when I came. One room, off Serangoon, that gave onto an airwell, and the airwell carried everything — the couple on three who fought about money every Sunday and made up loudly every Sunday night, a boy somewhere practicing the same four bars of piano for a year and never the fifth, a grandmother who shouted the wrong answers at her game shows, somebody’s rooster that nobody on the block would admit to owning.” He is smiling at the upper story, not at me. “I hated it. I tell people now I loved it. In ten more years I will believe I loved it.” He puts his hands back in his pockets. “You get out of the room, and then the room starts coming back better than it was. The worse the room, the better it comes back. Give it enough years. Even the rooster is halfway to a fond memory, and I hated that rooster personally.” And he walks on, and I take the line and put it away and do not turn to look at him while I do.

On the second floor Aunty Lim’s door is open, as it is every evening — the grille drawn across and latched, the wooden door behind it swung flat to the wall, and she is at her table with the Chinese paper and a small dish of something, and the canary is covered for the night under a cloth on its cage. She does not get up. She looks at the two of us going down past her grille with the look she gives everything, and then she says, to Arjun, through the bars, “You ate already.” Not a question. He stops. “We’re going for dosa,” he says. “The place in Holland Village, the one with the good sambar. We go most weeks now.” “Again,” she says, as if the frequency were the point. She takes this in. “Good. He is too thin.” The he is me; she does not look at me when she says it; she has decided my nutrition is a matter she will conduct through Arjun, the way she conducts the pool notices. “Last week the dustbin outside his door. Three days. Same bag.” She says it to Arjun, a man delivering a household report to the one party in it who will act on it. “Inside, what. One box. Cereal.” The word arrives with quiet contempt, the verdict of a woman who has cooked rice every day of her life. “A grown man, cereal for dinner. Aiyoh.” A small shake of the head. “Bring back, don’t bring back, but he should eat.” And she goes back to her paper, the audience over, and we go on down, and Arjun says nothing until the landing, where he says, quietly, “She likes you,” and I say, “She has never once said my name,” and he says, “That is how you know.”

We take the dosa at the wrong hour, half past eight, the stall nearly empty, the griddle scraped down and cooling at one end while the man keeps the other end going for the stragglers, and the uncle does look at us, and does say something Arjun lets go by untranslated, and we eat with the right hand, both of us competent now, the second dosa of the day, thinner and more brittle than the morning’s because the batter has been sitting, the edges shattering like a wafer when I tear them. Somewhere across the city a table for two goes to seven o’clock and then past it with no one in it, a small empty fact in a room I will never see, and is, by the kindness of a young man on a telephone, no one’s fault. The dosa comes to eleven SGD for the two of us. I pay for it because Arjun paid for the morning’s, and I do the small arithmetic without writing it down.

He stays late. When we come up from the dosa he does not pick up his bag, and that is its own small message, the bag left where it is, and I read it and say nothing.

In the dark the aircon runs its even hum and the racquet keeps its corner and I reach for him, the ordinary reach, the one the night usually answers. He takes my hand where it lands and holds it, flat, against his chest, which is not a no and is not a yes, and for a moment I think it is the prelude to the yes. Then he says, quietly, into the dark, “Not tonight.”

That is all. He does not give a reason. Arjun keeps reasons the way he keeps the other things, in the room he keeps even here, even with me, the door shut without sound. He is not turning away. His hand stays on mine on his chest, and his breathing does not change, and the refusal is not a wall so much as a held line, a thing he has decided in some room I am not in and brought out whole.

I wait for the part of me that should mind. A year ago it would have minded. There would have been a reading run on the held hand, the flat voice, the not tonight — the inch of air measured, the cost calculated, the body reporting in. The body reports nothing. I lie there with my hand under his and find that I feel less than I expect to feel, and that the not-minding is not peace. It is just quiet, the same quiet the chest went into in the lift. I say, “Okay.” He squeezes my hand once and lets it go. We lie there. The line holds and I do not test it. After a while he sleeps, and I stay awake a while in the dark on my own side of a bed we are both in, keeping the line for him because he asked, and feeling, about the keeping, almost nothing at all.

At some point he woke, and I did not hear him do it. He went home, in the end, the way he went home most Sunday nights. His life ran out of Holland Village — the suits in the wardrobe there, the Monday that started there — and a man could not run a Monday from a borrowed key and a racquet left in someone else’s entryway, and so on Sunday nights, late, he gathered the bag he had brought and not the racquet, never the racquet, and stood at my door, said something ordinary, and went.

That night he stood at the door and looked at the racquet in its corner. Head down, the grip tape freshly wound, ready for a Sunday I could already feel arriving behind this one, the whole rest of them lined up like that, identical, a year of them, more. He said, “Tuesday the pool’s closed. They put the thing in the water.”

“Aunty Lim told me.”

“Aunty Lim told me,” he said. “I’m telling you. There’s a chain of command.” And he laughed the short one, and he left, and I heard the lift come and take him, and the apartment was one man’s again, the aircon running its even unbothered hum, the racquet by the door.

I poured a glass of wine and drank half of it at the counter, and looked at the other half, and poured it out. That was the night I made the rule, though it did not need stating for a while: not on the nights he goes, because the glass on those nights wants to be two, and the second wants to become a sentence said down a phone that the morning has to pay for. The rule was not about the wine.

She was right that I never read the notice. The pool put its warning in the window, big letters, a picture. I was paid to read rooms.

There would be a great many more Sundays. I knew that, lying there — knew it the way you know the aircon will still be running when you wake. What I did not know, and could not have been made to believe by anyone, including Arjun, including the uncle, including Aunty Lim with all her information, was that they were a thing with a number on them. I thought they were weather. I thought they were the climate I had moved to.

India

The flight to Chennai is four and a half hours. Arjun has the window. He had planned the trip the way he reads a balance sheet: every leg on its own line, the layover costed, a friend’s wedding in Chennai the week before Christmas, with three days in Pondicherry first, the order reversed once so the wedding fell at the end. The invitation was his. My being on the trip was his too: he had decided it in October, standing in my kitchen with the dates already picked. By then I had stopped keeping track of which of our plans I had agreed to and which I had simply been issued.

He has the Piketty with him, the hardcover that has sat on his nightstand for six weeks. At altitude he takes it out, finds the page he has been on for three of those weeks, reads it for the length of the drinks service, and puts it back unread before the cabin dims. I have watched that book not get read for six weeks the way I watch everything, which is for the tell. There is no tell. He wants to be a man who has read it; he is unbothered that he is not, yet; he can carry an unfinished version of himself across the Bay of Bengal and feel no need to settle it on the way. The paperback on my own nightstand reproaches me by the third night.

Somewhere over the water he asks the window, not me, how comfortable I am flying these days. I tell him I have flown a lot, these last few years. He asks how comfortable. I give him the miles.

We had bought the kurta together, three weeks before. Arjun had a shop in mind, off Serangoon Road — third floor of a building I would never have found, up a stairwell that smelled of sandalwood and, on the second landing, of a photocopier run hot. The man knew him, or knew his family’s name, which in that shop came to the same thing, and the two of them talked in Tamil while I stood in the narrow aisle between bolts of cloth stacked to the ceiling: raw silk and cotton in a wall of folded color, a red so dark it was nearly brown. Arjun took three down and held each against my chest with the back of his hand, not looking at the cloth but at my face, the way you test whether a thing will work on a person and not on a hanger. He put back the red. “Not the red. The red wears you.” He held the green up, considered, put it back without saying why. The cream-and-gold he kept against my chest a beat longer. “This one. You’ll argue, and you’ll be wrong.” I had not been going to argue. He said one sentence to the man, and the man said a number, and Arjun said a smaller number with no heat in it at all, and they came to rest somewhere between the two with the unhurried back-and-forth of people for whom the haggling is a form of manners. He would not let me cover the difference he had talked down. “You’ll get dinner,” he said.

Since then I had put it on more times than I would admit. In the bathroom where the light was harder, in two photographs I took of myself and studied the way I study a deck before a client sees it, trying to decide whether it read as a man dressed for a wedding or a man in costume at one. I had not decided. Arjun had decided in the shop, with the back of his hand, in about four seconds.

He closes the book over his thumb now. He has the look he gets before he enjoys something at my expense. “You should be prepared. Sreya’s mother has a sister in Mississauga; the whole branch came down. You’ll be the only American.”

“I’d assumed.”

“The only Jewish person, probably in the postal code. The only one who has never once eaten off a banana leaf. The only one who will nod through four hours of Tamil and tell me afterward it was beautiful.”

“I might mean it.”

“You might. You’ll still be the only one.” The full grin. “I’m building you a profile, Daniel. It’s getting specific.”

“There’s a pattern emerging.”

He laughs the short laugh, the one for when I have said the thing he could have said better himself. “You can’t just go and say that. I gave it leading. You gave it landing.”

On the seat-back the map shows the plane crossing into Tamil Nadu, and in the dark window the little icon travels into the side of his face, the white plane going in at the temple. “Don’t worry about the dancing,” he tells the glass. The sangeet is the night of music and dancing that runs the evening before the wedding proper; it is, he has told me, the part I will like and the part I will least know what to do with. “Nobody dances.” A beat. “Obviously everyone dances. But don’t worry.”

Pondicherry is three nights in the old French quarter, a colonial town the sea has been working at the edges of for two hundred years. The streets run in a grid of mustard and powder-blue and oxblood walls gone chalky with salt; bougainvillea comes over the compound gates in a pink so loud it is nearly a sound; the whole quarter smells of low tide and frangipani and, by evening, of charcoal from the grills set up along the front. Our guesthouse is white. The walls are whitewashed thick enough to hold their cool against the afternoon, the floor is red oxide waxed to a shine that takes the prints of bare feet, and a ceiling fan turns at the speed of a fan built before air-conditioning, moving the warm air without taking anything off it. Late in the day the sun comes in low through the shutters and lies in long bars across the floor, gold gone to amber, and by six it is gone. In Singapore the sky had been flat and the same for so long that I had stopped reading it as anything that moved. Here it slants, and changes by the hour, and runs out at a particular time. I had forgotten a day could do that.

We swim badly. There is a step in the pool I don’t see and Arjun doesn’t warn me about because he is trying not to laugh; he goes under twice, I come up with chlorine burning in the back of my nose, and he hauls himself out laughing in a register I have not heard from him in Singapore — open, unmanaged, with no room in it that has to be kept.

Out of the water he lies on the hot tile with his eyes shut and one arm thrown over his face against the sun, and the day’s heat is already drying him in patches, the wet going first from the high places, the shoulder, the shin. He does not perform the lying-there. There is no one here he would perform it for. I am on my elbow beside him not reading anything, which is its own new country, and when he reaches over without looking and sets his wet hand flat on my chest, it is not for me to read and it is not a question and there is nothing in it being kept for later. He wants his hand where it is. He leaves it there. The sun takes the wet off it slowly and neither of us moves to be anywhere else. At dusk we walk the seawall promenade with what looks like the whole town: families, couples three abreast, old men with their hands clasped behind their backs, the Bay of Bengal flat and pewter beyond the rocks. Arjun says something in Tamil to two people going the other way and they say it back without breaking stride, and I understand, watching it, that he grew up an hour up this same coast, adjacent to a place I can visit and never arrive in.

The villa has one bed. We had known that from the booking and had not, in the booking or since, said one word about it.

The first night he is on the wall side with the lamp on, holding the Piketty. I ask him who will be at the wedding. There is a cousin who will try to set me up with his married sister; a great-aunt whose sambar I am to praise on the first bite or eat faster on the second. I ask the word for delicious. He gives me a whole sentence and tells me not to try it. I try it. He laughs the wide laugh and corrects a vowel I do not catch the first time and will not catch the second. Saa. Long, open. I ask the word for the place the women will sit at the wedding. Mandapam — the platform, he says, though it is also a hall, and also any covered structure; the boundary in Tamil between a room and a building-with-a-room-inside-it is more porous than in English.

“Of course it is,” I say.

“You can’t just go and say of-course-it-is when I am telling you something. It makes the whole language sound like a bit.”

“It’s a good bit.”

“It’s a good language.”

“One doesn’t,” I say.

He turns his head on the pillow. “One doesn’t what.”

“Render the whole thing as a bit.”

He is quiet a moment. “You’re using it now.”

“I learned from a careful teacher.”

He sets the book down without closing it and turns off the lamp, and the room drops into the half-dark the fan makes when it is the only thing moving. He crosses the sheet. The kiss is the one we have been working out for two years — his apartment, mine, a wall at the cafe in Dempsey, a February night we were the last two in a room — except the room is new, and the kiss in a new room is the thing every other kiss was a draft of.

I have read a thousand bodies for money. I can tell you the inch of air that means a man is about to close and the inch that means he is about to leave. I reach for that, here, and there is nothing to take hold of. Not withheld. Gone, the way the wall is gone when you turn off the lamp and the dark is not a thing you can read but a thing you are inside of. His hand comes inside my shirt and the heat of the palm lands before its weight, and the weight, when it comes, is the full give of a hand that has stopped holding itself up — the same hand that turned my wrist over at a dosa stall two years ago and pressed once at the base of the thumb, the squash callus, dry where the rest of him is not, and the fact of it surfaces and goes under without leaving a mark, the way a name you came in to say dissolves the moment the door closes behind you.

His face goes to the place between my collarbone and my neck and breathes there, once, and the air takes the breath off his skin and lands it cool against mine, warm then cool, the whole climate of him arriving at one inch of skin. He is heavier than I had let myself know he would be. There is the salt his skin gives off after a day in the heat, and under it the thing that is just him, the thing the soap stops being able to account for by evening. He makes a sound low in the chest that I do not have a category for and will not look for one. The part of me that takes notes does not clear its throat. It is somewhere far back in the dark behind my own eyes, and for once it stays there. There is only the body, mine, which wants exactly this, exactly him, the specific weight of his specific hand, and cannot be talked down into something quieter.

He moves over me and this time there is no side of the bed he is keeping to. The lamp is off; the fan is the only thing in the room with a job. The few times I had let myself think about having him somewhere no part of me was working, I had assumed I would do to it what I do to everything — take the minutes, keep the record running in a corner of myself. The corner is empty. There is his weight and the give of the old mattress under it. My hands learn him without asking me first: the long muscle of the back, the wet at the base of the spine, the catch a breath makes when I find the place at the side of his ribs. He says my name once, and not as a question. At one point our foreheads knock, hard enough to sting, and he laughs, low, into my jaw, and neither of us apologizes. Anywhere else I would have had the sorry out before the sting faded. The body does the thing the grammar is for. For however long it goes on — and I could not tell you how long, I who can tell you to the second how long a man lets a silence run in a conference room — there is no version of it being produced anywhere. Only the two of us, and the fan, and the salt, and the dark.

After, we lie in the heat the fan cannot take off us, his arm across my chest, his breath going long and even against my shoulder. My hand is on his back where the muscle has gone loose, and I leave it there. Here there is no separate room booked under the family rate, no desk where I have to find the smallest true word for him, no careful distance to keep in a room full of people who would want one kept. There is a man asleep against my shoulder in a white room the sea is slowly eating, and no one to be it for, and the not-having-to-be-anything is its own kind of heat, slower than the first and harder to hold. I do not reach for a sentence. He sleeps first. I stay awake a while, not because I am working but because I want to be the one still here while the room is this quiet. It is the closest I have come, in three years, to keeping a thing without first converting it into something I could file.

In the morning his side of the bed is warm and empty. The shower is running. The place his face was on the pillow is still warm when I put my hand on it. I put my hand on it. I do not, this morning, try to say what it was.

We take the bus to Chennai on Friday. The road runs north up the coast and the land changes as it goes — the tree-lined French-quarter outskirts give way to a dry scrub strip with the sea coming and going on the right, and then the strip thickens into petrol stations and tire shops and apartment blocks and finally the southern edge of Chennai itself. The driver plays a film soundtrack at the volume Indian buses play soundtracks. Arjun does not take the book out. I watch the window.

The Hilton is booked two nights at the family rate. Arjun stays with his cousins at his uncle’s house in Adyar; I am at the Hilton because we had decided, three weeks earlier, in his apartment, that it was the better choice. He had been at the stove when he raised it, his back to me, which was where he put the conversations he had already had once by himself. The stakes did not need spelling out, and he spelled none of them: a man brought into his uncle’s house and given a room stops being a guest and becomes an announcement, and the announcement would have been made for him, in the kitchen, in Tamil, before he had decided to make it. It is not that you can’t stay, he had said. It is that everyone will assume something they are not ready to assume. There was a space after it where a question would have gone if he had let it. I did what I do with a space. I’d rather not be the assumption, I had said, and it came out smooth and immediate, the way an approved line comes out, which is to say before I had asked myself whether I meant it. Right, he had said. That had been the conversation. Reasonable, both of us, the whole thing settled in under a minute. It settled that fast because I am good at settling things. I have wondered since whether my half was for him or for me, and I have never liked the answer. It was for me. The Hilton was full of men exactly like me, lanyards and roller bags, and I checked in among them like a man returning to his regiment. Two nights of being nobody’s anything, at the family rate, in the one week I stood closest to being somebody’s something. I slept wonderfully. That is the answer, and now it is entered: I slept wonderfully both nights.

I put the kurta on two hours before the sangeet, the cream-and-gold he had picked with the back of his hand. It fits the way the man measured it to fit, close at the shoulders, loose below; there is a line of embroidery at the cuff I had not seen in the shop. I look at myself the way a person looks at himself in a costume that is a costume to no one but him. Arjun comes at six in a deeper red he has clearly worn before. He looks at me. “It works.”

“Did you think it wouldn’t.”

“I thought it might. But it works.”

The cab takes thirty-five minutes. At the gate two of his cousins are waiting under an arch hung with marigolds; the taller one, in a navy sherwani, takes Arjun by both shoulders before pulling him in, and the embrace ends in the precise sequence that means they have been ending embraces this way since they were boys. They turn to me. My name has been said in this family before tonight — the taller one puts the stress where Arjun puts it. “Daniel,” the taller one says, and then, grinning, the thing you say to a guest you have been told to like, “We have heard about you. The American who has never once eaten off a banana leaf. Tonight we fix it.” He keeps a hand on my shoulder. “Arjun said you might be nervous. You look only a little nervous. This is good.” The shorter cousin, in cream, offers a hand at the careful angle of a man who has been told in advance what I am and decided, in advance, to make nothing of it. I say his name and miss the vowel. He says it again, without making a thing of it, and something passes over his face and settles. “It’s a hard one,” he says. “Don’t worry.”

Inside the canopy there are a hundred and fifty people and the heat they make together; the air over the dance floor runs several degrees above the air at the gate and settles on the back of the neck like a hand laid there. The aunts hold the perimeter in saris pressed to starched pleats, a wall of color that will not stay fixed — the blue going to teal in one light and back to blue in the next. A boy of about six in a tiny sherwani slides into the inner circle, turns once, runs back out; nobody scolds him; he was born into the room. Friends of Arjun’s from his Singapore years greet me by name and ask what I am doing for dinner tomorrow, as if the wedding were a thing happening in passing. The room holds me without naming me, which is the thing I came here able to do, and the thing that will damn me before the night is over.

Then the dancing. A man with a thavil — the long barrel drum, a stick working one head and the bare fingers the other — has been going since eight; a younger cousin takes it over an hour later. On a beat the drummer gives that is not louder, only a beat the bodies have agreed in advance to treat as louder, the circle reverses. Arjun is pulled in by a hand I cannot place in the moment, and then by another, and then by Sreya herself. He dances with his cousins, with the bride’s fifteen-year-old sister who is laughing the way fifteen-year-olds laugh at family weddings, with three friends in succession who all know the steps without counting. He dances in the register I heard at the pool. A camera that turned out, the next morning, to be Sreya’s father’s is taking all of it.

I stand at the edge with a glass of something cold and do the thing I have done in rooms since it became a thing I was paid for, and read it. The wrist turns out, the same wrist; then the shoulders; then the feet, and the feet come last and do not finally matter — you can miss the feet. The older women on the perimeter are not outside the dance: they are doing a smaller version of it at the level of the waist, and the smaller version is not the lesser one, it is the same sentence said more quietly. A man pulled into the circle who does not know the step is not made to know it; he is set between two who do, and the two carry him, and he laughs, and nobody counts anything for him.

Once, early in the dancing, before the circle had taken him all the way in, Arjun came around the near edge of it and found me at the wall. He did not stop. He put two fingers flat against my sternum on the pass, a press and gone, the way you touch a thing to confirm it is where you left it, and said, close to my ear under the thavil, You’re not going to dance and I’ve made my peace. The fingers were there and then they were the dance again. It was nothing — a man touching a friend at a wedding, deniable, the kind of contact the room held a hundred of that night. I felt the print of it on my chest through the kurta for the length of a song. I reached, the way I reach, to read what the touch had been for, and got only the touch, the warm flat press of two fingers, and the man already three rotations gone.

I have the grammar. I could write down the touch and its angle and its half-second, the way I have written down a thousand touches that were not for me. The system reaches for the next step and finds it missing.

An aunt I had been introduced to twenty minutes before puts her hand on my arm. “Come, come — just join, it doesn’t matter, nobody is watching the feet.” “Maybe in a bit,” I say. She holds the second past the second she needs. And I watch her face do the thing I have been watching faces do all weekend and have not, all weekend, managed to read: a small widening at the eyes, surprise or surprise’s near relation, and then a setting, the features coming back to their rest a half-beat early, a decision reached and filed before it can cross into anything she would have to say out loud. I have the whole sequence. I could draw it for you. I have drawn its like in conference rooms. Here the steps are exactly the steps, and the warmth in them is real, and none of it is turned toward me; I am at the rim of the circle, reading a closeness no one in it will think to ask me into. She pats my arm once, the pat itself unreadable, and goes back to the circle.

Arjun passes in the outer rotation, close enough that I could put a hand out, and his face as it goes by has nothing in it that is for me — not a face withholding something, just a face that is, for the length of the song, simply not addressed to me. He sees me at the edge. He does not signal. He turns to a cousin.

I decide to leave. I tell the shorter cousin, the one whose name I keep missing, that I have an early call from Singapore; it is not true, and he knows enough not to test it, and he waves a hand to a man at the gate. I say goodbye to no one. Arjun is three rotations into the circle and I do not want him to look up and watch me go; I want to be already gone by the time the song ends, a thing the room can absorb, without comment, without my having to be carried. That is the version with my name clear in it. The other version I have not entered until now: I know what an absence does in a room; it is half of what I am paid to know. I knew the leaving would be noticed, and noticed by him, and that it was the one ask I still knew how to place, set where it could not be refused. It worked. He came.

I cross the lawn in the dark. Behind me the thavil keeps on and the lights of the canopy throw my shadow ahead of me, long, then short, then gone where the venue lights stop. The man at the gate is standing with his weight back on his heels, his phone against his thigh, watching me come the way you watch a guest leave early — without judgment, only noting it. He says the hotel’s name into a radio in three syllables I do not associate with the word, and a car comes.

In the morning I come down too early. The breakfast room is not open yet; behind a glass partition a man in a hotel uniform is laying out the buffet, lifting the steel lids of the warming trays and setting them back, with the unhurried motion of a man for whom seven is the deadline and six is early. There is a rack of newspapers by the elevators, and beside it a table with an urn of coffee the hotel has set out for whoever is down this early; I fill a cup. At that hour there is nowhere else to stand, so I stand at the rack. The papers are in three scripts. One is English; the masthead is a word I know. One is Tamil, the rounded letterforms I have been looking at on signs and shopfronts for three days and have not, in three days, acquired. The third is angular, a Hindi paper the hotel keeps for the travelers it keeps it for. I read the English one standing up — a state election in a state that is not this one. Three days of staring at shopfronts have not made the Tamil paper mine to open; the rounded letters stay shut to me, a row of small locked doors with the light on behind them. I can stand at the rack. I can hold the coffee. I can say the one word for the sambar and get the vowel close enough. From the trees over the porte-cochère a bird is calling: a low, round start that climbs into bright rising repeats, drops, and starts over from the bottom. I note it with something like fondness. They have one of those here too. The man behind the partition could name it without setting down a warming-tray lid. The cousins could name it. The city around this hotel is full to its edges of people who could name it, in the language the newspaper in front of me keeps shut, and I stand at the rack with my coffee, warmed by the sound of a thing I believe I recognize, and it does not occur to me, then or for years, that there was anything to ask.

There is nothing on the phone from Arjun. He is, this morning, in his uncle’s house in Adyar among the cousins and the aunts, and the house in Adyar has a morning in it I am not going to be told about. That is the arrangement. I do not know how I am getting across the city to the wedding. It is not a small problem, and it is not the kind you hand a man on the morning of Sreya’s wedding, so it is, for now, no one’s; it is a fact I am holding by myself near a rack of newspapers.

The phone rings at twenty past six. It is the cousin whose name I said wrong twice, who both times corrected me with the patience of a man who has himself, somewhere, once, been corrected into patience. He does not give his name now. “Daniel. You are at the Hilton. I am passing it on the way. You will come with me — the traffic this morning is too much.” It is a fact with a car attached. The car is a white sedan; a brass deity the size of a thumb stands on the dash, and a garland of jasmine gone soft is looped once around the rear-view mirror. A second cousin I have not met is in the front. They are mid-conversation when the car pulls in and do not stop being mid-conversation when I get into the back; the first says over his shoulder, “You slept okay, na, the air-conditioning in those rooms is always too cold,” and then, “You want music or you want quiet.” He does not slow for the answer. “We are playing music.” The radio comes up a notch, and he turns back to the front and to Tamil. The radio is a morning program, a man and a woman whose work is to be awake before the city is. The city goes past with my window down two inches and the warm air coming in: shuttered shops, a tea stall already busy, a long institutional compound wall, tuk-tuks four abreast at a light. I do not follow the radio. I follow that the two cousins and the two voices and the men at the tea stall are all inside one morning, and that I am being driven through it in a car that smells of softened jasmine.

The wedding runs from seven to eleven. I watch. I do not, in any meaningful sense, take part. The groom is bare-chested for the central rite, a thin cord crossing from one shoulder to the opposite hip, new and bright against him; the older men attending the fire wear the same cord gone the color of the skin it lies on. No one names it and I would not know the name. Late in the morning the priest speaks a line that one of Arjun’s friends beside me translates without my asking, and the line lifts out of the cadence in what sounds like an older register. Across the crowd Arjun’s mouth moves with the line, half a beat ahead of the priest, the way a man sings under a song he has known since before he could read. The friend, when I ask, taps the line into my phone in Tamil script, amused at the request; I send it, with his English under it, to Revathi in Singapore, two rows over in the consumer practice, who reads Tamil and catches what the firm’s Tamil-language releases get wrong. Her reply comes back before I have finished the coffee: that’s a fairly classical phrasing — your friend’s translation is loose but not wrong. I look at his answer. I look at the priest. I have translated a priest at a wedding I was brought to as a kindness, and nobody asked me to. I delete the text and put the phone in the kurta pocket and watch the rest. Four hours, one camera nobody minds, no coverage, no takes. The rite goes at its own speed whether anyone gets it or not. I have not stood inside an unrepeatable morning in years.

The lunch is on the back lawn under cloth canopies that take the noon light and let it down a watery green, so the plates and the saris and the buffet tables at the far side all stand in the same underwater wash. Arjun is three canopies over, with cousins; I can see his back; he is still in the deeper red. A man comes up beside me with his own plate and sets it on the edge of the table — around Arjun’s age, a year or two older, a goatee, a kurta less practiced than Arjun’s.

“You’re Daniel. Mahesh. School with Arjun since we were six — Mylapore, three classrooms, four streets, our grandmothers had tea every Wednesday.” He lives in Boston now, he says; the aunties will ask about his own wedding three more times before he leaves and he will pretend to take the question seriously the way he has for six years. His phone lights on the leaf beside his plate — two kids in an American backyard, one of them caught mid-jump — and he glances at it and turns it face-down. “You should try the sambar. They didn’t make it sweet, which they sometimes do for the diaspora guests.”

I tear the dosa with my right hand. My hand knows how to do this. The motion arrives without the meaning: the keypad code my fingers learned without ever learning the numbers. Arjun is where he was.

I take the sambar Mahesh pointed me to. Across the lawn an older woman is watching me do it — a great-aunt, I am fairly sure, whose sambar the family still calls the grandmother’s. Praise it on the first bite, Arjun had said on the hotel bed, or eat faster on the second. It does not need the praise. I give it anyway — the one word he taught me, the long open vowel, saa — and get it close enough that she nods once and looks away, and I eat faster on the second bite regardless.

Mahesh finds Arjun with his eye, comes back to me, and there is something working behind the goatee, some calculation about how much to say to a man he met four minutes ago. “He’s mentioned you. More than once. Which, for him. He is not a man who mentions people, so more than once is a number.” He picks the fork up and sets it down without using it. “And I’m not going to tell you what he said, so don’t do the face. Everyone does the face. I have kids, I know the face. I’m not telling you. What I’m telling you is that he said it. That he said anything at all. You can sit with the difference.” He looks at me directly, the host’s-friend looking the new arrival over, and the warmth in it and the appraisal in it are the same thing. “We’ve been in each other’s lives since we were six. Six years old. I have watched this man my whole, I was going to say my whole life. Fine. It is my whole life. And I have never once seen him do this.”

“Do what.”

“This.” He turns a hand at the lawn, the cousins, the rest of it, and the hand circles twice, as if the word he wants is out there somewhere on the grass. “Bring someone. Here. Into the, the family thing. The aunties. The grandmother’s sambar. The cousins who will go home and talk, and believe me, they will be talking in three cities by Tuesday. He doesn’t do it. I’m trying to think of one time. One. I’m not finding it. Twenty-odd years. I’m not making a thing of it, I’m telling you what I have seen with my own eyes. Which is nothing. Until now.” He shrugs, but he sets it down deliberately. “His thirty-fourth, two years back. The flat in Singapore, the cake, too many of us in too small a room. Lovely night. Lovely. And it went the way they all went. Everyone leaves. No one came home with him after. No one in the kitchen the next morning. You understand me. So I see you in the kurta, holding a plate, three canopies from where he is standing, and I think.” He stops, and says the last of it quietly, to the plate in my hands. “So. Now I have seen it too.”

“He’s always said the flat wasn’t set up for guests.”

“The flat is the same flat. I am not talking about the flat, my friend. You know what I am talking about.” Gently, almost laughing, he lets it sit on the table between us to be looked at, or not. “I’m not trying to be heavy. I went to school with him. I’m glad he had you all this time. The firm was always going to take him somewhere — that was true long before any of us could have named the city. We knew it at school, even. The teachers knew. I am glad it was you.”

“Thank you.”

“It’s not a thank-you sort of thing. I’m just saying it.” A boy of about twelve comes and tugs his sleeve — sent to fetch him; somewhere across the lawn the Mylapore families are being assembled for a photograph and a body is missing from it. Mahesh stands. “I have to,” he says, lifts his plate, and goes. Mahesh had told me to sit with the difference. I am sitting with it. Arjun, three canopies over, turns and sees me. He smiles. He turns back to the cousin he is talking to, in Tamil. As I cross toward the buffet I pass the edge of his canopy and his voice carries half a sentence over the others — One doesn’t, he says, in English — and the cousin laughs, and he goes on in Tamil, and I do not catch what they were talking about and I do not turn around.

The night before the wedding, he had come to the Hilton at one a.m. He had not said he would. He does not knock; the desk clerk let him up because he said the room number with the assurance of a person to whom the room belongs in a way it does not. He sits on the edge of the bed, still in his kurta.

“I know what you’re going to ask.”

“I wasn’t going to ask anything.”

“You were.”

“Maybe.”

“Sreya knew. She didn’t say anything because there was nothing to say. The aunties — half of them know. The other half are old enough to have stopped looking. The cousins know. The uncles know and won’t say.”

“And you’re telling me this at one a.m. because.”

“Because you stood at the edge for the dancing and didn’t tell me you were going to. I would have told you it was fine.”

“It was fine.”

“I know it was fine. I’m telling you it was fine.” He looks at the curtain. “I’d have liked to know you were going, that’s all. Not to stop you. Just to know.”

“I didn’t decide until I was already at the gate.”

He lets that be enough. He kisses me on the temple. He leaves at one-twenty, closing the door behind him so quietly I have to listen for the latch. He has been up since six. He will be up at five for the wedding. The middle of the one night in between he has spent crossing the city to say it in the room instead of into a phone.

The morning after the wedding I come down with my bag. The checkout line is four deep, three of them carrying garment bags. One is a woman of about my mother’s age in a green sari folded down over jeans for travel, who had embraced Arjun at the venue the morning before in a way that meant she had known him a long time. She sees me. She places me before she speaks; I watch her do it.

“You’re the one who came with Arjun. From Singapore.”

“That’s right.”

“I knew it. I said to my sister, that one is the friend from Singapore.” She shifts the garment bag higher on her shoulder, in no hurry for the line. “He danced very well last night, our Arjun. He always danced well, even as a boy. Particular about everything, that one. His grandmother would let him stand at the stove for the morning milk and no one else, not his sisters, only him. You don’t boil it, she used to tell them, you stand with it. Some habits a boy keeps his whole life. And how long have you known him?”

“A couple of years.”

“A couple of years.” She says it back to me slowly, turning the figure over, setting it down somewhere beside the other things she has already decided about me since the lobby. “And you came all this way for Sreya’s wedding. That is a good friend.” She lets the word friend sit a half-second, not unkindly — a woman of her generation setting a word down to see whether I will pick it up. “And how long are you in Chennai? Are you seeing anything of the city, or only the —”

The clerk calls her name. She lifts a finger at me — one moment — and turns to the counter, and when she has her receipt she turns back, the smile fully intact, as if the interruption had only been holding her place. “You enjoy the rest of it. Have a safe flight.”

“You too.”

She walks out pulling her bag on its wheels. She does not look back. The clerk calls my name. The receipt says I stayed two nights and used the minibar for one bottle of water. I hold it a second longer than I need to before I put it in the inside pocket of my jacket.

Arjun is at the curb. The tuk-tuk he has hailed is idling at the edge of the entrance loop. The driver takes an elevated ramp, a single ridiculous lane thrown up to join two roads with no other way of meeting, which is not what tuk-tuks do; it runs toward the airport between fields and warehouses, and we ride at the only speed a tuk-tuk rides at up there while cars pass us and a truck passes us. The wind in the open side pulls at nothing of mine; the kurta is folded into the bag at my feet.

I feel it pulling anyway.

Thirteen

Chinese New Year

The expats came back in two registers. The ones who had flown home came back five pounds heavier and complaining about the weather they had not, in November, remembered they would miss. The other set came back tan — the places Singapore went in December, Bali and Penang and Hoi An — the pale band at the wrist where the watch had been still visible in conference rooms a month on. I had not gone anywhere. I had spent the Christmas week in the apartment with a cookbook of Sichuan dishes I’d carried from Brooklyn, cooked one badly and the rest not at all, and read three of the books that had been on my shelf since August. The one I cooked, I cooked by eye, holding my hand flat over the pan for the salt the way he said his mother held hers, who never measured and was never wrong. My hand was not her hand. The dish knew it, and came out wrong, and I ate it anyway. The reading patience arrived at year-end and left by the third week of January.

Arjun was back in Chennai with his family for ten days, this time without me. The apartment went back to being mine in a way I had stopped wanting; the evenings were longest at the hour he would have let himself in. The texts were short. flight ok. nothing changed. the dog still likes me best. I answered in the same compression. good. of course. One arrived in Tamil, a full sentence, sent to me by mistake or not by mistake, and I did not run it through anything.

They’ll surprise you, he had said of the courts back home, two years ago in Bugis. They like to. The surprise, when it came, went the other way: a headline at my desk, the December before this one, read once and set down. He never brought it up, that December or since. I looked for where it had landed in him and found nothing I knew how to read.

Arjun comes back on a Sunday, and by evening the key is in my door and the trip is being filed in its usual currency: the flight, the aunts, the dog. By Wednesday the firm has come back to him. He tells me that night in my kitchen, still in his work shirt, a beer going warm beside him, and he gives it to me flat, in order, each sentence set down and squared before the next, the way he handles anything that could spill. “They confirmed it. I’m on the track now.” “Where,” I say, because it is the only question. “That’s the part,” he says. “A posting comes with the track. The posting is not yet a single place.” I say congratulations, because it is the thing you say, and I hear my own voice deliver it cleanly, at production grade, and he says thank you, and then he watches me, waiting for the second thing, and I do not have a second thing. Then I get up and fill the kettle, though neither of us wants tea, because it puts my back to him, and he lets me. We have not, since, talked about it. Giving him room, I called it. The room was for me. It lives inside his apartment in Holland Village the way the cookbook, unread, lives inside mine. I walk past the dosa stall near his flat once, on the way to somewhere else, and do not stop.

My mother calls on a Tuesday at eight in the morning my time, eight at night hers, which means she is at the kitchen sink doing the dishes after dinner, the cordless tucked at her shoulder in the slight forward lean she keeps when her weight is on her right knee. Her side of the call is a woman doing one thing with her hands and talking about another. Water, a plate, a drawer. The small sounds take up the gaps, as they always have.

“Daniel,” she says. “One of these days I’m going to come out there and see it for myself.”

I stand at the kitchen window. The canal is at its low-tide brown. The blocks across the canal are as they have been at this hour every morning for two years.

“You should,” I say. “I’ll take you to the place that does the chicken rice.”

“And the one with the kaya toast. I’ve read about the kaya toast.” Water runs. “Will you order for me.”

“I’ll order. Most of the stalls have English.”

“What about the ones that don’t.”

“I don’t speak it,” I say. “Nearly three years.”

Another plate hits the rack. She does not name a month, and I do not ask for one. She never did come.

She does not ask how long the man would stay if she came. She does not, on the call, mention the man at all.

Sonja told me she was leaving on a Thursday at Dempsey, over the kind of long lunch the firm encouraged when it wanted you to feel that lunch was strategy. She had ordered first and barely touched it. She squared the cup on its saucer and said she had handed in her notice that morning.

“To go where,” I said.

“Nowhere. That’s the part everyone wants to fix.” She said it without heat. “There’s no next thing. I’m not stepping across to a competitor. I’m not taking a year to find myself in Bali. I told them I couldn’t do the work anymore. Not the hours. The work.”

I knew the thing we produced. I had spent three years producing it better than she had.

“You’ll lose the visa,” I said. The Pass was tied to the job; without the job the country gave you a window and then asked you to stop being in it.

“In thirty days. I know.” She turned the cup back the other quarter. “I’d rather lose it than keep doing the account.”

The account was a pharmaceutical client whose disclosures we made survivable. I had drafted some of those disclosures. So had she. The difference between us, sitting there, was that she had read her own work back one morning and could not, and I had read mine back every morning for three years and could.

“It’s the right move,” I said, because it was the thing you said.

“It’s the only move I had left that I could sign my name to.” She picked the fork up and put it down again. “And don’t, please, whatever you’re about to say. You’re going to tell me it’s brave, or that it took real courage, one of those, and I would rather you didn’t, because it isn’t brave. It’s late. My hands stopped doing it months ago. I would sit down to a draft and they just, they wouldn’t go.” She laid both hands flat on the table, palms down, and looked at them. “The rest of me is only now catching up.”

She stood, and gathered her bag, and looked at me a last time. “You won’t, though,” she said. “Stop, I mean. You’ll go back up this afternoon and there will be a draft waiting, and you’ll read it back, and it will read fine. It always reads fine. That’s the, that’s the whole problem. It reads fine. One morning I couldn’t read mine back anymore. I don’t think that morning is coming for you.”

She left the firm three weeks later. She left Singapore inside the thirty days. I helped her carry two boxes down to a cab on her last Friday — a woman who held herself very straight when she was tired, which she was — and watched the cab take the turn onto Holland Road, and I went back up to my desk and produced, that afternoon, a disclosure for the same client, cleanly, on time, and signed my name to the cover note. I never heard where she landed. She had said nowhere, and I believed her, and I still do.

The firm heard before I did, in the sense that the firm stopped saying her name in the present tense. In March a work ticket came down: the case studies she was quoted in needed refreshing. Refreshing meant her quote reassigned to a senior strategist, her face lifted out of the team slide, the remaining faces spaced a little wider so the gap would not read as a gap. It came to me because the accounts had been near mine. It took an afternoon. I did it well, and sent the deck back up, and no one ever mentioned it, because the whole point of work like that is that afterward there is nothing to mention.

I booked Penang from the cab back from that lunch. It was not a plan; it was a reflex with a credit card in it. Her sentence had come with me from the table, the one about reading the next one back, and it had not made me angry, which was the trouble. It had gone in the way an accurate thing goes in, without friction, and I knew what the weekend in the flat would be: two days of it working on me. I wanted a street with none of my history on it. Somewhere past the Botanic Gardens I had the phone out, and before the cab reached the office there was a Friday flight north, booked in the time it takes to do a thing you have decided not to examine.

The flight north was an hour. I landed at George Town in the middle afternoon, dropped my bag at a small guesthouse on Clove Hall Road, six rooms and a courtyard with a coconut tree, and walked.

I had no destination. The walk was the point. I went into the alleys of the heritage zone — the pastel shophouses, the clan halls, the small Tamil temples pressed flat against the storefronts — and ate char kway teow off a charcoal flame on Kimberley Street. The streets at ten had a quality the streets at noon did not. The sodium light soaked down into the pastel facades until the wall gave back a third color the day had not kept, and the carved transoms over the doorways threw small shadows onto the five-foot ways I walked through without stopping.

I could read the Roman on the shopfronts, which covered the English and the Malay. The Chinese and the Jawi were shapes. The Tamil was the one Arjun could read.

I had not, until that walk, thought about him. The thought arrived at the corner of Lebuh Pantai and Lebuh Bishop, in front of a Hindu temple whose carved figures I had stopped to look at, when a man came out of the side gate at a forward angle I knew, with the half-pause to register where the day was, and the recognition landed in my chest before it reached a word. He was not Arjun. He was a man at a temple in Penang. I walked back through the gate of the guesthouse and the coconut tree was where I had left it.

I texted Arjun once, from a kopitiam on Beach Street the next afternoon over a cold lager. kopi at a place that calls it teh. He answered an hour later. that is the joke, Daniel. I locked the phone.

I flew back on the Sunday and let myself into the apartment in the late afternoon. The aircon had run the whole three days I was gone, on the setting I left it on.

The week before the holiday, the firm does its lo-hei in the big conference room, the one with the view it keeps for clients. A caterer lays out the raw fish and the shredded vegetables in their colored heaps on a platter the size of a bicycle wheel, and the managing partner reads the auspicious phrases off a laminated card, holding it at arm’s length, working down the platter ingredient by ingredient — each one a pun — the senior associate beside me leans in: “the fish is yu, it sounds like surplus; the whole thing only works in dialect, half the room doesn’t actually get the puns, they just know it’s good” — and I understand that I am a foreigner being let in on the fact that most of the room is also, in a smaller way, a foreigner to this. “Now the crackers,” the partner says, “and these ones, very important, these are gold pillows —” and the same associate says, low, “Last year he called them gold cushions,” and the managing partner, who has the hearing of a man who has run rooms for thirty years, says without looking up, “Pillows. The card says pillows,” and turns it around to show us, and forty of us stand around the table with chopsticks raised, and on the cue we toss.

The higher you toss the better the year; this is the rule, and the rule is observed with the particular commitment a roomful of people brings to a thing none of them quite believe and all of them are unwilling to be seen not doing.

From the edge of the room it is a take: forty people performing prosperity on cue, the belief to be supplied later, in the year itself.

Jason, from corporate comms, tosses with both hands over his head like a man scattering seed. “You’re not tossing,” he says to me over the noise. “You have to say something while you toss. Silent tossing is just salad.” I ask what one says. “Anything auspicious. Huat ah. Prosperity. A raise also can. Just say it softly.”

The platter at the end is a wreck, and we eat the wreck off small plates standing up.

Sam Werner is at the window with a plate, telling the woman beside him the story he tells at this party every year, and I drift near enough to collect it. “Two buildings over,” he is saying. “Ten, twelve years ago now. Different firm, same caterer. Never confirmed, but. Same caterer.” He sets the plate down on the sill to free his hands. “The satay goes round at eight. By eleven, four of them, in the hospital. Overnight, two of them into the next day.” He lets that land. “Gleneagles,” he says, and delivers the detail like a credit. “The good one.” The woman asks if he was there, which is her mistake. “Was I there. I ate the chicken. A whole plate of it. Nothing.” He taps his sternum. “Twenty years out here. Iron gut.” He finds this funnier than she does, which does not appear to cost him anything. The senior associate is at my elbow again. “Last year it was three people,” he says, low, into his plate. “The year before, nobody stayed overnight. By the time he retires, that dinner will have taken out half the old firm.” Sam is on to the names now, told fondly, the way other men tell war stories. The story is, by now, also part of the catering.

On the way out everyone is handed two mandarin oranges from a crate by the lift, because you do not arrive anywhere over the New Year empty-handed and you do not leave anywhere without being given the means not to. The senior associate from the desk beside mine is the one handing them out, two to a person, counting under his breath. He gives me mine and then, when I take only the two, looks at me as if I have done the arithmetic wrong. “You give them to someone, lah. You don’t eat them. You bring them when you visit, the host gives you two back, you go to the next house, you bring those —” he turns a hand in a small circle, the oranges going round the city all week, the same fruit, “— it’s a system.” I ask what happens if you forget to pass them on. “Then you have a lot of oranges,” he says, “and everyone knows you stayed home.” I carry mine home. They sit on the counter for a week, two small suns, and I eat one and forget the other until it has gone soft.

Chinese New Year comes. The firm gives us three days; the city gives itself five. The blocks across the canal hang red lanterns from the third-floor balconies and the ground-floor gates, and the void deck — where on an ordinary week a man with a leaf blower dries the tiles after the morning rain — becomes briefly where the residents’ committee puts up a New Year concert stage. The loudspeakers reach my balcony as something the hand gets before the ear does: a bass that comes through the canal and the building’s concrete and surfaces in the steel railing as a fine buzz, the railing carrying it the way a glass on a table carries a passing truck. I have not, this week, written a deck.

On the eve I walk out to find dinner and there is none. Most of the hawker center under the next block has gone dark — a handwritten sign in red marker on the porridge stall, CLOSE FOR CNY REOPEN DAY 5. The provision shop two doors down has a steel concertina pulled across its front and padlocked at the floor. The 7-Eleven is open. It is the only lit thing on the street, and inside it a man in a paper hat is restocking the chiller alone. He looks up when the door chimes. “Reunion dinner ah,” he says, not a question, taking in the egg sandwich and the single beer I have set on the counter, and rings them up. He nods at the beer. “This one your whole family?” The corner of his mouth does something that is not quite a smile. He turns the egg sandwich over to scan it and reads the label like a menu. “Steamboat,” he says. “Ten courses.” He bags it with some ceremony. “Day five everything open again. You hold on.” He says it like you’d reassure a man about a delayed flight. I say I will manage. “Can see,” he says. I ask if he is working the whole holiday. “Somebody must,” he says, not aggrieved, ringing the no-sale to make change. “My boy is at his mother’s. I go day three.” He slides the receipt across. “Day five, ah,” he says, once more, as if we have made an appointment. He goes back to the chiller. I carry them home, and that is the reunion dinner. Across the canal the whole estate is doing the other thing. The third-floor balconies have their lanterns lit; behind the windows the tables are full, three and four generations to a flat, the steam off the steamboat fogging the glass from the inside so the gathered families show only as warm yellow shapes moving behind the fog. The smell reaches the street even through the closed doors — garlic, the particular sweetness of lap cheong, hot oil. The city has folded itself indoors and left the street to whoever did not have a table to be at.

Arjun comes over on the third evening. He lets himself in. The key has been on his ring long enough that I no longer hear it as a key, only as Arjun arriving. He straightens the squash racquet in its corner of the entryway and holds up a plastic bag, knotted at the top, the box inside already gone translucent with steam at one corner. “Nasi padang. The stall under your block. Everything else is shut, so it’s nasi padang or it’s nothing, and I refuse to sit on a balcony eating nothing.” He sets the bag on the counter and looks at what is on it: my one soft mandarin from the office crate, going under at the stem. “You let an orange die over the New Year,” he says. “In some traditions that’s a year of bad luck. In all of them it’s just sad.” He moves it aside with one finger, retiring it past argument. We take the bag and two beers out to the balcony and watch the stage across the canal, where a singer in a sequined outfit is doing a Hokkien song neither of us speaks. The chorus is the same phrase four times. The bass reaches us through the water.

“This is a holiday for neither of us, you realize,” he says.

“I realize.”

“The people for whom it is a holiday are across the canal having it for us.” He tips his head at the void deck. “Very generous.”

“Barely,” I say. “They’re doing it for the dead.”

“Better than working.” It is the joke, and I give him the small breath the joke is owed, and neither of us laughs. He tips the box of nasi padang toward me. “Two holidays we don’t keep, and we’re keeping them with the wrong food.” He picks out a piece of fried chicken. “The chicken’s good, though. The chicken doesn’t care whose new year it is.” He lifts a forkful of beef. “The man at the stall called this the holiday rendang. Less time. It does nothing bad to a rendang. He undersold it. There’s a lesson in that for your people.”

“My people don’t have a rendang,” I say. “That’s the whole problem with my people.”

“You have a brisket.” He says it like a concession. “I’ve had your brisket.”

“You’ve had the story of my brisket.”

“I’ve had the story twice. It gets better. The brisket, I suspect, does not.” He eats another piece of the beef, and takes his time with it, and I let him. “A brisket is a rendang that gave up halfway and called it heritage.”

“Don’t tell my mother that.”

“Tell her I said it was structurally ambitious.”

He sets the beer on the railing. We watch the singer.

The song comes round to its phrase again. Arjun does not pick the beer back up. He looks down at the wash the stage throws up the sides of the blocks — red and gold gone watery on the concrete. He lets the song finish before he answers the thing I have not yet asked.

I ask it. “Did you pick.”

He knows what I mean.

It is the third evening of the holiday, on my balcony, the singer going across the canal. I have my back to him. I could not see his face, and I have never been able to build the face I did not see, not his, not in that minute, not the way I build a stranger’s across a table. So I will not pretend to the inside of it. I will tell you what I learned, later and slowly, and what I have since decided it meant.

What he told me, when he turned, was the city. He kept the rest. I took the city for the whole of it, the way you take the version he hands you when the handing is the kindness, and it was a long time, past the going, past the rooms I am writing this from, before I understood that the keeping had been for me and not against me. He gave me London: the date, the gray river, the cold he would pretend to hate, the whole apparatus of the leaving, with the one dense fact of the choosing folded down inside it where I would not have to lift it. I had spent three years building that exact kind of true for other people, until it was the last of him I had.

“London,” he says. He says it to the canal, not to me. “Mumbai was the better story for my father. London was the better post. I picked the post.”

The word lands and I notice that I have already known it. Not the city — I had not known the city — but the shape of the sentence, the way you know the shape of a thing falling before you know what it is. There is a half-second where the body gets the news before the mind agrees to it: the beer is cold in my hand and then it is just a thing I am holding, the bass is in the railing and then I cannot feel it, and the whole balcony goes very slightly far away, the singer’s phrase reaching me down a longer corridor than it did a second ago. My face does nothing. I have spent three years training it to do nothing. It does nothing now, on a balcony, for an audience of one man who is not looking at me, and the training holds.

“Was it close.”

“No.” He drinks. “The story was close. The post was not.”

He tells me then about Chennai. Not the texted version, the dog still likes me best, but a longer one, lower, without the deadpan holding it up. His father does not understand the leaving. Not the angry kind of not-understanding; Arjun is careful about that. His father has spent ten years telling the relatives that Arjun was settled, that Singapore had worked, that the boy had gone out and the going-out had resolved into a result a father could name at a wedding. And now the result is getting on a plane. “He keeps asking what was wrong with it,” Arjun says. “With Singapore. And I keep saying nothing was wrong with it, which is true, and he keeps not believing me, because in his world you don’t leave a thing that nothing is wrong with.”

“And in yours.”

“In mine.” He stops. I watch him decline to finish a sentence, which is not the first time in the time I have known him, except this time the unfinished part is not a joke he is sparing me. He picks the beer back up. “In mine you can leave a place twice. Chennai at twenty-three. Here at thirty-six. My father did it once, the short way, the town to the city. He thinks two is one too many.” He looks out toward the singer. “Some weeks I think he’s right.”

I do not tell him he is not. I do not tell him the leaving is the correct move the way Sonja, at Dempsey, told me her refusal was the only move she had left to sign her name to. He is looking out toward the stage, and his face has the thing in it I have watched him let me see twice, and whatever I might say back would land wrong, would sound like a man producing a version, and he has not asked me for one. So I say nothing and let him keep looking. The bass comes up through the railing into my palm, and into his where his hand rests further down the rail, and the song goes into its phrase again. There is a foot of railing between his hand and mine. I do not cross it. It is not a foot anymore.

The singer is not visible from the balcony, only a frequency that reaches us through the water and the concrete and arrives in the railing as a buzz. When the song ends, it takes a moment longer to leave the steel. Neither of us takes his hand off the rail.

This is still the second morning, before the balcony. I come down to the lobby and Aunty Lim is in her doorway with a tin in her hands. She is wearing the pink blouse she wears on days she goes out, which she has not done in the four days I noticed her absence from the corridor, which means she is about to. Behind her, through the half-open door, I can see the inside of her flat the way you see the inside of a neighbor’s flat over years and never once enter: the family altar with its small electric candle, the orange already set out, the calendar from the provision shop turned to the right month, a photograph of a man I take to be her late husband garlanded for the season. The flat smells of the tarts even from the doorway, butter and hot sugar, the smell of a kitchen that has been working since before light.

“Daniel ah.” She holds the tin out.

Behind her the canary is going, the small relentless run of it, louder than usual, the way a bird gets when the flat has more people in it — the radio low under a New Year program, the rice cooker’s red eye, a niece’s children she has mentioned before and I have never seen. The cage has a strip of red paper tied to the bars, a tiny couplet I cannot read. “He also got new year,” Aunty Lim says, following my look. “Cannot leave him out, mah. Every year I tie. Then he sing more.”

“Aunty—”

“Take. Pineapple tart. Just made yesterday.”

“I can’t, you should—”

“Have to.” She puts the tin in my hands. She does not let go of it at once, and she looks up at me — she comes to about my shoulder — with the flat, patient look of a woman who has watched the comings and goings of this stairwell for longer than I have been alive, and has formed, about most of them, opinions she keeps in the same place she keeps the tin. There is a New Year decoration on the lid, a gold pig stamped on a red ground, that was there in 2014 and 2013, and I understand, taking it, that the tin is an annual lend, and that I have become, without my consent, the kind of neighbor a woman runs an annual pineapple-tart lend with. The warmth comes through the metal into my palms before the smell reaches me. Butter, then pineapple, then the yolk-yellow scent of the pastry, in that order, the lid letting them up one at a time.

From a dish by the door she takes two mandarin oranges and tucks them into the crook of my arm above the tin, not asking, and I have nothing to give back, which she has counted on, because the whole point of the gesture is that I arrive at the next part of the year owing her, lightly, the kind of debt a building carries as deferred maintenance, nothing urgent, nothing ever fixed, quietly accruing. “For luck,” she says, of the oranges. “You give some to your friend. Pair, ah. Always pair. Not one. One is no good.” She holds up two fingers. I tell her I am only one person. She looks at me as though I have failed a simple sum. “So? You don’t have friend? You give him.” She closes my fingers over the second orange without waiting for an answer, the matter settled as she settles all matters, by completing the gesture before the argument can reach it.

She adds, almost to herself, “Your friend. He stay long meh?”

It is not the first time she has asked. It is, by my count, the fourth or fifth. She has been asking some version since the year the racquet appeared by my door, and she asks it the way she reports the pool closures — flatly, as a maintenance fact about the building, a thing she is keeping track of because keeping track is what she does. I do not, in the moment, know how to answer it any better than the other times. I say, “He stays sometimes.”

She takes this in. She has, I have come to understand, a whole second language under the first one — the good, the aiyoh, the small click of the tongue — in which she conducts the other conversation while the surface one is about pineapple tarts and the price of fish. She does the small click now, not unkindly, registering a thing she had already guessed and is only confirming. She has seen, from this doorway, the racquet arrive and the mornings change and the particular way two men climb a stairwell when one of them lives there and the other has begun to. She has seen, lately, the climbing get heavier. I do not know how she knows about London. She does not know about London. She knows the weight has changed, the same as she knows from the corridor smell whose dinner is whose.

“Good.” She nods at the tin. “You eat. Don’t keep in the fridge, ah. Counter is fine. Two days, three days.” She looks past me into the corridor. She has said what she came to the doorway to say. “Going-lah, the kueh. My sister waiting.” She does not move. She adds, “Your friend, when he comes, also give him. Tell him from Aunty.” A beat, and then, because she is who she is and the second language is the one she trusts, “Tell him don’t be a stranger.” She says it lightly, as a pleasantry, the way you say don’t be a stranger to anyone. But she holds my eye half a second past the pleasantry.

“I will,” I say, to all of it.

“Going-lah.” She closes her own door.

I hold the tin. The lobby is quiet. The guard is not at the booth. Outside, the canal has its red lanterns in it, as it has for two days. I go up and put the tin on the counter and take one tart. It is the same tart Aunty Lim has made countless times: small, round, the embossed cross-hatch on top from a tool she has used as long as I have lived here, a mixture of pineapple jam and butter I will never be able to quantify, let alone reproduce. I eat it standing at the counter.

I text Arjun. aunty lim gave me a tin of pineapple tarts. she asked if you stay long.

He answers in eleven minutes. what did you say.

I said you stay sometimes.

He answers in two. she’s been keeping count. tell her thank you.

I read it twice. stays sometimes is the true answer and the small one, and we both know which weeks are left in the sometimes and neither of us writes the number.

I lock the phone and put it on the counter and take another tart. Then I take three of the tarts out of the tin and set them aside on a saucer, the way you set aside a portion for someone who is not there yet, for when he comes, from Aunty — and let myself do it, and do not take them back.

The kettle on the stove has begun to whistle without my having put it on, which means I put it on without thinking. There is a holiday going on across the canal for the people whose holiday it is. I make the tea. I leave the three tarts on the saucer. The whistle climbs and I take the kettle off before it can finish.
