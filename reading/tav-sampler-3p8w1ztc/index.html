The Approved Version

a novel · Jonathan Michael Kay

Thank you for reading some of this. The book is a novel called The Approved Version.

It follows Daniel Reiss, an American who moves to Singapore in 2012 for a two-year job and stays three. His work is making things safe to say: he sits in the room where the real thing gets said, then hands back the version that can go out. While he is there, a television crew films his life, and the version they air is missing someone. The book is about the years that version covers, and the person it leaves out.

What you have here is a sample: the opening of the book plus two more chapters, about 2 hours of reading in all. The chapters run in the book’s order, and where some are skipped a short note says what the gap holds. (If you read an earlier version of this page, the India chapter has come off it for now, and The Onion is in its place.)

Read it however you like, in one sitting or five — the page remembers where you stopped. If you want to tell me something along the way, each chapter has a small row at the end for it, and there is a box at the very end for anything bigger. You can also just text me.

— Jonathan

Prologue

Doha, 2019

My life had a man in it. His name was Arjun. I never called him my boyfriend, not then, not to anyone, and I am not going to start on the first page; but that is what he was, and we both knew it, and it was thoughtful and loving and ended too early, when his firm sent him to London. That is how I have put it for years, when anyone asked.

I lived in Singapore for three years. Partway through them a television crew flew in to film my life. I had asked them to.

They kept my apartment and the morning light through the oversized windows, the pool at my complex, the shophouses in the older neighborhoods, the hawker centers I ate at.

They took the man out and filmed me and the apartments, day after day.

He was shot for one scene, a Saturday morning at a café table, as a friend who knew the city well, and cut from it.

That version has been playing ever since, somewhere, on repeat. It is a story about a move and a big decision. The decision on the screen is which of three apartments, and it is reconciled before the half hour is up.

The premise was simple: a man, newly arrived from New York, choosing between three apartments as he looks to establish himself in a new country. On the screen he is newly arrived. By then I had lived in the flat at Fortuna Court for nearly two years, and had already learned everything the show was about to teach him.

The apartment he chooses at the end is the one I already lived in. The morning the crew arrived, a moving truck came for my furniture. Sometimes now I remember the light in that flat the way the camera had it, and have to put the furniture back by hand. I assume there are mornings I forget to.

What the crew did I recognized, because it is my job. For more than a decade I have sat in rooms while people said things, and handed back the version the room could use: the one a regulator would read and find nothing in, that a camera could air. I found it for hospitals, for a drug company’s lawyers, for my own mother. The crew did it to my flat in a week, and did it well.

I told you what Arjun was to me in the first lines of this account, and refused the word in the act of writing it down. There is a word. It is short, and people use it across dinner tables without lowering their voices, and I have written longer and more dangerous words into documents that lawyers signed. This one goes the way a foot goes off a curb you did not see in the dark. I could never set it down next to his name.

I am writing this at a desk in Doha, in a job I said yes to almost before it was offered. Singapore kept a law about men like us in a drawer it pretended nobody ever opened; this country keeps one of its own. I looked it up before I signed, because looking it up is the job. What I am writing is evidence. I write it anyway, at night, with the door locked and my shirt stuck to my back. When I stop for the night the pages go into the case with the client work, under the printed decks, where a man who found them would have to read a while before he understood what he was holding. I started doing that on the second night, before I had decided to.

The law is a true explanation. It is not a whole one. Neither is London. Finding the word a room can use is my one skill. I could not find it for him.

He waited a while for me to. The waiting was quiet; it was a pause he left after other people’s sentences about us, long enough for me to step into, and I never stepped. Then he stopped waiting, and went, on a departure that had a date on it long before either of us would say there was one. For years I called that London.

Two things in this room came out of Singapore sealed. One is nine seconds of an old man talking at the dosa stall Arjun took me to. His head was tipped my way, and he was speaking Tamil, which I can hear and cannot enter. No phone holds them; I do. They are warm and shut, a kitchen door with the family on the other side of it. I have kept them whole these five years and never translated them, though the phone on this desk holds the thing that would, and all it would cost is saying them aloud once. Everyone here speaks my language. Nine seconds of a man I cannot understand is the nearest I come to being spoken to. The other is on that phone: one sentence Arjun sent me in Tamil, and I have never asked the phone what it says.

And this, the pages under my hands, is what I open instead. I have written about a man once before: myself, in the third person, in an email to a casting department. That was the first version. The broadcast was the second. This is the one I could never produce.

Production Week

Production week was filmed. The man in it walks into the kitchens and says the lines and decides, and has gone on deciding without me for years. Nobody has called to ask whether he got it right. This is the other week, the one I was in.

The night before the crew arrives there are drinks, and I have agreed, mostly reluctantly, to be the reason for them. Graham insists on Neil Road, on the smallest bar on that strip of shophouses, over every roomier and better-cooled option, because his fondness for a place runs inverse to its square footage. He was the first person at the firm to put a hand out to me, at the security desk on my first morning, and he has been putting things in front of me ever since: bars, opinions, people I have known longer than he has. British, boat-tanned year-round, and built on the assumption that no room has ever wanted him to leave it. It is the kind of room where the bar is the back wall and the crowd is two shifts of the same men: the ones who ate at the hawker center on the way in, and the ones who will stumble over there afterward to soak up drinks they have already forgotten. The room runs hot. The ceiling fan turns at a speed that is purely ceremonial, and by ten everyone has rolled their sleeves back or done away with sleeves entirely, and a few with shirts. Mine stays on. I am three drinks in and the back of it is dark with sweat from collar to belt, and it stays on in front of this room.

By eleven the occasion has been thoroughly observed, most of it by me through the bottom of a glass. One sentence has been doing laps of the room all night, passed hand to hand like a lighter, and it finds me again: You’re going to be on TV!

“Daniel! What are your opening lines gonna be?” I have a line ready, of course; I am paid to have lines ready. But I am not yet drunk enough to give this room a performance the night before I owe one to a camera. “Well?” It is Graham, one hand flat on the bar, the weight of his jaw jutting into your space, enjoying the attention his handsomeness conjures, mine included. “So House Hunters International, mate,” he says, his voice pitched to unnerve me. “How’s our newest American celebrity going to introduce himself to the world tomorrow?”

“I don’t have a speech prepared. I’m reacting to apartments I’m seeing for the first time —”

“— the first time?! Are you getting new retinas before the filming?” Graham waits for the appropriate level of laughter to arrive.

I stumble. “— well, it’s – it’s the character’s first time seeing the apartments. I describe what the rooms look like, I voice my reactions — it’s really just a glorified apartment tour.”

Graham reaches for his glass at the stem and twirls it, which he does well, and the shirt goes tight across his chest, and I look. Two years of drinks with the man and I still look, every time, at the same place, like a dog at a door. His eyebrows go up. He has me, and he lets me watch him know it, and my comeback is still in my mouth and already useless. Fine. It’s a good chest, and I am on television in the morning.

“Oh, honey,” Graham says. “They did not fly a crew to Singapore to film you describing a room.”

“They want the rooms described. There are cameras. People can see what it looks like.”

“They want the room sold. To you, by you, while you pretend the selling is news to you.” He is delighted. “You’re going to be on TV.“

“I produce things for a living,” I say. “I know how the sausage gets made.”

“Darling, nobody is questioning the sausage.” He sets his glass down with the care of a man laying flowers. “I’m questioning whether you’ve grasped that you are the sausage. They flew economy-plus across twelve time zones for the look on your face when you walk into your own kitchen and feel something.” A finger up. “Authentically.“

“I can do authentic.”

“You can do produced authentic, which is worse, which is why they’ll love you.” He leans back and considers me. “I want you to picture it. Tuesday afternoon. A woman in Ohio, let’s call her Doris, has the iron going and the TV at deafening volume. Can you hear it? She may even be smoking a cigarette, God help us all. Suddenly you pop up on her screen, walking through a flat you already live in, and marveling at the sheer height of the windows.” He gives marveling a small filthy laugh on its way past, entirely for himself. “Doris wholeheartedly believes your performance; that is the tragedy. She will believe you and you will know, in the moment, exactly how you made yourself believable. You will probably be disgusted at what you were part of, but Doris is a fan, and she’ll go on the forums and defend your decision to complete strangers.” Graham turns to the table at large. “He’s going to be insufferable about this. He’s going to come back Thursday saying things like eyeline, I’m calling it now. Mark it; somebody mark it!”

I said yes to being on television and never once thought about the part where I would have to be convincing. I am thinking about it now.

Shots arrive in a configuration no one will later admit to assembling. That is when Arjun gets to me. He has been at the far end of the table all night, where Graham put him, doing the thing he does at other people’s occasions, which is to make whoever is next to him feel like the occasion; I have been watching him do it in the mirror behind the bar for two hours and pretending to listen to my own party. Now he is at my shoulder with two of the shots, one of them for me, the sleeves of the office shirt turned back the way they were the first night I clocked him across a room, the same decision made about the same cuffs, nearly two years of him by now. He leans in close, his mouth at the side of my head, and says something warm and a little damp into my ear that I could not have repeated ten seconds after he said it.

His hand is flat on my back the whole time, between the shoulder blades, the heel of his hand warm through the wet cotton and the squash callus at the base of his thumb catching once on the weave, a steady pressure I lean back into. I would lean the rest of the way if there were a rest of the way. Nobody at the table looks. They stopped looking a long time ago.

Somehow, a taxi arrives and Arjun and I hurl ourselves into the back. The driver eyes us in the rearview mirror for longer than is comfortable, but then refocuses on the road, suddenly determining that speed limits are optional after midnight. We pay the comically low fare and then tiptoe up the stairs, elaborately quiet and not quiet at all, Arjun’s hand pasted to the small of my back for balance, his or mine I can’t remember.

A crew is coming at eight. The responsible version of this night ended hours ago. I was supposed to use this evening to assiduously clean up before the cameras arrived. “This place is a mess,” I garble, to Arjun but also to the refrigerator.

He gets as far as the couch, the couch he has slept on exactly twice in two years, both times this drunk, and is out just as he slips his shoes off. I get my clothes off, which takes longer than it should, the shirt stuck to me and coming away with a sound, and I get into bed in my briefs and give the sheets whatever sweat is left. I lie there and listen to him breathe on the couch, twelve feet away, and I want him in the bed, not for anything, for the weight of him, for that hand flat on my back again, and I do not get up, and I could tell you it is because of the crew at eight. In the morning we will each blame the other for the sleeping arrangement and neither of us will mean it. Nothing gets tidied.

What wakes me is the call in the rain tree outside the window, the room already bright at the seams of the blinds. It starts low and climbs, each pass higher and more insistent than the last, until the bird is no longer making a sound but making sure I have heard it. Then it stops. Then, from the bottom, it starts again. It has woken me most mornings since my first week in the flat. I have never once been glad of it, and I could not tell you what it is. Under it, when I hold still, is a lower sound, a vehicle the size of a small truck arriving in the lot below, exactly where, days ago, the production said it would. My mouth tastes of the bar. The truck is early, or I am late, and the machine that has come to film me is already on its mark and idling, and I am flat on my back in my briefs with a bird shouting at me and a tongue that has spent the night somewhere else.

My head takes the rest of the morning in installments. The first is the light. The second is the apartment, which has the composition of a room several people used and none closed out: a glass on the floor stands upright and full, set down by a man who fully meant to carry it to the kitchen, which is somehow worse than broken. On the couch Arjun is asleep sitting up, more or less, one arm still in its jacket sleeve, the sit clearly meant to have been temporary. Through the window, the pool is a flat pale rectangle, no one in it yet. My phone, when the search for it concludes, carries the time, and beneath the time a message from the production company confirming what I have been told twice: the movers will be at the flat at eight sharp. Movers. It takes a moment to come back to me. First they need the place empty, because I have never seen it before. Then, in the afternoon, they need it full again, so that I can be filmed “three months later,” settled in and pleased with myself. The mental gymnastics find the exact spot behind my eyes where the bar left off.

Twenty minutes.

Down in the lot, a man in a cap is backing the truck toward the service entrance. The sky is the color it is at seven-forty in July, the sun not arcing up from anywhere but simply present, committed, since well before I was awake. The room smells of last night — spilled beer, the bar’s cigarette smoke that two of us must have carried home in our shirts — and under that, the sealed smell of recycled air and the small heat a building makes by being lived in. There is a candle somewhere, a housewarming candle from someone at the firm, never lit, and for one stupid minute I hunt for it, as though a crew that flies around the world filming kitchens will not know what a bar smells like the morning after. I find the candle. I cannot find a lighter. I open the balcony door instead and let July in, which is like fixing a leak with a bigger leak. I have not slept so much as I was switched off, and my body is still rebooting: the headache not a pain yet but a pressure behind both eyes, a thumb pressing from the inside, the mouth dry in the way that is half hangover and half the bar’s cigarettes, and under all of it, absurdly, hunger, the kind that does not care what it gets. I drink the glass of water still standing full on the floor. It is warm, and it tastes of the floor, and I finish it.

Arjun surfaces in stages, against his will, with a brief facial tell that a complaint will certainly be filed. He takes in the ceiling, then me, upright on the bed in last night’s briefs, doing arithmetic at a wall, and then, with fresh dismay, the jacket he never finished taking off. He arrives at the morning in roughly the order I did — the wreckage, the hour, the arithmetic — and adds to it. “There’s a truck,” he says. There is a truck. He finds one shoe at once and approaches the second as a separate project. When he has both, he carries the glass of water from the night before to the tap, refills it, and puts it into my hands without my asking.

“You’re a terrible influence,” he says, into the side of my neck. “I’m going to tell people.” His mouth at my neck, a truck idling below: I am halfway to hard off that alone, and I hold the glass where it will do the most good.

“You’re the one who ended up on the couch.”

“I’m the exhibit,” he says. “Nobody sleeps in the bed with the exhibit.”

“And you were doing so well unlabeled.”

“Then you’re the curator. You have one good piece in the collection and no idea what to write on the card.”

“I know what I’d write.”

“You don’t,” he says, pleasantly. “Remind me how many days they get you for.” He has heard the schedule enough times to recite it.

“Three. A fourth if their rig won’t behave.” I recite it at the ceiling. “Today’s the apartment. Walk in, marvel at the windows, say something appreciative about the light, pretend I’ve never seen the place. Tomorrow I ‘discover’ a neighborhood I’ve lived in for two years. Then a day of touring two units I’ll never rent and choosing this one, on camera, as if I hadn’t been asleep in it last night.”

“You’ll view your own apartment and be surprised by it.”

“And choose it in the end. That’s the format.”

“So you are playing yourself.” He is not quite smiling. “They flew a crew across the world so you could play yourself. Really.”

“It’s not a big deal.”

“They’ve got me down for a piece of it, you know. Not the apartment. One of the outside things. A coffee somewhere, and maybe the gallery; I forget what they settled on. They sent an email with a lot of capital letters. RELEASE FORM. TALENT. CALL TIME, which is apparently seven, which is a thing they invented to ruin a Saturday.” He stands, phone already out. “It gets better. I am, it says, your FRIEND WHO KNOWS THE CITY. Capital letters, all five words.”

“You do know the city,” I say.

“Twelve years. I have a tailor. I have a regular order at two hawker centers and a feud at a third.”

“Then the caps are just true.”

“It’s a defined term. I checked. Somewhere under TALENT there is a box, and in the box there is me, warranting and representing that I know the city.”

“That’s what forms are for.”

“I’m going to use it at dinner parties. Someone asks what I do. FRIEND WHO KNOWS THE CITY. Licensed. Ask me anything.” Then he reads the line once more off the phone, flat, in the show’s own house voice, and for the length of the line his face forgets to do the joke. Then his mouth finds it again. “My one line is about a flat white. They will have flown a crew across the world to capture it. You have to try the flat white here.” He gives me the line twice, once warm, once confidential, and waits for notes.

He scrolls the email again. “Who writes these, do you think. Somebody wrote this. Somebody sat in a room and decided what I am.” “A committee,” I say. “It reads like a committee.” It reads like me. “No, but think about it,” and he is enjoying it now, building it the way he builds a bit, “somewhere there is a man who read about us. Some file, some form. He read the whole thing, and friend is where he landed.” And then he stops. Not the way a bit lands; the way a man stops when he has heard himself. The flat is quiet for one beat, two. He looks at the phone as if it has said something further. I could put a sentence into the silence; I have the sentence; it is my job to have the sentence. I notice that I am relieved he stopped where he did. I would rather the form said it than say it myself. I let the silence do its own work, and after a moment he sets the phone down and asks what we are doing about breakfast.

“You drink kopi,” I say.

“I drink kopi in my real life. On camera I am a flat white person. The kopitiam did not sign a location release. The café has good light, and that is where Singapore drinks its coffee, according to Ohio.”

“They’ll want it more than once.”

“They’ll want it nine times, and somewhere around the sixth I will stop recommending a coffee and start narrating a perfume ad.” He composes his face and gives me take six: “The flat white here is a revelation. Silken. A little poem of steamed milk. The beans are Ethiopian and the milk is practically a lullaby.” He drops it. “By the fourth the foam will be dead, someone whose entire job is the tray will bring a fresh one, and I will recommend the drink again, with my whole heart, to a man I have watched drink coffee for two years.” A small dry beat. “It’s Singapore. An American, on camera, even on the dumbest show on the dial — that’s a thing here. People will watch. People you know.”

There is no short way to say what he is to me, and the long way would take the rest of the morning, so I do not start. And the label is not false; that is the part I do not look at. He is my friend. He does know the city. The form is competently drafted, is the thing. Two true facts and a role in capital letters, and nothing that would slow the review chain: medical would initial it, legal would initial it under medical, regulatory would not even reach for the pen. I draft that sentence all day for other people. Accurate, cleared, incomplete by design; I could tell you the job number it bills to. It is the first time I let their version of him stand without a word from me.

At the door he takes my hand. The grip begins as a handshake and then does not proceed as one and does not release, holding for a count longer than a handshake needs and shorter than anything that would have asked to be named — our own thing, the one we keep for doorways and crowded rooms, that looks like nothing and that we both know is not nothing, his thumb pressed once into the soft of my palm where no one can see it.

Right then I go looking for the word that belongs under the touch, and nothing comes up, and nothing ever has. Below us, in the lot, the truck is reversing toward the building with the patient beeping of a vehicle that has all morning. He lets go on the sound of it. My palm stays warm; the word stays missing.

I stand at the closed door. His footsteps go down two flights and then I lose them, and the flat is the size it is when he is not in it, which is bigger. Outside the window a myna works the lip of the pool, stepping along the edge in its deliberate way. The myna I can name; I just did. The one in the rain tree I cannot, and this morning the not-knowing itches, a word held at the tip of the tongue that never comes the rest of the way. Two years on this island, and most mornings the only thing that addresses me is a bird I cannot name back. A blue rectangle of sky sits above the building opposite, a clean solid blue, the kind that means no rain before noon. The apartment is still and warm and mine, and in twenty minutes it will not be mine in the particular sense of looking like it: the furniture will be on a truck, and strangers will come and fill the rooms with their idea of what a person who lives here might want. But right now it smells of last night and Arjun’s jacket and the rain-tree outside the open window, all of it still mixed, not yet sorted.

The stairwell’s heat comes in under the door after him and settles on my face, wet as a cloth. Nobody has asked me to be anyone yet. I stand in it a second longer than I need to.

I shower in water that never quite runs cold and stand under it anyway with my forehead on the tile until most of the beer has come out of me. By the time I have found a shirt that is not the shirt from the night before, the two men from the truck are on the landing.

They come in without much greeting and count my furniture with their eyes; there is not a great deal of it to count. All this time in the apartment and I have a bed, a desk, a few things on the walls, and a bookshelf with two of its shelves in, the other two leaning behind it, waiting on an errand to the lighting-and-hardware row on Balestier Road that I keep not getting to. They have all of it on the truck in under twenty minutes. What they do next I have not been told about. They begin bringing things in.

Not my things. A rug I have never owned. Two lamps. A low table, and on the table a stack of books arranged at an angle: I pick up the top one and it is a glossy survey of Peranakan shophouses, and beneath it a history of the city written in the warm, managing tone the city uses about itself. A bowl is set on the floor by the kitchen, the kind of bowl a dog eats from. I do not have a dog. A folded plaid throw appears on the back of the couch where none has been, slightly the wrong colors for it, arranged with the kind of precision the ones people actually use never get.

I sit on the arm of the couch and look at the room. Not a long look. The lamp they’ve set by the window throws the wrong quality of light: softer, warmer, a light a person would choose for reading in the evening. The rug covers the scuff where the desk leg dragged on the laminate the first week, when I moved the desk to the wall. The Peranakan book lies at the angle you’d arrange it for a coffee table you meant to show someone, not a coffee table you used. I know all of this. I recognize every substitution. But the room I live in is gone past finding now, every true thing in it covered or moved or restyled for a viewer who will never arrive.

The kitchen they clear to the walls. On the counter there is a steel bowl with a plate over it, two days into its slow ferment, batter for a Sunday that is not on the schedule, and a mover lifts the plate, looks under it, puts the plate back, and carries the bowl down to the truck with my things. It sits in the truck all morning, in the dark, while upstairs I am filmed meeting my own kitchen. The schedule the production assistant carries has the whole day on one page: the morning block is called FIRST VIEWING, the block after lunch is called REVEAL, and in the white space between the two lines the show intends three months to pass. So at half past one the movers climb the stairs and reverse themselves. Down go the rug and the lamps and the bowl of the dog I do not have; up come the bed, the desk, the bookshelf. The two of them carried my bed down in pieces at eight and they carry it back up in pieces at two and rebuild it, and neither of them finds this remarkable, because neither of them is being asked to sleep in it tonight and believe he has been sleeping in it for months. They rebuild the bookshelf from a photo one of them took on his phone that morning, two shelves in, two leaning behind it at my exact angle, my unfinished errand to Balestier restored to the frame. The one exact record of the room as I had it is the photo on the mover’s phone, taken so it could all be put back. I never saw it.

By two my life is back where it stood at eight, and at four I am filmed living in it: settled now, months in, easy in rooms I left that morning, admiring a ceiling I am supposed to have stopped noticing. The steel bowl comes up with the last load and takes its old place on the counter. I set the plate straight and do not lift it, that day or after. By Sunday it has stopped. Arjun lifts the plate off it and looks for a while at a thing that is not doing what it should. “It died,” he says, conversationally, and tips it out and washes the bowl and sets the next one soaking, and that is all that is ever said. The clipboard had listed it, I assume, as a bowl.

None of it is explained that first morning, and I do not ask, because a man has come in from the corridor with a clipboard.

He is American, somewhere in his forties, and the clipboard is not for holding information. He steps over his own crew’s cable and takes the apartment in slowly, already lighting it in his head. The ceilings go up two stories. The canal throws its light at the far wall as it has every late morning I have lived here, and I watch him find that light and decide what it is for. He reads the apartment with his eyes before he sets the clipboard down, and sorts it: this part useful, this part furniture. The windows are useful. The canal-light is useful. My life in the place is furniture, and by the time the lamps are lit the useful things have cables running to them and the furniture gets carried down to the truck.

He says Beautiful. Then he sets the shoot — not by explaining it but by walking it, stopping where he wants me stopped, sweeping a hand at the kitchen to mean all of it.

You come through the front door, he says, behind your lovely realtor, and you stop here, in the kitchen. Fresh eyes. First time you’ve seen the place. The lavalier goes on under the shirt, the sound technician’s fingers inside it, quick and not interested, and the wire cold against the skin over the sternum — a thin cold line where the sweat has not yet started, the show reaching your chest before anything else of it reaches you. The director has been saying you since the kitchen; from here it is the only name the shot has for me — for the length of the take, the only one I have. You have not seen this place. You have slept in it. You did arithmetic in it ninety minutes ago. But you have been hired to keep not-recognizing your own apartment, and through a low and even headache you find that you can.

The first walk-through takes eleven minutes. You say the things you have been given to say — oh, wow, look at this space; so this is the living area — and then, on cue, the question they want.

“And the rent here?” you ask the room.

“Two thousand even,” Stephanie says, without a flicker. She is the realtor the production has hired to be my realtor, small and composed and in her fifties, and she sits like a closer, very still, hands folded. It is a price for an apartment that is not this one, delivered at the cost of nothing. The figure on the lease is $2,850 a month, a third of what reaches the account each month, steep for one person on a good salary that is not a great one, which is what you are. The production has decided the man it is building lives on less, and so that man pays two even. It is the first number she invents for you. Even now, when someone asks what the flat cost, the even number arrives first, and I set it aside and say the real one.

You ask after the figure you signed for two years ago as though the answer might surprise you. It does not. The bed is up a short flight of stairs to the mezzanine the double height leaves room for; the single bathroom sits tucked beneath those stairs. You went looking for exactly that and nothing larger — one bedroom, one bathroom, a rent a single income could carry, a building with a name you could hand a taxi driver — and you found this, and signed for it, and were asleep in it that morning while a man on a phone arranged for the truck. The show means to spend two days proposing that you have not yet decided. The man the show is assembling does not seem to know he decided already.

He asks for the ceiling, the look up, and then more of it, and you give him more: the double height you have looked up at every morning you have lived here, more air than a person needs in a city that meters its air by the cubic meter. The sweat has started under the mic tape now, the tape tugging at the chest hair each time you breathe in too hard, the collar going dark at the back, and a single bead stands at the hairline, and goes cold, and runs down behind the ear, and you cannot lift a hand to it without breaking the shot. They want the face of someone meeting a room. They do not want the evidence, in the chest, in the breath, in the wet at the temple, of how long you have already been in it.

By the eighth take I can see how he works, and it is not a complaint about him. It is a noticing. He works by removing the words that would have told me what I was doing. He has not said act, and he is not going to. He never says again; he says one more, just to have it, as though the take were a thing already mine that he is helping me collect. When a take fails he tells me I very nearly let it happen and then, at the last instant, went and did it on purpose instead.

The sound technician, between takes, lowers the boom and lets out a single short breath through his nose. The director catches it. “Don’t help him,” he says to the technician, mild, not looking up from the monitor. “Let him get there.” The technician had not appeared to be helping anyone. He catches my eye for the length of a frame, wipes the mic clean of me with a cloth from his back pocket, and does not appear to decide to.

“We watched your island tape last night,” the director says, to the monitor. “You were easy, on the island. Be easy.” Island tape. The phrase locates nothing — we are, after all, on an island, the whole country is one — and I have not worked out what he means by it before we go again.

“You’re confirming it,” he says, a few takes on, in the voice of a man reporting the temperature. “You go straight up to the ceiling — you already know it’s there. What I want is the half-second before you know it, the look arriving on your face before you’ve decided to wear it.”

The heat is in my collar and the headache is behind both eyes and last night’s vodka is leaving me through the shirt, and the only answer I have is the produced one.

“I’m finding it,” I say.

“You’re letting me watch you decide to find it. Find it before you decide to. Ideally, find it before there’s a you who could. The camera only wants the difference.” He nods once at his own sentence. I spend the next take trying to locate the difference between finding a thing and deciding to find it, and fail, and the failure is, he says, the closest yet. Then he moves a light a few inches and goes on without telling me what was wrong with the last.

The island, when I finally let myself think about the phrase, is Pulau Ubin. Not the Singapore most people picture: an island off the northeast coast that you can only reach by boat, a ten-minute bumboat from a jetty at the end of the bus network, old granite quarries gone to flat green water, the last of the kampong houses still standing in red dirt under the trees. The country has kept it mostly unbuilt, the way a gut renovation keeps one wall of original brick: something to point at, proof of everything it knocked down. The production had asked each candidate for a self-tape — film yourself, alone, say who you are and why you want this — and on a long Saturday last February I carried a camera out there to make mine, because it seemed like the kind of place a person with a larger life would think to go. It has since become the island tape. Become, as if the island volunteered. The name is theirs, and I have just used it.

Late in the day they turn the monitor toward me. It had been facing the director; now it faces me, and on it is Pulau Ubin in a wide gray light — the jetty, the tide low, the strait behind it the flat brown of weak tea — and a man sitting at the end of the planks in a shirt I have since given away.

The man is you. You set the camera down yourself, that February, and walked to your mark and sat, and did it four or five times until the framing held. There was a prompt, emailed: Tell us who you are and why you want this. You answer it into the empty air over the water. You say you are a person taking a chance. You say you wanted a larger life and went out and got one. You say you are glad, on the whole, to be exactly where you are — and as you say it you open one hand a few degrees toward the strait behind you, as though the strait were a thing you could offer in evidence. No one gave you the gesture. You found it alone, on a jetty, with no one there to be eased by, and it was good, and it was entirely yours. You say all of it well. You believe a workable amount of it.

The room is quiet for the length of the playback’s click.

You had it on the island, the director says, by way of context, and steps back so I can see.

They play it through, and the room watches it with me — the director, the cameraman, the sound technician, all of them angled at the small bright screen where a man on a jetty is being easy about his own life. He has it without reaching — that February, no one stood behind a monitor to tell him it was a thing a person could be asked for. The sentences are mine, every one of them mine, written by me, said by me, into a camera I carried out and set down myself. I watch him say them. Then the playback stops, and the room turns from the screen to me and waits for me to produce the description again, live, and matching. On the screen the man on the jetty opens his hand toward the strait without being told to. I can see the gesture. I cannot make my own hand do it, not now, not with four men watching the monitor to see whether it will. I have not caught my hand doing it since. Or I have, and it was the copy, and it felt like mine. That is what the copy would feel like.

We go again. The director does not say beautiful, and by now the absence of the word is itself a direction; I have learned to hear the silence where it should be faster than I have learned anything else today. He says Reset, and we reset. When the look still has not come he tells me to take my time, there is no clock, in the voice that said Beautiful and Fresh eyes and Be easy, the voice of a man handing me, as a kindness, a thing I cannot use. There has been a clock all morning and everyone in the room can hear it running. Somewhere in the high teens of takes a thing arrives in my face that I can neither name nor send back — the monitor catches it, low, around the mouth — and the director watches it arrive and says, not unkindly, that we have plenty, that the rest is the edit’s problem.

Before the island tape there was the email. The July before I moved — before I had a Singapore address, before I had signed the thing that would get me one — I found the casting team’s email and wrote to them unasked, describing myself in the third person: a man about to take a big chance, a man about to accept a transfer to his company’s Singapore office. I wrote that man two full years before I would have to be him, and then I went and was him.

I understand the motive now, though I could not have named it then. I have spent my working life sending sentences up for approval, and somewhere in those years approval stopped being a step and became a property of the sentence: an uncleared thing did not feel false, exactly. It felt pending. I had lost the part of a person that can mean a thing before anyone has signed off on it, and I did not notice the loss. The part that would have noticed was the part that was gone. So I could not take the chance until a version of the man taking it had been submitted somewhere and not rejected. A casting department’s silence is not permission, but it files the same way. I cleared my own life with the only reviewer I could find.

That is the pathology, and it is true, and I would rather leave it there, because the pathology at least is interesting. The other motive is not. The reply came and I felt picked, and the feeling went straight down the front of me like a drink. I wanted the forums. I wanted Doris with the iron going and the volume up, defending me to strangers, a woman in Ohio who would never know my rent. I wanted to be looked at by people who had no way to look back, which is the only kind of being looked at I have ever been any good at. I read the email three times standing up and once sitting down, and I was thirty-one, and it was a cable show about apartments. I wanted it. Some nights at this desk I still do, and that is the part with no dignity in it.

Making it up was the easy part.

Saturday’s call time is seven. I am there ten minutes early. The van is already double-parked on the shophouse row with its flashers going, and the crew is moving fast, the fast of people who know the van cannot stay where it is: one camera, the sound technician, the director without his clipboard, a production assistant whose whole job today is a folder and a tray. Half the shutters on the row are up. The first tables are wiped. The heat is already in the street. The kopi crowd at the corner units has seen a van before.

Arjun arrives at seven exactly, which is what he thinks of a call time. He is in a shirt I know from our Saturdays, nothing bought for the occasion, and something under my ribs lets go at that. The production assistant meets him with the folder and he signs the release against the side of the van, reading it first, actually reading it, one page and then the second page, while she holds the tray and does not hurry him. Somewhere on that page the show acquires him under the word TALENT. He hands it back and says something to her that makes her laugh and look at the director first, to check the laugh is allowed.

The staging is two chairs at an outdoor table, and the table gets two flat whites the crew did not have to be told to order, because the questionnaire I had filled out in the spring had told them: the café, the drink, the friend. The phrase was mine before it was theirs. I typed it at my own kitchen table, with Arjun concealed in the paragraph, unnamed. The paragraph has become a Saturday with a van in it. The cups go down. I sit, and the backs of my thighs are stuck to the chair before the first take. The steam is wrong for the shot inside a minute, and the assistant replaces them, and that is the job of the tray.

They shoot it fast, from the one angle, the camera at the curb with the street behind us. There is no lavalier for him; the boom hangs over the table. The director wants one beat: the new arrival and his local friend, the friend recommends what the neighborhood drinks, the arrival is charmed. Arjun delivers his line about the flat white to the middle distance between me and the lens, once, twice, the same words each time and the second time not the same. The director says, one more, just to have it. We have it, he says, after the third.

And then, because the crew is at the van sorting a battery, and the cups have been replaced again, and no one has said anything to us in a minute or two, the scene stops being one. He looks at the flat white in front of me and starts in on it, low, the real opinion, the one his line was the cleared version of: the drink that everyone orders, the drink for people who want the idea of coffee without any of its consequences, and it is teasing, and then somewhere inside the same sentence it is not teasing, or it is teasing about a bigger thing than the coffee, and his voice drops into the register with no one else in it, and I turn my head to him, mid-beat, and say something back. He answers. The camera at the curb is still rolling; the battery at the van is for something else; nobody is watching the frame. I am not going to pretend I kept the words. I kept everything around them: the light, the wet ring the first cup left, the turn his face took. The words themselves went where the things I cannot clear go, behind a door at the back of the job, a door swollen in its frame the way every door in that flat swelled by the second monsoon. It has never opened.

After, the director wants safeties, spare shots of me alone. Singles: the arrival at the café table, settling into his neighborhood, no friend in the brief for this part. But a man at a table looks at something, and the eyeline has to land where the scene needs it, so the production assistant sits in Arjun’s chair. Eyeline. I hear it in Graham’s voice, collecting on his bet. She is given his cup. She sits the way the frame needs a person to sit, which turns out to be roughly the way he sits, one forearm on the table, the weight back, and I am asked to play the beat again, the charm, the appreciation, at a stranger holding his coffee in the shape of him. The beat comes out cleaner than it has come out all morning. Nothing in her is at stake for me, and the take shows it: charm with nothing to guard. The part of me that grades takes files this one as usable and is pleased. The pleasure arrives on the spot. The fright I can only date to later. Arjun stands behind the camera with his arms folded and watches this being manufactured, and his face decides something about the room. The singles take eleven minutes. She gives him back the cup. “She sits better than I do,” he says, to nobody, in the show’s house voice, with nothing in it. “Book her for the Saturdays.” It has gone cold, and he drinks it anyway, looking at me over the rim.

That night, running the day back the way I run every day back, I get to the curb and stop. The camera was up. Whatever it kept, it kept. Somewhere tonight there is a card in a padded case with the two of us on it, the register with no one else in it, filed under a working title, and this week a stranger will watch it at speed with the sound down, deciding what the episode needs. There is an email that would fix this. Three lines. I write these for clients before lunch, and the show would grant it; asks like that are granted all the time. I never write it. Not that night, not any of the nights after, and for years I told myself I had simply not gotten to it. I wanted the take to exist. I wanted someone, even a stranger, even at speed, even with the sound down, to have seen what we were when nobody was watching the frame. Even if nobody ever saw it, I wanted it seeable. That is the wanting. I am not going to dress it as anything smaller.

The gallery is the other thing they settled on. Midday, Gillman Barracks, the old army blocks the city handed to art: a white room the crew likes instantly, because a white room is a listing that hangs paintings. There is no line for Arjun here. The director tells him, kindly, in the vocabulary of a man arranging furniture, that they have what they need of the friend, and Arjun takes himself to a bench at the side of the room, near the long glass wall, out of the way. The shot is me at a canvas: stop, tilt, consider, hold the stop a beat past looking, and again. Arjun’s bench is behind my shoulder, out of frame, and the shot keeps my face at the canvas, so the glass is the only way I can see him: the long wall holds the whole room in reflection, him included, just past the edge of the frame. Somewhere in the fourth take he comes forward on the bench, elbows onto knees, settling in to watch, and I watch him do it in the glass. He gets the produced me, the stop and the tilt and the held beat. I get the real him, at one remove, and all afternoon that is the trade. Once I want to turn around and look at the actual man on the actual bench, and the shot keeps my face at the canvas. The glass is not in the brief. We wrap by one. A production assistant carries the folder of release forms to the van, Arjun in it somewhere under TALENT, and the van pulls out with its flashers still going, and Arjun and I stand on the row with the whole heat of the day arriving and no one at all pointing anything at us, and he says he is keeping the Saturday, meaning the rest of it, and we go and spend it.

The problem with the other two apartments is that they are both two-bedrooms. Two-bedroom, two-bathroom, each of them, and the apartment the show has me hunting for is a one-bedroom. That is the brief: a one-bedroom, on a budget, for a single man. It is also, as briefs go, true. A one-bedroom is what I earn, and what I looked for, and what, before any of this, I found. The two comparison apartments were chosen to lose the contest, and they would have, except that they are too much apartment in the one way a camera cannot quietly correct: a whole extra room, with a door on it. So the production spends the better part of two days deciding, out loud, what to do about a wall.

COMPARISON 1

This is already plain by the third day, when the crew loads us into a van — Stephanie, the director, two cameras, the sound technician, and me — and drives northeast to film the first comparison. The first apartment of my own life is at Fortuna Court, in Balestier, ten minutes from the city by a road I could walk. The show has filmed it, and now it sends the man it is building to the literal edge of the island to look at where else he might live. Off the expressway, the last stretch takes us past provision shops with awnings sagging in the heat and a row of shophouses whose pastel plaster — pink, mint, the yellow of a bruise in its second week — has the patience of facades repainted every decade until the paint itself becomes the architecture. At a red light the cameraman has his window down and the lens cap off, filming the intersection. None of it will reach the show, and he does it, I have noticed, at every stop — the street as raw footage, uncommissioned, a city he is collecting for some private inventory.

Stephanie, beside me, watches him do it and then begins, unprompted, to narrate the window. She prices a passing block the way other people read a street sign, automatically, aloud, before she has decided to. “Those are conserved,” she says, of the shophouses. “The fronts can’t change. Inside can become anything.” The squat blocks behind them are HDB, public housing, and the older HDB, she says, holds its value in a way the property textbooks keep failing to predict. The green netting two streets on is an en-bloc: a whole development sold off at once, by its owners, in concert, standing empty now and waiting to be knocked down. “Everybody’s hoping for the en-bloc,” she says. “You buy the flat to live in it, and then you pray the building gets bought out from under you.” She says it without bitterness, as a fact about the city, as she says all of it, easily, to fill a van, and the camera is up front with its cap back on, pointed at a street.

“And yours?” I ask. “Are you hoping?”

She considers the question the way she considers a number. “I tell people I’m hoping,” she says. “It’s the polite thing to hope.” The camera stays pointed at the street. “Don’t put that on the show.”

“There’s no microphone in the van.”

“There’s always a microphone,” she says, pleasantly, and goes back to the window.

And I check. I can’t help it. The lavalier they clipped to me is off, coiled in my bag, and I watch it not be on. But there is a small black puck on the dash I had filed as a phone mount, and a cable runs from it forward into the footwell and disappears, which a phone mount does not need, and the camera up front still has its cap on, seated loosely, the cap of a thing someone might want off in a hurry. I run back everything I have said in this van, to her, about the en-bloc, about hoping, and find I cannot swear that any of it was off the record. And having started, I cannot stop. The doorway this morning, twenty minutes before call, his mouth at the side of my neck, saying the thing he says when it is early. Whether the pack was already clipped to me. Whether the light on the puck was on. There is no one I can ask and nothing I can play back. Two days of it are on somebody’s card or they are not, and nobody is going to tell me which. I look at the capped camera the rest of the way out.

“That one.” I tip my chin at the dash. “Is that on?”

She does not look at it. “I never assume off,” she says. “Twenty years of other people’s living rooms. The owners step out, very gracious, take your time. But the helper is in the kitchen. Or the baby monitor is on in the nursery. Or the phone is on the shelf, charging, face up. Somebody is always half-listening in this business, so I talk like the owner is standing behind me. Every room. After a while it isn’t discipline, it’s just my voice.” She watches the street. “One time it mattered. A deposit dispute, the owner produces a recording, very dramatic, two hours of it. And what’s on the tape? Me, saying the kitchen faces east.” The camera stays pointed at the street. “You’re new. You still think off is a setting.” She is right. There is always a microphone.

The van slows in Pasir Ris, as far from the city as the island lets you get without leaving it, and stops at the foot of a block the exact brown of strong tea, and Stephanie stops narrating.

The cameraman reads the silence before I do. “This one yours to show?” he says.

“It is mine,” Stephanie says. “I live there.”

The first comparison property is Stephanie’s apartment. Not a listing of Stephanie’s, not a unit her agency holds the keys to — the apartment Stephanie goes home to at the end of a day of showing other people theirs. The production needed two more apartments by the third day, apartments the wrong size in a way the edit could massage, and Stephanie had access to two: her boss’s, and her own.

We go up. The apartment of the person whose entire profession is the presentation of living space has not been presented for some years. The furniture is darker and heavier and older than anything in mine, and there is a lot of it: a Burmese teak coffee table that looks too heavy for the floor to bear; two velour armchairs at the perpendicular angle Stephanie’s mother presumably arranged them at in 1978; a glass-front cabinet with porcelain figurines arrayed by height, one of which — a Lladró shepherdess at the front, third from the left — is facing the wrong way; a rosewood standing fan, motionless; an upright piano with a stack of Straits Times folded on the closed lid. The walls are warm-papered. The air-con unit is older than mine and loses to the crew’s lights inside a minute; I stand where I am put, in someone’s mother’s living room. The props the crew carried up the stairwell become, the moment they cross the threshold, a negotiation with what was there first. The woman with the camera moves between the rooms with the efficiency of someone who has stopped apologizing for the space; at one point, holding a light meter, she sits down on a folded blanket against the far wall and waits. The cameraman sets up a shot at the kitchen counter and abandons it. The director, the man with the clipboard he does not need, takes four minutes inside the place and arrives at the verdict the cameraman reached in one: let’s get the wide and move on. Before the wide, the crew does what it can inside the four minutes. The family photos on the cabinet go flat into a drawer. The Straits Times comes off the piano. And the woman with the light meter opens the glass-front cabinet and turns the Lladró shepherdess to face forward, because a figurine facing the wrong way is the kind of thing a frame notices. Stephanie watches from beside the piano. They get the wide. Between the wide and the closer shot after it, while the cameraman changes position, she crosses her own floor, opens the cabinet, and turns the shepherdess back the wrong way. Not for the camera. For the room. The closer shot rolls, and nobody notices, or everybody notices and nobody says so, and the shepherdess holds the wrong way in the take, facing the back of the cabinet, the way she has faced it for years.

Stephanie does her job. On camera she is composed and exact, a realtor presenting a property, wearing the expression rooms have been assigning her for years. Between takes the face does something else. She catches my eye across her own crowded living room and her mouth starts toward a smile and declines to finish one, a flicker, visible to me and probably to the camera. I come to wait for it.

“This is nice,” Stephanie says, on camera, standing in her own living room. Behind her, through a doorway the camera is pointed away from, is the second bedroom.

“But it’s a two-bedroom,” I say.

The director lowers his clipboard.

“We’ll frame it as a one-bedroom with a study,” he says.

“It has two full beds in it.”

“We won’t show the second room.” He says it the way he says everything, as good weather. The cameraman is already finding the angle that ends at the wall before the second door.

“And what’s the rent on a one-bedroom-with-a-study out here?” I ask, to no one, for the mic.

“Nineteen hundred,” says Stephanie, who has now priced a bedroom out of existence and is standing in front of the bedroom while she does it. “Pasir Ris. Very reasonable.” The second bedroom is four feet behind her, full, with a door on it.

Stephanie looks at me. I look at Stephanie. The flicker.

COMPARISON 2

The third apartment, the last of the three, belongs to Stephanie’s boss: a unit near the top of a newer tower over Robertson Quay, the river a brown ribbon below and the central city stacked up behind it, the kind of address that would go for forty-five hundred a month before anyone had finished saying the postcode. “It won’t.”

“It won’t.”

“You’re getting paid for this.”

“OK then.”

DECISION

The pool is real. The pool at Fortuna Court, the building whose name I can hand a taxi driver, is real, and it is, that afternoon, empty of swimmers and bleached silver-flat under the equatorial midday, the sun at the same angle from before noon until five. The crew films me walking past it, a man passing an amenity. What the pool is, is where my body goes at six-forty most mornings: thirty laps before the office, goggles and chlorine, the building standing over the water while I count, the one thing I do in this country that nobody asked for. They do not film that. Tomorrow at six-forty I will swim it again, for no one, and then come back in to the apartment where my furniture has been returned to approximately, but not exactly, the positions it occupied before.

I keep noticing the approximately. The moving crew, on the first day, put everything back in under thirty minutes, less time than it takes to eat a bowl of laksa at the hawker center down the road, and the double-height apartment at two in the afternoon is virtually identical to the same apartment at eleven in the morning when they filmed the “discovery” scenes, because the sun in a fourth-floor Singapore apartment in July does not arc the way it arcs in Brooklyn. The angle is the same. It sits. No one could sort the takes by the light in them. I have tried.

I do not mention this to the director. I do not mention it to Stephanie. I mention it to no one.

That night I go to Neil Road.

The crew wraps at five. The director shakes my hand and says “Great day” in the tone of a person who means it professionally and does not mean it in any other way, and the sound technician peels the mic tape off my chest. It comes away with a small downward tug at the hair there and takes a few with it, and a strip of me goes cool where it was, his eyes already on the next cable. By the third day my body has stopped reading the hands at my collar as anything but the hands that do this, which is its own small loss: a man’s hand inside my shirt twice a day and nothing in me so much as looks up. Then I am out of it, and the apartment is mine again, the furniture in its approximate positions and the impression of the mattress back where it was, and through the window the clouds above the canal are working through orange to mauve. One degree north of the equator, dusk is a fifteen-minute event, the same fifteen minutes in December as in June, and it does not wait: at the first hint of burnt yellow you run to the window or onto the rooftop. I stand at the window and watch the whole fifteen minutes go by, the first thing all day nobody has asked me to look at twice.

I shower, and get the tape glue off my chest with a thumbnail, and dress. I am going to see him, and I want to arrive as someone nobody has lit. On the way down to the lobby I pass Aunty Lim’s open door on the second floor; she is at her kitchen table with the Lianhe Zaobao and a cup of barley water. She looks up at me over the paper.

“Three days, so many people,” she says. “All for what?”

“They’re filming the building.”

“Filming the building.” She turns a page. “So many years I ask management fix the lift. Nobody come. Now got people, got lights, all for the building.” A small sound that is not quite a laugh. “The big one, with the clipboard. He stand in the lobby this morning, look up, look down, write something. I think, wah, finally, somebody from management. I go ask him about the lift.” She turns another page. “He tell me he is from America. He is filming. The lift is not in the shot.” The paper goes flat on the table. “Not in the shot. Aiyoh. Forty years the lift is not in the shot.” A pause. “I tell him the lift is the shot. He write that down also. So at least somebody write it down.” The paper comes back up. “Good. About time somebody look at it nice.” She looks back down, which is the end of it, and somehow it is me she nods to on the way past, not the crew who have been on her corridor for three days.

I take the MRT to Outram Park. The platform is the familiar cold, the train cooled to the same temperature as the office, as every enclosed space on the island — a communal decision about what indoor air should feel like, from Orchard Road to the food court off Tanjong Pagar. I get out at Outram Park into the evening air, the cold-train smell of recirculated air and someone’s dinner giving way to the heat, the heat pressing on every part of me at once, the way it does here. Not unpleasant, exactly. More like permission. I walk the six minutes there without hurrying. A cart outside the HDB block sells pong piah — tiny wrapped pastries arranged in rows, the vendor’s whole attention on the waxed paper — and something else is frying at the end of the block, the char smell carrying the whole length of the street, and I have eaten nothing since a sandwich off a tray at one and my whole stomach turns toward it. I don’t stop, because he is up there already and I would rather be hungry in front of him than late. By the time I reach the corner the heat is on my face and my chest and my forearms, and nobody is pointing anything at me, and I could walk into it for an hour.

Arjun is there. When I come through the door at the top of the stairs he is mid-sentence at a table near the back, his hands making a shape in the air — the gesture he makes when he is building an argument and hasn’t decided yet whether it holds. He sees me before he finishes. The hands drop. He keeps talking, but one corner of his mouth moves, and I could not tell you what it has decided about me, arriving damp-shirted and slightly raw from the day. I stand up straighter. I know I am doing it and I do it anyway, the way you pull your stomach in for a photograph. Thirty-three.

He is there with four or five people, mostly expats, a mix I recognize from the circuit of dinner parties and Sunday brunches and somebody’s-friend’s-birthday-drinks that constitutes, in those years, the gay social infrastructure of the city. He is wearing a dark short-sleeve button-down, untucked, the kind of shirt that is slightly too big and that on him looks like a decision rather than an accident, the sleeve standing off the arm so that when he reaches you get the inside of the elbow, which I have looked at more than is reasonable.

I have known Arjun for not quite two years; Singapore has had him for a dozen. He is also the person my eyes go to first when I arrive, before the door has finished swinging, every time, and I have stopped calling that a habit. A habit is what you call a thing you could stop.

“Daniel,” he says. He has a way of saying my name I have not heard from anyone else. The emphasis falls faintly on the second syllable, as if the name were a question he has already answered. Graham says it like a toast. The director has not said it once in three days. Arjun says it and it goes in under the ribs, where the hunger was, and I would let him say it all night.

“How was your thing? Your filming thing?”

“It was — ” I pick up someone’s vodka-soda, the nearest one, sweating on the table, and take a sip that is more than a sip, the cold going down the front of me into a stomach that has had nothing since one, and put it down before the person whose drink it is can object. I stole the move from someone in college and I still do it when I want a room to think I am at ease, which is when I am not. “It was a thing.” I sit.

“You’re going to be on television,” says someone at the table, a British man, blond, whose name is either James or Julian and who works in finance and whom I will not see again after March.

“I’m going to be on television,” I say.

“Are you going to be famous?”

“I’m going to be on a show that airs on a Tuesday afternoon on a cable channel that my mother’s friends watch.”

“So yes.”

“My cousin was on it,” Kelvin says, from the far end, not turning. Kelvin is the only Singaporean among us, and the one whose flat we end up in when nobody wants a bar. The first thing he has said about the television all night. “The Penang one. They made her flat look like it had a view.” He lifts two fingers off his glass. “It does not have a view. It has a wall, and beyond the wall a car park, and beyond the car park, if you put your face on the glass and look hard left, the sea, technically. They lit that one window for forty minutes. She keeps the blinds down on it the rest of the year, because the afternoon sun cooks the room to a heat she described to me, on the phone, as homicidal.”

“Forty minutes for a wall,” the British man says.

“Forty minutes for a promise of a wall,” Kelvin says. “The wall they cut. They kept the bit where she stands at the glass and says, and I just love the light in the mornings. She works mornings. She has never been in that flat in the morning in her life.” He turns the glass. “They also gave her a husband.”

“They what?”

“A man on the sofa. For the establishing shot. They needed a someone, and the budget did not run to casting a someone, so the someone was the gaffer, in a shirt, holding a coffee. He is in the frame for six seconds. Three relatives have asked her, seriously, who he was.”

“Did she tell them?”

“She told them. Two believed her.”

“Did she mind?”

“She framed the screenshot,” he says, and lets that be the whole verdict. Then, after a moment, to his glass: “The screenshot of the wall. Not the husband.” He drinks. “She is particular.”

The British man is delighted. “They can just do that? Invent a husband?”

“They can do anything the schedule allows,” Kelvin says. “A husband is cheap. The gaffer was already on payroll.”

Under the table’s laugh, Arjun turns his head my way. “You should ask for one. They have the shirt.”

I laugh where the table laughs. Under the laugh I do the other sum.

Her flat was short a man, so the show added one. Mine has one too many.

“Bargain, as husbands go,” the British man says.

“And returnable,” Kelvin says. “Shirt back to wardrobe, man back to the lights.” And then, to his glass, at a volume built to be missable: “Subtraction’s cheaper, of course. No wardrobe involved.”

The table moves on. The sum does not. One word on a form, FRIEND, and the flat comes out of the edit with the right number of people in it, which is one.

Kelvin wears the television the way he wears the rest of it, lightly: a polo gone soft from washing, the weekend uniform of a man whose weekday hours bill at a rate you can read in how he holds his glass. He has been coming to this bar since before it had the name it has now, since before most of the men at the table had a reason to know the city was a place a person could be from. The bartender sets down a drink he has not ordered; Kelvin lifts two fingers off the glass and lets it sit. He checks his phone once, thumbs a short reply, turns it face-down. Then he goes back to listening. He listens to expats narrate the city to itself without correcting. When my eyes go to Arjun and stay past what the room allows, Kelvin catches it, and shifts his knee, and makes room he does not need to make.

Arjun leans in. “What were you doing?” he says, quieter.

“Saying things I’d already said. They had a tape of me from February. They’d been playing it back.” I drink. “And two years ago I wrote them a scene I didn’t have yet. Me, three months in, with friends I hadn’t made.”

He looks at me with a patience I cannot quite place, except that his own work runs on the same machinery as mine: he spends his days turning what a room wants into the version that can leave the room. “Did they shoot it?”

“They thanked me for the suggestion.”

He turns that over before he answers. “They’ll use the worse take,” he says. “They always use the worse take.”

“That’s a grim thing to know about your industry,” I say.

“It’s a grim thing to know about people.” He says it warmly, which is how he says the grim things. “You give them the true one and the near-one, side by side, and they reach for the near-one every time. You should put that on your tape.”

“They have my tape.”

“They have the February one, the one they kept playing back.” He turns the words over. “Was it the worse take of you, or the true one?”

“The true one. That was the problem.”

“Of course it was. You can’t compete with the version of you that didn’t know it was being.” He stops on the missing word. Two fingers and a thumb at the rim of his glass, he turns it a quarter turn where it stands, then another, unhurried, while he goes and gets the word. “Watched. Graded. Whichever.” He lets go of the glass without having drunk from it. “That’s the con, then. They find the one tape where you forgot to perform, and they spend three days asking you to perform it.”

“Then they already know,” I say.

“Then they already know,” he agrees.

He is leaning back with one forearm along the rail of the booth, the sleeve fallen off the wrist, in no hurry, a man who has decided the night can take as long as it wants, and I want the night to take as long as he wants. I spend my days making the version of a thing a client is allowed to be shown — the sayable cut, the line Legal will clear, the sentence with the weather taken out of it. He has just done it to my own evening, better than I would have, and I would like to kiss him for it, at the table, which is a sentence I can finish at this desk and could not have started at that table.

Someone orders a round: vodka-sodas, the cheapest thing at the bar, the drink you order when the bar is the point and not the drink, and the first one goes down in three swallows and I feel it land, the day coming off me a layer at a time. The booth is a green tufted vinyl banquette that has absorbed a decade of someone else’s perfumes; the air conditioning is fighting the July heat and winning by a narrow margin and the fluorescent panel above the bar is humming the single flat note every Singapore commercial fluorescent holds, the one your ear stops hearing and your skull keeps. Arjun is talking to someone across the table about a work thing — a client, a deadline — and I am not listening to the words. I am listening to the sound of him, which I have no file for: lower than his work voice and quicker, sentences left unfinished where the other man has already caught them, nothing in it framed for anyone. He laughs in the middle of his own point and does not go back for it.

The scene I wrote them sits on a list somewhere, available to anyone else the show films, in any other city. And here I am at the bar with a vodka-soda and a table of people who know my name and my job and my orientation and who do not need me to pretend any of these things are different from what they are, in a city where what we are doing is against the law. Not the drinking, not the sitting, but the being, the ambient fact of men attracted to men, sitting closely together at a table on a street, some hugging, some singing, all absurdly sweaty. The law is still on the books: 377A, colonial, a crime the state keeps and does not enforce. When I say so, Arjun does not look up from his drink. “They left the light on in a room nobody uses,” he says. The room nobody uses has a switch in me all the same. A hand at the back of my neck in a bar like this one, and my eyes run their small circuit before anything in me has decided to. Door, bar, street window.

The second one is going down faster than the first, and the table has started to tilt very slightly toward him, which is not the table. Arjun catches my eye across it. “And here. After.”

“After,” I say.

He does something with his mouth that is not a smile and not a question. Not here, not yet.

Outside, two men share a cigarette in the shophouse doorway opposite, one of them watching me through the smoke. I will be back on camera in the morning. The director will want one more walk past the pool. The light will be the same. It was. The light, at least, I can vouch for. The rest of me, that night: two vodka-sodas in, the collar gone stiff with salt, his knee an inch from mine under the table. I wanted the after. I sat there and wanted it, sweat through to the shirt, and drank, and put my knee against his.

Six

Sunday

This is what the staying looks like.

The racquet has been by my door since March.

It is Arjun’s. It leans in the corner of the entryway where a person would keep an umbrella, head down, the grip wrapped in a tape gone gray along the edges, and it has been there long enough that I have stopped seeing it. Arjun does not live in the apartment. He has a flat in Holland Village with his own bed and his own kitchen and a closet of the suits he wears to an office I have been inside exactly once. Lately the office has been circling him: a bigger job, a floor higher and a continent over, that he brings up with his mouth full and his eyes on the television and then does not bring up again. I have not asked, and he has not said. On the nights he stays at Fortuna Court he brings a bag, and the bag goes home with him, and the racquet does not. Neither of us has ever said anything about the racquet. It arrived one Saturday and then it was a fact of the apartment, the way the aircon is a fact of the apartment, and by this summer I have learned not to ask a question whose answer I already have and do not want said aloud.

It rained in the night. I know it before I am properly awake, from the smell that comes in under the door of the balcony, wet concrete and soaked leaf, green, with the iron tang of the drains running somewhere below and the rain trees letting go of what they caught all at once when the wind moves them. The light is not yet light, only the gray that comes before it, and the aircon has been off all night because Arjun cannot sleep in it and I have learned to sleep without it, in the thick warm dark with the window open a hand’s width and the city breathing in. He is on his back, one arm off the side of the bed, all the way under, breathing in the slow way I read before I learned anything else about how he sleeps. The sheet is pushed down. The heat of him is along one whole side of me, hip to ankle, and the cooler air from the window is along the other, and I lie there half a body warm and do nothing about either, because there is nowhere I have to be and no version of the morning I have to produce. I could put my mouth on the shoulder nearest me. I want to, and I don’t, because I would rather have him asleep against me for twenty more minutes than awake and looking for his shoes. The balcony rail outside is beaded with rain going slowly to nothing in the first of the warmth. I count the twenty minutes in his breathing and get to eleven, which for me is a personal best.

Before he goes, there is the milk. He cannot start a Sunday without it warm — not heated in the microwave, which he holds in something close to moral contempt, but stood over in the small steel pan he brought up from his own kitchen one weekend and never took home, the heat low, the surface watched for the first crawl of a skin. He drinks it plain, no coffee in it, no sugar, a thing his grandmother did and her mother before her, he told me once and only once, in the offhand way he gives me the load-bearing facts so I will not make an occasion of them. I have learned to leave the pan to him. The one time I tried to do it for him I let it climb too fast and a skin came up gray and wrinkled at the edge.

“It’s fine,” I said. “It’s milk.”

He looked into the pan, and then at me, with enormous gentleness. “It is not fine. You boiled it. One doesn’t boil it, one stands with it.” He took the spoon out of my hand without any drama at all. “You can do many things, Daniel. You will not be doing this.” And he scraped the skin off the edge and started again, patient with the milk in a way he was patient with almost nothing else, and let me keep the toast.

This morning he has it going at quarter to eight, one hand on the pan handle, the other turning the spoon in slow figure-eights, while the radio he keeps tuned to a station I cannot find again when he is gone plays something with a flute in it under a man reading the shipping notices. The kaya toast is already gone from me and I am hungry again, and I sit at the table and watch the back of his neck and want two things at once and will get neither before eight. There is a steel bowl on the counter under a plate; he soaked rice and dal in it the night before last and ground it yesterday, and the batter is fermenting toward the middle of the week, when he says the sourness will be right, and the bowl sitting there working, a thing started days before the meal it is for, is the most domestic object in the apartment, more than the racquet, more than the key. It smells, already, faintly sour under the plate when I walk past it, and I walk past it more than I need to.

He has set a second bowl to soak before he leaves, smaller, the rice and dal for next Sunday’s, a week ahead of when anyone could need it. The dal settles and ticks against the steel as the water takes it; the counter still smells of the ghee and the cardamom he cracked under the flat of the knife. He does it without telling me he is doing it, and when I ask him why two he says, “So there is always one going,” and does not look up from the soak, and I understand that there is a version of this kitchen, running on a clock I cannot see, that he is keeping for a reason that is his, and that I am not going to be given this morning. He wants the batter working on my counter and not on his own. That much I can read, at quarter to eight, with the pan going and the aircon still off. The why he keeps. He pours the milk into the one glass he uses, rinses the pan, sets it upside down on the rack, and drinks standing at the window in the flat early light. His throat moves. There is a white line on his upper lip after, and he takes it with his tongue without knowing he has done it, and I say nothing, because there is nothing in the moment that wants saying, and because I would have liked to do that for him.

Before he leaves he lifts the upturned plate off the bowl and looks at the batter underneath and stirs it once with the back of a spoon, down to the bottom and up, reading it the way he reads the milk and the price and the cricket, for whether it is doing what it should. “It’s coming,” he says, to the bowl. “Slowly. Your apartment is cold for it. In Chennai this would be done by lunch.” He bends and smells it, frowns, not displeased, the frown of a man taking a reading he trusts more than the calendar. “Another day. Maybe two. It tells you. You don’t tell it.” He settles the plate back. Under his breath, once, a word in Tamil, to the batter, not offered for translation. “My mother would have it on the stone by now. She did not measure anything. You ask her how much and she shows you the hand.” He cups his palm, holds it out, a measure that is no measure. “This much. And it was always right, and if you used the same hand it came out wrong.” He says it lightly, the grandmother and now the mother handed across in one Sunday, and I want the whole of it, the stone and the hand, and I know better than to ask, and then he washes the spoon and sets it on the rack and goes to find his shoes, and the bowl sits on the counter working at its own speed, slow, in the cold of my apartment.

He has gone to the courts at eight. He plays squash on Sunday mornings at a club near his flat, with a rotation of men he has been playing with for years: a corporate lawyer, an Australian whose name I never retain, a Tamil uncle. He comes back from it unhurried, his hair wet from the club shower and combed with his fingers, smelling of a soap that is not mine, and I like the wrong soap on him. I like that he comes back smelling of somewhere I wasn’t and gets into my shower anyway.

In the kitchen, in the slow hour I have to myself after he leaves, I notice the salt-and-pepper grinder is still where I set it down after dinner, the width of its own base to the right of where it lives. Yesterday afternoon, while I was in the shower, he had moved it home; I never saw him do it, but I have reached for that grinder often enough to feel when it is off. Counter, stove, grinder, press. He moved it without saying he had moved it, the way he moves a book on the nightstand back into the order of the books beside it. The kitchen has a settled grammar I did not put there. I pick up the grinder and set it down on the side the kitchen has assigned it, and stand there with my hand still on it.

There are other corrections. The dish towel hangs folded in thirds over the oven handle, which is not how I hang a dish towel; I bunch it through the handle, and at some point in the spring it stopped being bunched and started being folded in thirds, and I have never once seen him do it, only the result, every morning, the towel in thirds. The mugs face the same way on the shelf now, handles all to the right. The bag of his particular tea, the one with the cardamom already in it that he buys at the Mustafa Centre at one in the morning because that is when he ends up at the Mustafa Centre, lives in the cupboard behind my coffee, where I would never have put it and where it is, I have come to understand, exactly correct.

None of it was negotiated. The apartment was mine and then, by a slow accretion of small things done right and never mentioned, it became a place that a second person also lived inside the logic of, and I find I can stand in my own kitchen and read, in the position of every object, the hand of a man who is at this hour on a squash court across the city, missing a ball on purpose to keep a point alive. This is the one hour of the week I am alone in this country and not lonely in it, and I know the difference. Lonely is the balcony at eleven at night, the lights on across the canal, a whole city that needs nothing from me on a Sunday. I can still walk that kitchen from here, object by object, the grinder on its side, the towel in its thirds.

Once, early, I caught him at it. He had the grinder in his hand and the guilty stillness of a man who has been seen moving a thing he had decided to move quietly.

“You moved the grinder,” I said.

“I moved the grinder.”

“There’s a right side.”

“There is a right side. You keep putting it on the side where the cloth gets caught when you wipe down. I am not saying anything about it. I am only correcting it.” He set it down, an inch to the left, where it has lived ever since. “When you are not here I leave it on your side. So it doesn’t get used to me.”

He went to find his shoes, and I never moved the grinder back.

There had been a first morning, a year and some months back, before the racquet had a corner and the milk had a pan, when he stayed over for the first time.

We had come up late, and it had been the good kind of late, and then he was asleep, on his back, one arm off the side of the bed, all the way under, and I was awake, all the way awake, because of the plain animal fact of another body in the bed, taking up room I had gotten used to having, breathing on a rhythm that was not mine and would not adjust. The window was open a hand’s width and the night came in at intervals, the drains, the frangipani, once a motorbike going up through its gears somewhere past the canal. At some point he had shifted in his sleep, and where his arm had been against mine there was now a gap the width of a hand, and the skin of my forearm was keeping the warmth of him and losing it, and I wanted the arm back. That arm, at that stretch of skin. I was hard, on and off, the whole gray hour, and it was not even about that; it was the arm. I lay in the gray-before-light wanting it and not moving, because a man you have slept beside once is not yet a man you can reach for in his sleep.

I could have slept. I did not want the morning to start, because starting it would spend it, and I did not yet know there would be another one.

When he woke he did not reach for his phone. He turned his head, found me already awake, and looked at me for a while, unhurried, wanting nothing I could detect except to be looking. “You’ve been up,” he said. “A while,” I said. “You watch people sleep. Of course you do.” He did not seem to mind being watched. He did not offer a sentence about the night or ask for mine. He pulled me in by the back of the neck, dry-palmed, the callus at the base of the thumb, and we did not get up for the milk for a long time, and I was loud, for me, which is not very, and neither of us made anything of it.

I have a list. I have kept it.

It is a Sunday, and Sundays do not need lists.

The night before, while Arjun slept in the manner I had already learned to read, I took a Calder Rowe meeting agenda from my bag, turned it over, and wrote on the blank side a sequence. Dosa. The pool. The gallery at Gillman Barracks, with the show that is closing. A table at seven at a place in Tiong Bahru that takes bookings. I wrote them in order, and I circled the table, because the table is the part I am least sure of and most want: a room with cloth on the tables and two hours across from a man nobody in that room has to be told anything about, at a price I have decided not to look at.

He lets himself in a little after ten.

He has a key. He has had a key since the spring, since around the time the racquet appeared, and the key arrived the way the racquet arrived, which is to say it turned out, one day, to exist. I had the copy cut at the hardware shop on Balestier Road, on an errand folded into other errands, and I set it on the counter without ceremony. He picked it up without ceremony and put it on his ring.

I hear the door, and then the specific sequence of Arjun arriving: the bag set down, the shoes off and stood against the wall rather than left where they fall. He comes into the kitchen carrying the heat of the walk from the taxi, his shirt dark down the spine and stuck there, and he looks at me, and then at the agenda on the counter, turned over, my handwriting on the back of it.

“What’s that,” he says.

“Nothing. A list.”

“A list.” He picks it up and reads it top to bottom, unhurried, and something moves in his face that I do not get to name before he speaks. “We have a list.”

“It’s options.”

“You’ve circled one of the options.”

“That’s not a circle. That’s where the pen — it’s where I rested the pen.”

“You rested the pen,” he says, “in a perfect ring, around the dinner.” He sets the agenda down, face up, so that it is a Calder Rowe agenda again, and not the back of one. “Daniel. It’s Sunday. You’ve project-managed a Sunday.”

“I’ve made some suggestions about a Sunday.”

“There’s a dependency.” He taps the list. “Look. The pool is gated to the dosa. The gallery is gated to the pool. The dinner is gated to the gallery. If the dosa runs long, the whole afternoon slips. You’ve built a critical path, Daniel. On a Sunday you have built a critical path.” He laughs. Not the wide one, the other one, the short exhale through the nose he gives a thing that is true and that he is going to let me keep anyway. He goes to the sink and drinks a glass of water standing up, and I watch his throat go, and the wet shirt, and want him out of it, in my kitchen, at ten in the morning, over a list, and the thing I settle for wanting is to be forgiven for the list, and I am not going to be, not yet. Fine. I would make the list again.

We go for the dosa.

The stall is in Holland Village, near his flat, the same stall he took me to on the second Saturday, when my fingers were too slow and the dosa tore wrong and the sambar ran down my wrist and he reached over and adjusted my grip, without looking up, the way you adjust someone’s hold on a racquet. I understand it now as the first lesson in a curriculum neither of us ever called one. It went on, Saturdays and Sundays, at this stall and at others, the places off Serangoon Road where he orders in the script I cannot read, and the proof of it is that my hands have learned.

This is a small thing to be proud of. I am proud of it anyway. I tear the dosa with the right hand, thumb and the first two fingers, and scoop the sambar with the torn edge, and I do it without watching my own hand do it, and I am hungry enough that the first piece goes in too hot and I take it anyway, the roof of my mouth paying for it, and a run of sambar goes down my thumb and I get it with my tongue before the wrist. Arjun, across the steel table, watches me not watch my hand, and eats his own.

The radio over the drinks counter is carrying the cricket, and the man at the griddle has it turned up for himself and for the two old men at the corner table — regulars, their kopi long gone cold in front of them, their whole attention on the over — and against the rest of us. Arjun listens to a passage of it with his head tilted, all the way to the end of the over, and then makes a small disgusted sound. “He’s left it. Look at him, he’s left a full ball outside off, this fellow, he will leave and leave and leave and then one day they will write that he was patient.” He tears his dosa. “Patient. He is not patient. He is frightened. And they will put patient on the stone.”

The man at the griddle says something to the corner table without turning from the iron, and both uncles answer him at once, and the boy laughs. The one word I catch is cannot, and it isn’t even Tamil.

“What happened.”

“He left another one.” Arjun does not look up. “The whole stall is now against him. The man cooking is against him.” He catches my face. “You don’t follow it.”

“I follow that everyone is upset with a man on the radio.”

“That is the whole of it, Daniel. You have understood cricket.” He goes back to the over.

“You sound like you’ve met him.”

“I have met a hundred of him. My uncle is him. My uncle played for the district — district, Daniel, this is a serious thing, you would not understand, it is more serious than the Test side — and he left every ball that frightened him for forty years and they called it technique.” He says it with enormous fondness, the way he says all the things about home. “He kept the scorebook from a match in 1971 in a tin with his medals. Eleven not out. He batted four hours for eleven. Four hours. The other side went home. The umpires went home. And in the tin it says eleven, not out, and beside it, in his own hand, unbeaten.” He shakes his head, half in awe. “The man at my club is the same. He does not run for one ball. Not one. And he wins. You know why he wins?”

“Tell me why he wins.”

“Because the rest of us are running,” he says, delighted with it. “Because he has made a study of the one ball he cannot lose to, which is the ball he does not play.” Arjun points the torn dosa at me like a man making a closing argument. “You would love him, Daniel. He is you. He stands in the middle of the court doing nothing with total conviction and lets the game come apart around him, and at the end he has the most points and the cleanest shirt.”

“I run for balls.”

“You run for the idea of balls. You run for balls that have not been hit yet.” He perks up entirely now. “You are running for Tuesday. You are running for a table at seven o’clock that does not exist.”

He goes back to his dosa, and the over ends, and the uncle at the next table says something that makes both uncles laugh, and Arjun catches a word of it and laughs too, late, on their behalf.

The stall is four steel tables under a tin awning, a man at a flat black griddle the size of a manhole cover, a boy who is probably the man’s nephew running plates. The griddle gives off a smell I could not have named two years ago and could now find blindfolded across a city — the toasted-rice smell of the batter hitting the hot iron, the ghee going at the edges, the curry-leaf and mustard-seed crackle from the pot of sambar kept at a low boil all morning. The tin holds the heat down on us. Arjun sits with his back very straight, which he does everywhere, and eats fast and neatly, and between mouthfuls he rolls his right thumb absently against the side of his first finger — the squash hand. I have stopped pointing it out to him. I just watch the thumb work, and I want it, that thumb, on the inside of my wrist where it goes when he is reading me, and I eat instead. The uncle who runs the stall knows Arjun’s order and has come, over the year, to know mine, which is Arjun’s order, because at some point I stopped ordering and started arriving. “That’s the whole sentence.”

“That’s the whole sentence.”

There were two impressions in it, not one. Past the middle, for two words, the uncle’s voice went flat and quick, and it was Arjun’s deadpan, done well.

The sound itself I kept. All nine seconds of it, whole. I did not decide to.

I tear another piece of dosa. The uncle, refilling a water jug two tables down, is not looking at us, and is, in the specific way of a man not looking at a thing, entirely aware of us, and has been for a year. Two men who arrive together on weekends and order one order and leave a tip calculated by the one who is not from here. He worked us out long ago, and it has not once changed the warmth with which he sets the plates down or the small flourish with which he tops up the chutney we did not ask for. I have stopped, mostly, noticing the noticing.

The trouble, when it comes, comes in the lift.

We take a taxi back to Fortuna Court for the pool, and come up through the lobby, and the lift is on the ground floor with its doors open as though it had been waiting, and Aunty Lim is beside it, back from the market, and the three of us get in. I press the 4; she presses the 2. The 12 is Tal’s floor. On other nights my thumb has found it without instructions, the button lit before the doors finished closing. Today my hand stays at my side, and the 12 stays a plastic circle with nothing to announce. These pages get the button. I have counted the other nights. The pages do not get the count.

Aunty Lim lives on the second floor and has lived on the second floor since before the building was called Fortuna Court, since it was called something else, and she conducts her relationship with the rest of the building from a position of total information and total economy: she knows everything and spends almost none of it. She is carrying a cloth bag of vegetables and a folded Chinese newspaper, a small woman in her seventies whose hands on the bag’s handles have the dry, precise grip of a person who has carried things the same way for longer than I have been alive, and she looks at the two of us, and I begin to produce.

This used to come with a tell. There used to be a thing that happened in my chest before it, a contraction, a squaring, the body reporting the cost before I paid it. The body has stopped doing that. I notice that it has stopped, the way you notice your own hands only when they have gone still. There is no contraction. There is only the smoothness arriving on schedule, the throat finding the right syllable, the breath measured out, and no charge on it anywhere.

“Aunty Lim,” I say. “This is my friend Arjun.”

It costs nothing from the outside: the most ordinary sentence a person could say in a lift. But I say it at the temperature of the Calder Rowe agenda with my list on the back of it, of every room in which I have ever made a true thing presentable: cold enough to keep. I introduce the man whose racquet has stood by my door since March as my friend Arjun, and I do it smoothly, consulting nothing.

Aunty Lim looks at me. Then she looks at Arjun as though the two of them have had an arrangement for some time.

“Your friend,” she says, to me, without inflection, banking the phrase rather than spending it.

You tell him.” She tips her head at me, on him, and I stand in the lift and let her put us there. “He never reads the notice.”

“I’ll tell him,” Arjun says.

She looks at the bag of vegetables and then at Arjun, as if the two were related items. “Last time also you tell him. He still went Tuesday. Stood there.” She does not look at me. “Toweled.”

“I read part of the notice,” I say.

“Part.” She says it to Arjun. She shifts the bag to the other hand, a small redistribution of weight that is also, somehow, a verdict. “The notice is one paragraph. Big letters. They put a picture even. A man, water, a cross over it.” A pause, the kind she leaves before the line she has been saving. “He has a degree, is it.”

“Two,” Arjun says.

“Two.” She lets that sit with the vegetables.

“He never reads the notice,” she says again, to Arjun, settling it between the two of them, two adults discussing the maintenance of a household and the habits of the third person in it, and the lift reaches the second floor, and the doors open, and Aunty Lim gets off with her vegetables, and my friend Arjun and I ride on to the fourth.

In the corridor Arjun is quiet for three steps. Then he says, “Your friend Arjun.”

“Don’t.”

“I’m only repeating it. It’s a good line. I want to learn it.”

“Arjun.”

“My friend Daniel,” he says, trying it in the air, turning it over, finding it wanting. “No. It doesn’t —” He makes a small balancing gesture with one hand. “It doesn’t carry the weight. You have a gift. I would never have thought to call myself that.” He unlocks the door and holds it. “Four words and a name. She filed it before the doors opened.” He is enjoying this far too much. “Twelve years I have lived in this country and no one has ever introduced me so completely. My friend Arjun.” He is smiling when he says it. Under the smile, for a second, something else, there and gone.

The smile is the wide one, the one I did not see until I had known him some months and then could not stop seeing, the one that opens the whole face, and he does not look angry. I am, and not at him. My face is hot the whole length of the corridor and it is not the heat. I introduce him as my friend in a lift and then spend the walk down a corridor being teased about it by the person I did it to, and I would do it again tomorrow, at the same temperature.

The pool is not closed on Sunday. We have it nearly to ourselves.

I have a plan for the pool, too. I have not written this one down, which I tell myself makes it not a plan. The idea is that we swim first, properly, a few lengths, and then we do the part where we stand at the edge and do nothing, and then, with the doing-nothing banked, we have the gallery in the afternoon with the light right. There is an order to it.

So when Arjun puts the towel down and walks straight to the edge, and I get his bare back in the sun for the four steps it takes, and want him, the four steps’ worth, and spend them checking the order instead, and he lowers himself in and stands there, immediately, in the chest-deep water, doing the nothing, the plan stubs its toe.

“We could do a few lengths first,” I say.

“We could.” He does not move toward doing a few lengths.

“While we’re fresh.”

“Fresh for the lengths.” He tips his head back and lets the water take the weight of it. “Or fresh for the standing here. The standing here also rewards being fresh.”

“It’s just that if we do the lengths after, we’ll be hot for the gallery.”

He opens one eye at me. The one eye sees everything; it always has.

“Tell me,” he says, “the order. I want to hear the order.”

“There’s no order.”

“There’s an order. You have done the pool in your head already. You’re at the gallery right now, checking the light. That’s the trouble with you, Daniel. You’ve already had this day. I’m still in it.”

“The show closes today,” I say.

“It is good,” he says, delighted, settling it. “The light in your head is very good. You have lit the gallery.” He pushes off the wall and floats onto his back, arms out, addressing this to the sky. “I am in love with a man who has lit a gallery he is not standing in.”

He says it to the sky.

The short laugh first. And the worst of it is that he is right, exactly and without margin: I have a clear, false memory of a swim I have not had.

“I’ll do the lengths now,” I say, with what I believe is dignity, and push off the wall, and get through perhaps four strokes of a front crawl before I understand I am swimming lengths to prove a point about not having a plan, which is its own kind of plan, and I stop in the middle of the pool, standing, the water at my chest, and look back at him floating there reading the sky, and that is when the wide one comes, the whole face, because he has watched me work it out in real time and waited for me to arrive at it.

“There he is,” Arjun says, to the sky. “He’s found it.”

A family at the shallow end, the parents under the one umbrella, and otherwise the long blue rectangle and the heat coming straight down and the towers of the building across the canal standing in the water upside down — the nearer tower holding its shape, the farther one already coming apart, kept from being a building by the smallest movement of the surface.

Arjun swims a slow front crawl to the far wall and back, and I watch the shoulders do it, the water going off the back of him in a sheet at the turn, and I want him, here, in the chlorine, with the family at the shallow end, and I put the thought back where it came from and take it out again. I go looking, the way I always end up looking with him, for the seam, the place where the ease is being managed, and I cannot find it. It is a face that stays itself when it is looked at. I spend my days with faces that arrange themselves toward whoever is watching, and his does not arrange. He stops and stands with his forearms on the tiled edge and his back to the sun, and I come and stand next to him, our forearms on the edge. The family at the shallow end packs up and leaves — the father carrying the smaller child upside down and shrieking under one arm, the wet footprints going out the gate and drying behind them before they reach it — and then it is the two of us. The afternoon does not pass so much as deepen, the light getting heavier rather than lower.

Somewhere in there I notice that my breathing and his breathing have arrived at the same rate, the rise and the settle of two ribcages against the same tiled edge, and I do not know which of us matched the other and I do not test it by changing mine. He turns his head and rests his cheek on his own forearm, facing me, eyes shut against the sun, and says nothing, and the wet at his hairline catches the light, and a drop runs down past his ear and off his jaw and back into the pool without his moving to stop it. We breathe at one rate and watch the broken towers stand on their heads. It is the most married I have ever felt.

His phone goes, on the towel, at the pool’s edge.

He climbs out. I watch him do it, the water coming off him, down the groove of his back, the shorts gone heavy and low on his hips, and I look, because there is no one left at the pool to see me look. He stands dripping on the tile and reads the screen and then takes the call, two fingers pressed to his free ear, and speaks for the length of a short call, and every second of it is in the other Arjun, the office one, the voice with the corners squared, and I catch some of it. A name, a number, the word Tuesday, the word London said once, flatly. Then he says a thing that makes whoever it is laugh across the connection, and he hangs up and comes back to the edge and lowers himself into the water and his breathing takes a few seconds to find the rate again.

“Work,” he says. “On a Sunday.”

“What did they want.”

“Nothing. They want me to know they’re thinking of me. It’s a whole register, Daniel. Being thought of. They make you feel it’s a gift.” He wipes water from his eyes with the heel of his hand. “A man rings on a Sunday to tell you that you were in a room you were not in, that your name came up and was set down again gently. And you are meant to spend the rest of the day a little warmer for it. Which I will. That’s the annoying part, I will.”

“So what did he give you,” I say.

“Nothing. He gave me nothing, Daniel. There was no work in it, no decision, not even a question I could go away and answer. Ten minutes of a partner’s Sunday, and partners cost, they cost by the quarter-hour even when they are being warm. All that money, spent, to hand a man nothing.”

“Is it.”

“It’s a gift the way a list is a list.” He bumps his shoulder against mine, once, under the surface, where it doesn’t count, where no one can see it. “Don’t worry about London. It’s just where the men on the phone like to imagine themselves.”

“You said London like it was a meeting.”

“It is a meeting. There’s a panel. They fly you somewhere, they look at you, they decide whether to look at you more.” A cloud crosses the sun; the water cools half a degree against my arms, and I let the word panel sink down into it. He floats it off the way he floats everything, on its back, arms out. “It’s nothing, Daniel. It’s a thing firms do. You make a man feel chosen once, when he is thirty and hungry, and after that he does the rest himself. He keeps himself worth choosing. You never have to do anything again, maybe ring him on a Sunday. I’ve watched it done to better men than me.”

And he ducks under the surface and comes up with his hair flat. I go back to watching the towers in the water.

We come in from the pool in the early afternoon and the apartment is cool and dim with the blinds half down against the west light, the aircon cold on the chlorine still drying on my arms, and for a while nothing happens. He showers first and comes out in a pair of my drawstring shorts that have become, without discussion, his, and that sit lower on him than they ever did on me, and lies down on the sofa with one of the books that have started living on my shelves at the end nearest the door — paperbacks with cracked spines, a history of the partition, a novel in a language I cannot read that he is rereading for the third time — and he reads with the stillness of a man who can disappear into a page. I make coffee for myself, two scoops, the machine he tolerates and rarely uses, and I bring the weekend paper to the armchair, and I read the business pages and then the obituaries, which I read everywhere I live as a way of learning what a place considers a life, and the aircon runs its even hum and a delivery bike whines past on the road below and somewhere down the corridor a child is being told, at length and without effect, to put on shoes.

He reads with his knees up and the book against them, and twice he laughs at something on the page, the short laugh, to himself, not for me — and the second time, he reads me the line without lifting his eyes from the page — low and unhurried, the words handed over rather than performed, his voice dropping the half-step it only drops for me. It is a sentence about a railway minister in 1955 and a bridge that was opened a year before it was finished. “They cut the ribbon,” he says, “on a bridge you could not yet cross. And everyone clapped. And then they all went home the long way.” He lets it sit. He looks up to see if it landed, and finding that it did, lets me have the small unguarded smile he gives no one in an office.

A few pages on he goes still in a different way, the way he goes still over the milk. “Here,” he says. “Listen. The minister built a guest house at the bridge for the dignitaries to stay in for the opening. Forty rooms. And then the bridge was late, so the opening was late, so the guest house sat empty two years with a cook kept on, full salary, in case the dignitaries came early.” He shakes his head, delighted and scandalized in equal measure. “Two years a man cooked, every day, for guests who were not coming, so that the day they came it would look as though he had always been cooking.” He looks at the blinds a moment, and I would bet the flat he is watching the cook lay dinner for nobody. “You should put the cook in one of your decks.” Then he goes back into the page and is gone again, and I do not say anything, because the thing did not want a reply, only a witness, and I am the witness, in the chair, with the coffee going to the temperature of the room.

Earlier, still reading, he had reached a foot across the small distance between the sofa and the chair and set it against my ankle, the cool sole of his foot on the side of my foot, and gone on reading. He turns a page. I turn a page. The foot stays. Twenty minutes go by like that, two men reading in separate languages with one point of contact at the ankle, and for those twenty minutes I do not want anything I do not already have, and nothing in me is grading anything against the sole of his foot.

Later, when he comes up out of the book, I tell him a deck is the wrong word for what had been on my desk all week. I had a monk. A real one, a Buddhist monk with a condition in his hip that the device people wanted as their case, and so we had built him a courtyard that photographed better than the one he prayed in, and lit it, and staged a doctor’s appointment he had already had, twice, so a camera could have it a third time. I tell him about the consent forms the monk signed with great patience, and the day we lost to the legal team over whether the man could say, on camera, the actual name of the thing in his spine.

Arjun puts the book face-down on his chest to give it his whole attention. “They made a monk audition his own pain,” he says, pleased and appalled in the exact proportions he keeps for things from my world. “For a word he was not allowed to say.” He thinks about it. “Your job is worse than mine, Daniel. I only move the cumin.”

He does not pick the book back up. He comes across the small distance between the sofa and the chair and takes the coffee out of my hand and puts it on the floor and gets me up out of the chair by the wrist, not gently, the way you take a plate to the table, no announcement, because that is where plates go. “The page can wait,” he says, and that is all the explanation I get, and I don’t need one, because I am already standing and already hard and already, God help me, a little embarrassed about how fast, in the middle of a Sunday afternoon with the blinds half down and a child down the corridor still being told about shoes. He wants me. Not the map, not the grip, not the side the grinder goes on. Me, plainly, now, and I am the man who sees everything coming, and I did not see this. The blinds put their stripes on us and move them. Nobody is the dry one; the shorts that are mine and are his come down under a hand that does not ask, and at some point I hear myself, a sound I had not planned and could not have cleared with anyone, and I do not care, and I make it again, louder, on purpose, to see what he does, and what he does is not stop. Nothing from that hour got filed. I have looked. There is no tell to find, no shift in the breath. He wanted me, and it was the best hour of that year, and I spent the next one rearranging the cushions, and if you had asked me that evening what happened on Sunday I would have said we did the market.

We do go to the gallery, in the middle of the afternoon, because it is the one on the list with a clock on it. Gillman Barracks is the old army camp, low colonial blocks the color of weak tea set among rain trees with their canopies grown into one another, the whole place quiet. We walk in from the road under the trees, and the heat sits in the shade differently than it sits in the sun, heavier and stiller. A security guard in a plastic chair nods us through without getting up.

The closing show is in one of the back blocks: large photographs, a Malaysian artist, palm plantations shot so close and so flat that the rows of trees stop being trees and become a pattern, a green going to black at the edges of the frame. The room is cold with the gallery aircon turned down to the temperature art is kept at, and we are the only two in the room but for a girl at a desk by the door. Arjun goes slowly. He stands a long time in front of one print, hands clasped behind him, and I stand a little behind him and watch him look, which I would rather do than look at the photograph: the back of his neck where the collar sits, the two tendons, the place I had my mouth an hour ago and would put it again now, in the cold, with the girl at the desk six feet off, and he is looking at a palm plantation. After a while he says, not turning, “My grandfather managed an estate like this. Not this one. One like it.” He does not say more, and I do not ask, and we move to the next print, and the fact of the grandfather hangs in the cold room with the green going to black, given to me and not explained, and I carry it out into the heat with the rest of the things he has handed me sideways today.

In the gift area by the exit there is a rack of postcards of the show, and I watch him almost buy one and then not — pick it up, turn it over, read the price on the back, set it down. “That much,” he says, to the rack, “for a photograph of a photograph.” Outside, the four-o’clock gold has come in low across the lawns. The rain trees fold their leaves. We walk back through it without talking, and it is exactly what I had wanted, exactly what I had lit a gallery for in my head at the pool, and I have it, and Arjun is in it, and neither of us says a word about the light.

On the way back there is a provision shop he likes, a narrow one off the main road with the goods stacked to the ceiling and a fan turning over the till, and he stops without asking whether I want to stop, because he needs curry leaf and the small green chilies and one particular tin he never has to read the label of — a sharp yellow powder whose name I keep failing to retain — that the bigger places stock and then stop stocking the moment he relies on them. The shop smells of dried fish and jute and something at the back gone past ripe, and the fan only moves the smell around, and I stand in the one aisle with my hands in my pockets, because there is nowhere in it to put a man my size. The man knows him. Of course the man knows him. He weighs the chilies by feel, pours them off the pan into a thin pink bag, twists it, and says a number. Arjun says a smaller number. The man says the first number again, gently, and Arjun puts two of the chilies back as a matter of principle, and they settle a cent below where they started, both satisfied.

“You talked him down one cent,” I say.

“I talked him down nothing. I gave him back two chilies he did not want returned and we both got to feel like men.” He lifts the tin of yellow powder, checks the seal with his thumb, sets it in the basket. “My father did this every Sunday of my childhood. The same shop, the same man, the same forty paise that changed nothing. I thought it was about the forty paise. I was twelve before I understood it was the only conversation the two of them had ever had, and they had been having it, happily, for thirty years.” He hands the basket to the man.

“You don’t need all that,” I say, of the curry leaf, which is a whole branch, far more than a man cooks with in a week.

“You always need all that. You take the leaves off the stem and you keep them in the cold and they last. The stem you throw.” He hands me the bag to hold, the bag passed to me without a word, my hands enlisted. “My grandmother kept a plant. On the back step. You took what you needed in the morning and the plant did not mind.” He pays, and pockets the coins the man gives back without counting them, and we go out into the heat with the curry leaf giving off its sharp green smell from the bag in my hand, and the smell is on my fingers for the rest of the night. I keep finding it there.

The fight is about the table.

It starts at five, in the apartment, the two of us showered and the aircon on and the light going amber on the canal, when I say we should think about leaving by six-thirty, and Arjun, on the sofa with the squash racquet across his knees, rewinding the grip tape, says, “Leaving for what.”

“The table. Seven.”

“I thought the table was an option.”

“It was on the list.”

“Everything circled is an option,” he says. “You told me that. In the kitchen. The pen rested itself.”

“I’d like to go to the dinner.”

“Then call them and move it to a Saturday. I want —” He sets the racquet down, flat, on the cushion, which is a thing he does when he is about to be more direct than the racquet permits. “I’ve had a good day. I had the dosa and I had the pool and I got teased in a lift. I would rather not put on a collared shirt and sit in a restaurant being served by a person who has to perform that he’s glad to see us. I would like the day to stay the shape it is.”

“It’s a forty-minute dinner.”

“It’s not the dinner. It’s the getting ready for the dinner. It’s the —” He moves a hand. “It’s that the day has been a day, and you want to put a frame around it before it’s over, no? And I want to find out what the day does on its own.”

“So I’ll cancel it.”

“Cancel it, then.”

“I can’t cancel it at five-thirty. It’s a twelve-table place. If I cancel now they can’t fill it.”

He looks at me.

“They can’t fill it,” he says.

“It’s a small restaurant. They hold a table from the time you book it. If I call at five-thirty and say I’m not coming, that table sits empty at seven, and they’ve turned away —”

“Daniel. You are now defending the restaurant.”

“I’m not defending the restaurant. I’m describing how a reservation works.”

“You’re describing how a reservation works to me.” He is not raising his voice. “We are sitting in your apartment, on a Sunday, and we are having an argument about the moral standing of a restaurant’s empty table. Do you hear it? Do you hear what we’re —”

“I hear it.”

“Then why are we —”

“Because I made a plan,” I say, “and you’ve spent the last hour treating the plan like a symptom, and it’s a table, Arjun, it’s a table at a restaurant, people book them, it is the most ordinary thing a person can —”

“It is the most ordinary thing a person can do,” he says. “Yes. I agree.” And then the edge comes up under the flat, for the first time all afternoon. “You don’t even like these places. You like that you can afford them. Except you can’t, quite, and I am the only one at this table who can do the arithmetic.”

Under the table my stomach says something low, and long, the kind of sound you cannot pass off as the furniture. Kaya toast at seven, one coffee, a dosa at noon I ate too fast to count, and nothing since; the arithmetic he means has been done on the stomach too, since Thursday, and the stomach has just said so, out loud, in the middle of his sentence, and neither of us laughs.

I go looking for what is under it — contempt, or worry, or some piece of my own week he has reached across and picked up — and I find the words and nothing beneath them.

“So why does it feel,” he says, “in this room, right now, like it is not about the table.”

And there it is, set down on the cushion next to the racquet, the sentence neither of us is going to pick up, and we both look at it, and then we both look away from it, because picking it up would require a vocabulary I do not own.

We do not talk for a while. He goes back to the grip tape. I sit in the chair by the window and look at the canal, and the aircon runs, and the apartment sits at the one temperature it has ever had, and the silence is the bad kind, the kind with a meter running. Somewhere in it a sentence assembles itself, whole, the way they do when I am paid to make them. It is about the arithmetic. It is accurate, which is the reason it would work, and it would take four seconds to say, and I know what it would do to him at the exact resolution I know what a headline does. I keep it where I can reach it, priced, and I do not spend it. The silence goes on long enough that the light changes.

I take my phone out to the corridor.

The corridor smells of someone’s dinner from the far end and laundry from the near end, and is warm after the apartment, and the phone is slick in my hand before anyone picks up. I call the restaurant. A young man answers, and I say the name the booking is under and that something has come up and we won’t make it tonight, and I brace for I don’t know what; for the table, for the cost of the table, and the young man says, no problem sir, thank you for letting us know, we hope to see you another time, warmly, with no weight on it at all, and hangs up, and I stand in the corridor with the phone in my hand having been forgiven, instantly and completely, for a thing the man on the other end did not even register as a thing. Someone at the far end of the corridor is frying garlic. I stand there and breathe it in as if that counted as dinner.

“We had the dosa.”

“We could have it again. The uncle will think we have a condition.”

“We do have a condition.”

She does not get up. She looks at the two of us going down past her grille with the look she gives everything, and then she says, to Arjun, through the bars, “You ate already.” Not a question. He stops. “We’re going for dosa,” he says. “The place in Holland Village, the one with the good sambar. We go most weeks now.” “Again,” she says, as if the frequency were the point. He is too thin.” The he is me; she does not look at me when she says it; she has decided my nutrition is a matter she will conduct through Arjun, the way she conducts the pool notices. “Last week the dustbin outside his door. Three days. Same bag.” She says it to Arjun, delivering a household report to the one party in it who will act on it. “Inside, what. One box. Cereal.” The word arrives with quiet contempt. “A grown man, cereal for dinner. Aiyoh.” A small shake of the head. “Bring back, don’t bring back, but he should eat.” And she goes back to her paper, the audience over, and we go on down, and Arjun says nothing until the landing, where he says, quietly, “She likes you,” and I say, “She has never once said my name,” and he says, “That is how you know.”

We ride over for it after dark and walk the last stretch from the taxi. The day’s heat has come off the pavement and what is left is the night air, the drains breathing their faint green smell, the frangipani in the planters along the road letting go of the day’s perfume. The shophouses have their grilles half drawn; a man is hosing the apron in front of a closed bakery; a cat the color of dust crosses ahead of us, unhurried. Arjun walks with his hands in his pockets and his head a little back, looking at the upper stories as he always does, the old plasterwork and the bottle-green louvers and the one window with a birdcage hung outside it, and he says, of no building in particular, “I would have liked to live up there. When I first came. Before I could afford to want things sensibly.” He says it without weight, an offcut of a man. “Up there,” he says again, of the window with the birdcage, “with the bird and the window propped open on a chopstick. I had a room like it when I came. One room, off Serangoon, that gave onto an airwell, and the airwell carried everything — the couple on three who fought about money every Sunday and made up loudly every Sunday night, a boy somewhere practicing the same four bars of piano for a year and never the fifth, a grandmother who shouted the wrong answers at her game shows, somebody’s rooster that nobody on the block would admit to owning.” He is smiling at the upper story, not at me. “I hated it. I tell people now I loved it. In ten more years I will believe I loved it.” He puts his hands back in his pockets. “Even the rooster is halfway to a fond memory, and I hated that rooster personally.” And he walks on, and I take the line and put it away and do not turn to look at him while I do.

We take the dosa at the wrong hour, half past eight, the stall nearly empty, the griddle scraped down and cooling at one end while the man keeps the other end going for the stragglers, and the uncle does look at us, and does say something Arjun lets go by untranslated, and we eat with the right hand, both of us competent now, the second dosa of the day, thinner and more brittle than the morning’s because the batter has been sitting, the edges shattering like a wafer when I tear them, and I eat it fast and with my head down, and I do not stop to be embarrassed about the speed until it is gone and Arjun is looking at me. Somewhere across the city a table for two goes to seven o’clock and then past it with no one in it, a small empty fact in a room I will never see, and is, by the kindness of a young man on a telephone, no one’s fault. The dosa comes to eleven dollars for the two of us. I pay for it because Arjun paid for the morning’s, and I do the small arithmetic without writing it down, and for once it comes out in my favor.

He stays late. When we come up from the dosa he does not pick up his bag, and that is its own small message, the bag left where it is, and I read it and say nothing.

In the dark the aircon is off for him and the racquet keeps its corner and I reach for him, the ordinary reach. He takes my hand where it lands and holds it, flat, against his chest, which is not a no and is not a yes, and for a moment I think it is the prelude to the yes. Then he says, quietly, into the dark, “Not tonight.”

That is all. He does not give a reason. Arjun keeps reasons the way he keeps the other things, in the room he keeps even here, even with me, the door shut without sound. He is not turning away. His hand stays on mine on his chest, and his breathing does not change, and the refusal is not a wall so much as a held line, a thing he has decided in some room I am not in and brought out whole.

I wait for the part of me that should mind. A year ago it would have minded. There would have been a reading run on the held hand, the flat voice, the not tonight — the cost calculated, the body reporting in. The body reports nothing. I lie there with my hand under his and find that I feel less than I expect to feel, and that the not-minding is not peace. It is just quiet, the same quiet the chest went into in the lift. I say, “Okay.” He squeezes my hand once and lets it go. We lie there. The line holds and I do not test it. After a while he sleeps, and I stay awake a while in the dark on my own side of a bed we are both in, keeping the line for him because he asked, and feeling, about the keeping, almost nothing at all.

At some point he woke. He went home, in the end, the way he went home most Sunday nights. His life was kept in Holland Village — the suits in the wardrobe there, the Monday that started there — and a man could not run a Monday from a borrowed key and a racquet left in someone else’s entryway, and so on Sunday nights, late, he gathered the bag he had brought and not the racquet, never the racquet, and stood at my door, said something ordinary, and went.

That night he stood at the door and looked at the racquet in its corner. Head down, the grip tape freshly wound, ready for a Sunday I could already feel arriving behind this one, the whole rest of them lined up like that, identical, a year of them, more. He said, “Tuesday the pool’s closed. They put the thing in the water.”

“Aunty Lim told me.”

“Aunty Lim told me,” he said. “I’m telling you. There’s a chain of command.” And he laughed the short one, and he left, and I heard the lift come and take him, and the apartment was one man’s again. The aircon ran its even hum. The racquet kept its corner. I stood in the kitchen in the shorts he had worn that afternoon, which were mine again and smelled of him anyway.

I poured a second glass of wine and drank half of it at the counter, and looked at the other half, and wanted it, plainly, the way I had wanted the arm back that first morning, and poured it out. That was the night I made the rule, though it did not need stating for a while: one glass on the nights he goes, because the glass on those nights wants to be two, and the second wants to become a sentence said down a phone, and the morning has to pay for that.

She was right that I never read the notice. There would be a great many more Sundays. I knew that, in the bed afterward — knew it the way you know the aircon will still be running when you wake. What I did not know, and could not have been made to believe by anyone, including Arjun, including the uncle, including Aunty Lim with all her information, was that they were a thing with a number on them. I thought they were weather. I thought they were the climate I had moved to.

The Onion

When the production flew me back to New York for a day, it was to stand on my old block in Brooklyn. Nothing about that day took me up to Ridgewood. The block they wanted was the one with a story on it, and I gave them the block. The apartment was up there all the same: a third-floor walk-up over a key-cutter’s, four rooms strung in a line the way the old railroad flats ran, each one opening into the next, so that to reach the back bedroom you went through the kitchen and the middle room and whatever was happening in them. There was a bathtub in the kitchen with a hinged enamel lid that became counter space when the lid was down. The radiator knocked on a schedule the super could not account for and would not fix. The bedroom window faced the airshaft and did not close all the way; in winter a rolled towel went into the gap and grayed there until spring, and in summer the window stayed open because it could not do otherwise, and the airshaft carried up the building’s whole unguarded life, a downstairs radio and someone’s frying and a dog and the sound of people who were not arranging themselves for anyone. On the fire escape there was a milk crate turned over for a seat and a coffee can for the ash. The man whose apartment it was had lived there eight years and hung nothing on the walls, on the standing theory that he was about to leave, which he never was.

Nothing in those rooms had ever been set down with a camera in mind, and no camera had ever wanted them. They were not a place a production company would fly to. The building I lived in by then, in Singapore, was exactly that: the air held at one even temperature, the pool kept the blue of a pool in a brochure, the elevator that hummed, the whole bright machine the show had already come for. Ridgewood had none of that.

I am not going to write his name. I did not write it on the address line of a postcard either, with the pen already down.

I called it staying. The lease in Brooklyn stayed in my name and my roommate Reuben’s, my mail went there, my winter coat hung in its closet, and for a year I slept in Ridgewood six nights in seven and carried my clothes north one bag at a time, the way you carry a thing you are not going to admit to in one trip. Reuben watched the bags go and said nothing, which is a thing Reuben can do for a year. Staying. I was thirty and I had a word for it that a man uses for a hotel.

The hours I want back are the ones on the fire escape. I would come up the three flights with the day still on me, a whole afternoon of sentences sent up for approval and sent back down with a comment in the margin, and the key-cutter’s grinder would have stopped at six, and the building would be doing what it did at seven, which was nothing in particular, loudly. He would already be out there on the milk crate. I sat on the steps beside it, one rung down, because there was one crate and he had had it eight years, with a beer sweating through my fingers and my tie off and my shoes off and my socks off, and we looked into the shaft at the back of somebody’s kitchen and said very little for an hour. That is not a figure of speech. I have had an hour timed by a boom operator and I know how long one is. Nobody came to the window. Nobody wanted the take again. I would go in and start dinner when the shaft got dark enough that I could not see my own feet, and I looked forward to that hour across whole meetings, across whole clients, and I never told anyone at the office where I was going at five-thirty, because there was no cleared version of it, and I could not have gotten one past myself.

That October, then. I was at the tub with the lid down, cutting an onion on it, because the lid was the only surface in that kitchen that held still, and he was out on the fire escape on the milk crate with the paper and the coffee can, and the window between us was open the eight inches it was ever going to open. It was warm for October. I had my shirt off, which I would not have done in any kitchen I paid for, and the onion was in my eyes, and up the shaft came the whole building: the radio on two with somebody’s team losing on it, a pan going somewhere with garlic in it, the dog on four that barked at pigeons and not at people. Nobody in that shaft knew there was a man at a bathtub lid with an onion, and nobody would have looked if they had. For whole evenings in those rooms nobody was reading anything. Nobody was going to send the onion back with notes. My whole trade went quiet in there the way a fridge goes quiet, and I only heard that it had when I went back down to Brooklyn for a shirt and heard it start up again in the hallway. I liked it the way you like a room where you can eat with your hands, and I did, standing, off the tub lid, more often that year than I have eaten sitting down since.

He said my name a lot. The whole of it, every time, as if checking it was still the one. From the fire escape, from the bed, from the shower with the curtain half across: Daniel, is there beer. Daniel, come look at this. He is the only person I have known who used it as punctuation. That night he said it through the window with the paper still up in front of his face, and then he called me the other thing, the short one, the one people use across dinner tables without lowering their voices, in the register he used to say the coffee was on. He did not put the paper down for it. That was the thing about him, and it is the thing I have never gotten level with: he could set it out on the sill like a cup and go on reading.

Mine went up. I felt it go, from under the ribs up to the hinge of the jaw, and it stopped there with the onion in my eyes and my hands wet, and what came out of my mouth instead was that the onion was strong. He laughed. It was funny. I had made the joke, and the joke was the near-one, and he took it, and that was the first time the word did not come. I filed it, standing there, as a thing that would come next time. You know already that it never did; I told you at the start. This was the first time, and it was over an onion, and he laughed.

We went to bed through the middle room, past the one lamp and the chair with his jeans on it. The mattress gave toward his side, eight years of him in it, so that whichever side you started on you were rolled against him by one in the morning, and after the first week I stopped fighting it. He was heavier than me and warmer, hair on his forearms and his chest and not much anywhere else, and he slept with one foot out from under the sheet on the window side no matter the month. I wanted him that night and I had him, and that part was never the trouble. I could want a man in Ridgewood with the window open eight inches and the whole shaft listening, and I did, on my back and then not on my back, with his hand over my mouth at one point because the radio on two had gone off and the quiet was worse, both of us laughing into the hand. After, he wiped his palm on his own thigh and not on mine, which I noticed every time and never said, and put the foot back out from under the sheet, and was asleep inside two minutes, the way the rest of the building was asleep, without arranging it. I lay awake under the window that would not close. The shaft breathed on us all night, a cool wet breath with somebody’s cigarette in it, and I lay in it with my mouth still full of the word, and it was the happiest I have been in a bed, and I knew it while it was happening.

In November the towel went into the gap and grayed there. The radiator knocked and I learned its schedule, which the super never did. I wrote none of that year down. I carried each piece of it at cost, and I did not know yet that one day I would have to write it down.

Then July. The heat in a railroad flat in July has nowhere to go but through you, and the window that would not close was the only thing in the apartment on my side. Eric had called in the spring, and his word for what I did was compliance. Reuben had known since March. There was a number in an email from a city I could not have placed on a map the year before, and I had read the number enough times to have stopped seeing it, and nothing was signed, and I had not said the name of that city inside those four rooms, not once, not to the man who lived in them, who was asleep two rooms back with his foot out of the sheet.

We had eaten on the fire escape that night, off one plate, because the plate was the thing that had to be washed and the fire escape was the thing that had to be sat on: cold chicken, tomatoes with salt, a beer each and then the second beer split, his mouth on the bottle and then mine, the shaft giving up nothing cooler than what we had. He had the paper. He read me a thing about the Mets that he thought was funny and it was, and I laughed, and the laugh went down into the shaft and somebody on two laughed too, at something else. He went in at eleven and did what he always did, which was be asleep by the time I had rinsed the plate. I stood in the kitchen in my T-shirt, and it was stuck to me between the shoulder blades in the shape of a hand, and I peeled it off and hung it on the tub faucet and stood there bare to the waist at one in the morning with the whole building asleep around me and the one man in it who knew my name asleep too. Nobody was looking. I had a year of that, and I had learned to do things in it.

I put the tub lid down. I put the laptop on it, and the enamel was the one cool thing in the kitchen and I set my forearms on it and let them cool. It was after one. The shaft was carrying up somebody’s fan and somebody’s argument and the dog, who had opinions about the heat. I had found the casting team’s address that afternoon, on the third page of a site I had been on for a reason I would have described, if anyone had asked, as idle. Nobody asked. I opened a message to it and I wrote about a man.

I wrote him in the third person and it was the easiest paragraph I have ever written, and I have written for lawyers. He was thirty-one. He worked in compliance and was comfortable in the gray. He was about to take a big chance; he was about to accept a transfer to his company’s Singapore office. He was single. He lived in Brooklyn with a roommate. Every sentence was true and every sentence had been arranged so that the true thing was not in it. The man on the page had no bathtub in his kitchen. He had no window that would not close. He was not bare to the waist at a tub lid at one in the morning with his forearms going warm on the enamel, and nobody in his paragraph was asleep in a back room with one foot out of the sheet, and he did not have a word stuck behind his teeth since October. He was single. I had written single and looked at it and left it. I gave him the Brooklyn address, where the mail went. I did not describe the man in the back room, because the man on the page did not know him, and for the length of a paragraph I was the man on the page, and it was the most comfortable I had been all July.

I read it back once and did not need to read it twice, which should have told me something. It told me it was good. I sent it at one-forty in the morning to people I had never met, and the laptop made the small sound it makes for sending, and the sound went up the shaft with the rest of the building’s noise.

What I did not do was go back through the middle room, past the lamp, and wake him, and say the name of the city. I had had a year to do it and I did not do it that night either. It was not that I could not find the words. I had just found them. I had found them for strangers and they had come out perfect, a man about to take a big chance, and a man who could write that to a casting department at one in the morning could have said it to a man forty feet away, through two open doorways, in his own kitchen. He could have. He sent it to the strangers instead, because the strangers could not say Daniel back.

I closed the laptop and went back through the two rooms. He turned when the mattress went, and his hand came out and found my hip, and he said my name with the question in it that he only put on it when he was mostly asleep, and I said, it’s me, and the mattress rolled me in against him. The window was open on the shaft. The email was gone. I did not sleep and he did.

I never told him. I mean that exactly. A bag went south in August and then another, and the reply came from the casting people, and I felt picked. He watched the bags go and hung nothing on the walls, and near the end, with the paper up, he said he supposed I was about to leave, and I said something about the lease. Eight years he had kept those walls bare so as to be ready to go. I was in and out of those rooms in twelve months and never hung anything either, and I was the one who went.

The postcard is in this account already, the one with no name on the address line. He wrote back and asked for a real one. He was asking for the paragraph I had already written, in the third person, to somebody else.
