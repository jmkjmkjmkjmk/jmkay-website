The Approved Version

a novel · Jonathan Michael Kay

Thank you for reading some of this. The book is a novel called The Approved Version.

It follows Daniel Reiss, an American who moves to Singapore in 2012 for a two-year job and stays three. His work is making things safe to say: he sits in the room where the real thing gets said, then hands back the version that can go out. While he is there, a television crew films his life, and the version they air is missing someone. The book is about the years that version covers, and the person it leaves out.

What you have here is the prologue and two chapters from Daniel’s first years in Singapore, about 2 hours of reading in all. The chapters run in the book’s order, and where some are skipped a short note says what the gap holds.

Read it however you like, in one sitting or five — the page remembers where you stopped. If you want to tell me something along the way, each chapter has a small row at the end for it, and there is a box at the very end for anything bigger. You can also just text me.

— Jonathan

Prologue

Doha, 2019

My life had a man in it. His name was Arjun. I never called him my boyfriend, not then, not to anyone, and I am not going to start on the first page; but that is what he was, and we both knew it, and it was thoughtful and loving and ended too early, when his firm sent him to London. That is how I have put it for years, when anyone asked.

I lived in Singapore for three years. Partway through them a television crew flew in to film my life. I had asked them to.

They kept my apartment and the morning light through the oversized windows, the pool at my complex, the shophouses in the older neighborhoods, the hawker centers I ate at.

They took the man out and filmed me and the apartments, day after day.

He was shot for one scene, a Saturday morning at a café table, as a friend who knew the city well, and cut from it.

That version has been playing ever since, somewhere, on repeat. It is a story about a move and a big decision. The decision on the screen is which of three apartments, and it is reconciled before the half hour is up.

The premise was simple: a man, newly arrived from New York, choosing between three apartments as he looks to establish himself in a new country. On the screen he is newly arrived. By then I had lived in the flat at Fortuna Court for nearly two years, and had already learned everything the show was about to teach him.

The apartment he chooses at the end is the one I already lived in. The morning the crew arrived, a moving truck came for my furniture. Sometimes now I remember the light in that flat the way the camera had it, and have to put the furniture back by hand. I assume there are mornings I forget to.

What the crew did I recognized, because it is my job. For more than a decade I have sat in rooms while people said things, and handed back the version the room could use: the one a regulator would read and find nothing in, that a camera could air. I found it for hospitals, for a drug company’s lawyers, for my own mother. The crew did it to my flat in a week, and did it well.

I told you what Arjun was to me in the first lines of this account, and refused the word in the act of writing it down. There is a word. It is short, and people use it across dinner tables without lowering their voices, and I have written longer and more dangerous words into documents that lawyers signed. This one goes the way a foot goes off a curb you did not see in the dark. I could never set it down next to his name.

I am writing this at a desk in Doha, in a job I said yes to almost before it was offered. Singapore kept a law about men like us in a drawer it pretended nobody ever opened; this country keeps one of its own. I looked it up before I signed, because looking it up is the job. What I am writing is evidence. I write it anyway, at night, with the door locked and my shirt stuck to my back. When I stop for the night the pages go into the case with the client work, under the printed decks, where a man who found them would have to read a while before he understood what he was holding. I started doing that on the second night, before I had decided to.

The law is a true explanation. It is not a whole one. Neither is London. Finding the word a room can use is my one skill. I could not find it for him.

He waited a while for me to. The waiting was quiet; it was a pause he left after other people’s sentences about us, long enough for me to step into, and I never stepped. Then he stopped waiting, and went, on a departure that had a date on it long before either of us would say there was one. For years I called that London.

Two things in this room came out of Singapore sealed. One is nine seconds of an old man talking at the dosa stall Arjun took me to. His head was tipped my way, and he was speaking Tamil, which I can hear and cannot enter. No phone holds them; I do. They are warm and shut, a kitchen door with the family on the other side of it. I have kept them whole these five years and never translated them, though the phone on this desk holds the thing that would, and all it would cost is saying them aloud once. Everyone here speaks my language. Nine seconds of a man I cannot understand is the nearest I come to being spoken to. The other is on that phone: one sentence Arjun sent me in Tamil, and I have never asked the phone what it says.

And this, the pages under my hands, is what I open instead. I have written about a man once before: myself, in the third person, in an email to a casting department. That was the first version. The broadcast was the second. This is the one I could never produce.

Five

Bugis

I meet Arjun because we reach for the same mochi. In anything I produced for pay I would have cut that for being too neat. It is what happened.

This is at a convenience store in Tanjong Pagar, the kind that stays open past midnight in the lobby of an office tower. I have been at a work dinner three streets over that became drinks, and I have left early because I can feel the evening becoming a night, and I have had enough of the wine to know what I would do with a night, and I would rather not find out which version of me does it. The store is on the way to the train. I go in for water and for something sweet, and the something sweet is a mochi — three to a package, soft rice-flour shell, red bean filling, cold from the case.

His hand arrives and stops, because my hand is already there, and for a moment we are both reaching and neither of us is picking it up. He is wearing a collared shirt with the sleeves turned back twice, not once, the second fold a half-centimeter under the first, which by my second month I had stopped reading as carelessness.

I know the shirt before I place the man. I have seen him before. Three times, now that he is close enough that I have to admit it: a party in a crowded flat in Tiong Bahru, a birthday at a bar off Club Street, Sonja’s Sunday brunch where I stayed past the point of having any reason to. Each time the same sleeves, the same listening that keeps the other person’s pace. I could have asked someone his name at any of them; the rooms were small and the city is smaller. I did not ask, because a name is a thing you have to do something with. Walking home from each one I told myself it was the heat — the pulse going in both wrists, the shirt stuck between the shoulder blades by the time I reached the lift, and in the lift, once, the beginning of being hard, which I put down to the humidity, which is not a thing humidity does. Singapore gives a body a hundred reasons. None of them was the reason. Now he is an arm’s length away under the white light, the dark hair on his forearm, the stilled weight of his hand over the same cold package as mine, and the wanting comes back.

“Go ahead,” he says.

“There’s another one behind it.”

He looks, and there is, and he takes the one behind it, and we stand in the refrigerator aisle holding identical packages while the condensation beads on the wrappers and runs down our fingers.

“You’re going to lose that race,” he says, nodding at the package in my hand. “It sweats faster than you can walk home.”

“I’m three blocks.”

“Then you’ll lose by a little.” He looks at me. The face open and still, the size of the smile not yet decided on. “You’re not from here,” he says. Not a question.

“No.”

“I could tell. Not the accent.” He lets that sit, deciding something, and I watch him decide it. “Neither am I. Originally.” He smiles. “Let me buy you a coffee sometime. Before the mochi situation repeats itself.”

This is how, on a Wednesday in October, in a convenience store in Tanjong Pagar, I am asked out by a man who reached for the same mochi. In this country, asking another man for coffee in a tone we both understand is, on the most technical reading of the law, a step toward a crime the law still keeps a number for.

He does not seem concerned, and I am not, in the moment, concerned either. What I am is warm at the back of the neck in a refrigerated aisle. The mochi is already sweating through the wrapper.

Arjun. He gives me the name on the way out of the store, in the doorway, the way you hand someone a thing they will need later — Arjun, and then, because I have given him nothing back, he waits, and I give him mine, and the trade is the first thing we do that we both keep. The name I can write. I have just written it, and nothing happened. It was the other word that failed me, the one that goes beside a name, and it failed me in the doorway with his name still warm in my mouth, and it fails me now.

The desk can wait. We have coffee the following Saturday at a place on Purvis Street that he chooses. A broken ceiling fan, an Italian espresso machine whose steam has nowhere to go but the room, so that by the second sip the watch strap is sliding on my wrist, a barista who knows his order without asking, which tells me how he moves through the city: rooted, unhurried, a man with routes. He is a year or two older than me and has been in Singapore the better part of a decade. He is Tamil. That last I get not from him but from the barista, who greets him in it and gets it back easily, his two words to the barista’s three, a laugh splitting the difference. It is the first thing about him I learn from someone else. I order a flat white, which is new to me and which I have started ordering because it is what people order, and because I would still rather copy a stranger than ask a question.

He asks me what I do. I tell him the version I have arrived at over two months of the same conversation. He says, “So, medical,” and sets the word down lightly, the way he sets down any word he is testing for whether it is the right one.

“Medical-adjacent. The communications side. I work with hospitals and device companies. I produce things for them.”

“What kind of things?”

“Videos. Print. Events. The version of what they do that other people are supposed to see.”

“The version,” he says, setting the word down the way he set down medical. “So the hospital does the thing. The surgery, the trial, whatever the thing is. And then you make, what would you call it, the telling. The one with the good lighting.”

“The one with the good lighting,” I say. “Nothing in it is false. Most of the job is keeping it not false.”

“But it’s not the thing.”

“It’s not the thing. The thing already happened. Nobody was filming.”

He nods and drinks his coffee, black, no sugar. His hands around the cup have long fingers and clean nails, the left thumb against the ceramic in a way that looks practiced until I see it isn’t, it is just how he holds things. I am aware of his hands. I want one of them on me, I do not much care which, and I have known his name for a week.

“You’re staring at my coffee,” he says.

“I’m not.”

“You are. It’s all right. People stare at it.” He pushes the cup across the table. “You can try it if you want.”

I try his coffee. It is better than mine, and my mouth is where his mouth was a second ago, and I put the cup down a little faster than the coffee deserves.

“It’s better because it’s his,” Arjun says, tilting his head at the barista, who is wiping the wand of the machine with a cloth tucked into his apron and not listening, or giving it the half-ear you save for a song you have heard too many times. “Mine, yours, the same beans. He just doesn’t burn it. You burn it. I’ve watched you order. You let them leave the milk on the steam too long because you’re being polite and you don’t want to be the one who says stop.”

“I don’t say stop.”

“You never say stop. It’s going to be a problem.” He says it lightly, into the cup. “You’ve been here how long?”

“Two months.”

“Two months. And you’ve found this place, and the bar three blocks from your flat, and the minimart, and nothing in between. You go out in a straight line and come back on the same line. There’s a kopitiam one street over that’s better than this and half the price. The coffee’s worse but the people are the point. There’s an uncle there who has been wrong about the same horse for forty years and tells you about it whether you sit or not.”

“You’re managing my whole map.”

“I’m not managing. I’m offering.” A small look over the rim of the cup. “You can keep the straight line. Some people like a straight line.” He finishes his coffee, black, and looks at the bottom of the cup, and sets it down. “Saturday after this one I’m getting a dosa. The good one, near my place — not the one near here, the one near here is for tourists and people who have given up. You should come. You’ll do it wrong.”

By the second Saturday he has taken me to a dosa stall near his flat in Holland Village. A counter and four steel tables, the dosa on a plate the size of a manhole cover, the dosa itself bigger than the plate, hanging off both sides. I look at it.

“You eat it with your hands,” he says.

“All of it.”

“All of it. With a fork, the whole thing collapses and you’re just pushing a wet flag around a plate. The right hand does the work. Tearing with the thumb and the first two fingers, then you scoop the sambar with the torn edge.” He demonstrates, fast. “Not the left. The left hand does nothing. The left hand is on holiday.”

My fingers are too slow and the dosa tears wrong and the sambar runs down my wrist toward my elbow, hot, then not, a line of it drying tight on the skin before I have decided what to do about it. Arjun watches this happen.

“You’re attacking it,” he says. “It’s not going to leave, no? It’s a pancake. It has nowhere to be. You’re the only one in a hurry.”

I laugh, the real kind, the one that gets out before the part of me that manages laughs can get a hand on it, and a piece of dosa goes the wrong way and my eyes run and I put the plate down. I have not laughed like that in this country. He is already laughing, low, pleased with himself, pleased with the situation, and for a second we are two men at a steel table losing a slow fight with breakfast, sambar down my wrist, sambar on the steel, sambar on the one clean cuff I had left.

“Stop,” he says, and I stop, and he reaches across and takes my wrong hand by the wrist and turns it over, palm up. His thumb presses once at the base of mine, and I feel a hard ridge of skin on it, a callus, racquet-shaped, that I will learn the origin of months from now and am only aware of now as a roughness on a hand that is warm and dry and on mine in a food center at nine in the morning. “Two fingers and the thumb. Not the whole hand. You’re not catching a fish — you’re not strangling it either.” His hand stays on the back of mine after the lesson is done, and we both notice it stay, and when he takes it back he takes it back slowly, and the place his thumb was stays warm, and I want the hand back, on the wrist or anywhere, at nine in the morning over a pancake. “Now tear with the side, not the point. The point fights you. The side gives.” I tear with the side. It gives. “There,” he says.

The man at the griddle slides a second dosa onto a fresh plate and sets it down between us without being asked, and Arjun pushes it half an inch toward me, the host’s gesture, and tells me in the same breath that the trick with the sambar is to wait for the first dosa’s edges to go soft in it before you start, which I have just failed to do.

The man has not gone back to his iron. He asks Arjun something short, with his chin at me, and Arjun answers him in two words, easy, without looking up from my plate, and the man looks at me a second longer and goes.

“What did he ask you?”

“Who you are.”

“What did you tell him?”

He pushes the plate the rest of the way. “Eat.”

An auntie at the next table is watching my hands with the frank attention old women give a grown man learning to eat, and when I finally get a clean tear and a clean scoop she makes a small sound of approval and goes back to her own plate, and Arjun catches it and does not let it pass. “See. You’ve got the gallery on your side now.” The coffee here comes in the same green-rimmed cups as everywhere, and he drinks his black, and I drink mine with the milk because I have not yet learned to want it the other way, and the ceiling fan turns its slow turn over both of us, and for the length of a Saturday breakfast there is nowhere either of us has to be.

Outside the stall the heat has not yet arrived in full, and there is a half-hour, this early, of something almost like cool — the night’s wet still on the five-foot way, a coffee-shop uncle hosing down the tiles in front of his unit, the smell of the hose-water hitting warm concrete coming up sharp and mineral and gone. A column of schoolchildren in white and blue goes past the open front of the stall, two by two, a teacher at each end, and the auntie who approved my tear watches them with unsurprised eyes. The steel of the table has gone warm under my forearms. The fan turns. Arjun eats without once looking at his food, tearing and folding and lifting it up through the sambar in a single motion his hands settled the rules of decades before he could remember, and I eat with the whole of my attention, about half of it on the plate.

That night, in my own kitchen, I tear a slice of bread with my right hand the way he showed me, and get it wrong, and tear another, and get it wrong in a different way, and eat the wreckage over the sink like a man who has been set homework and wants the grade. I do not mention this to him.

There was a thing I was not learning. A few mornings on, he takes me to a Murugan Idli Shop on Syed Alwi Road, steel tumblers and a steam-clouded counter and idlis three to a plate, soft as something not quite set. The man at the till knows him, or knows his family’s name, which in this kind of place comes to the same thing, and says something to him in Tamil with my plate still in his hands, and whatever it is, it moves across Arjun’s face, and I watch it happen and cannot name it. I read other people’s faces for a living, the half-beat where a man decides what he will let me see, and I see Arjun decide nothing. The till-man’s sentence lands and his jaw does not set, his eyes do not cut to the door, the mouth does not arrange itself toward the easier version of whatever he is feeling. Something passes under the surface of the face the way a fish moves under a still pond, a darkening and a shift of weight, there and gone, and the surface closes over it before I can name the shape that made it. He can read what I want off my face from the far side of a hawker center. I cannot get a sentence off his across a table. I sit with an idli going cold in a sentence I have been shut out of, in a language I am a few months and nowhere into, and the not-knowing is not unpleasant; it is only total. I eat the idli anyway, cold, with the right hand, and he watches that instead, and whatever the till-man said stays where he put it.

By the new year Arjun has begun to fold me into the rest of his life. He does it without announcing it, so that the folding has happened before I understand it is a thing I might have declined. The order is his: first the barista who knows his order, then the man at the idli counter who knows his family’s name, and then, when he has decided I am ready for it, Kelvin’s table.

Kelvin has us to lunch on a Sunday. He keeps a flat in one of the older estates, three rooms he could long ago have traded up out of and has not, with the settled order of a place two men have furnished slowly and lived in a long time. Two armchairs worn to two different shapes. A second pair of reading glasses on the shelf by the door that nobody mentions. Kelvin meets us with a dish towel over one shoulder and moves back into the flat, a hand going flat to the back of a chair as he passes it, not for balance. He takes the wine Arjun brought, reads the label the length of a courtesy, and sets it on a shelf where I suspect it stays.

Graham is already in the good chair, talking at a volume the room is too small for, which he maintains on principle. He has the regional press’s entire week in him and means to spend all of it.

“He’s gone to Hong Kong,” Graham says, to no one, of someone. “Rupert. You don’t know him. Again, honestly. The one with the boat. He goes to Hong Kong the way other people go to a clinic, and comes back thinner and won’t say what for.”

“The boat’s the part that gets me,” Graham goes on, warming. “He doesn’t sail it. He’s never sailed it. He had the hull repainted last year, sight unseen, picked the blue from three photographs by email. It sits in a marina in Aberdeen accruing barnacles and dock fees and he flies up to look at it, like visiting a relative you’re hoping will remember you in the will.”

“He’ll come back,” Kelvin says from the kitchen, not turning. “They always come back. To tell you Hong Kong is finished.” He says it as if reading an arrivals board.

“Finished,” Graham agrees, savoring it. “They all come back to tell you the place they just spent three days in is finished. Hong Kong is finished, Bangkok is finished, Bali was finished the moment they personally discovered it. The whole region is a wake they keep flying in for.” He raises his glass to no one. “And yet here we all are, drinking the deceased’s wine.”

“Everyone’s leaving or rehearsing it. Half my address book is a departure lounge. There’s a man I’ve had to dinner four times, Duncan, lovely, useless, who’s been about to move to Berlin since 2009 — he’s furnished the imaginary flat, he’s got opinions about the U-Bahn. He’s picked the neighborhood twice. It gentrified out from under him both times while he was deciding about the taps. He’ll die here. They all die here, the ones who talk about leaving.” He drinks. “The quiet ones are the ones who actually go. I hear about it afterward, from their landlords. I resent it enormously.”

“You went quiet once,” Kelvin says from the kitchen.

“I went to Bali for a long weekend and you’ve never let me forget it.” Graham looks at me for the first time, openly, already enjoying it. “Except you, mate. You washed up. The American who came the wrong way down the escalator while the rest of us were sprinting up it.”

“I’m on a contract,” I say.

“They’re all on a contract, to be fair. The contract is the thing they leave.” He turns the stem of his glass without drinking. “And how long have you got this one for?” He tips his head at Arjun, fond, proprietary, past the line, which is where Graham does his living.

“As long as the firm has me,” Arjun says, before I can find a register for it. He is on the arm of the sofa, loose, a plate balanced on one knee, and he answers Graham and not me. “They post us about. You know how it goes.” He rights the plate on his knee without looking. I let Graham’s next question carry it off.

“I do know how it goes. I’ve watched it happen to better men than him.” Graham is delighted with himself. Then, to me, lighter, fonder, sharper: “Does your boyfriend know you make that face when he speaks Tamil? You’re making it now. It’s indecent.”

“Arjun’s not —” I say, and the sentence comes up in my mouth with no end on it, because the end is a word I do not own and have not, in three months, gone looking for, and the heat goes up my throat while the room waits. All day I have the next word ready for other people. This once, nothing comes. “Well. Arjun is —”

Kelvin comes in from the kitchen with a dish in both hands and sets it down in the place where the rest of my sentence should have been. “Eat,” he says, to the room, to me, to Graham, to all of it at once. “Before Graham talks it cold.” He does not look at me. He opens a space at the table that does not need opening, and says nothing about that either.

Arjun has heard the unfinished sentence. He reaches over and takes a piece of whatever Kelvin brought off my plate, and eats it, and says to Graham, “His face is like that for everyone. It’s a condition. You should see him with a spreadsheet. There is a column he loves more than he will ever love any of us.”

“A man with a favorite column,” Graham says, delighted, to the ceiling. “You’ve found yourself a romantic, Arjun. Hold on to him. The accountants make the best ones. Still waters, deep tables.”

Kelvin, from the kitchen, without turning: “He’s not wrong about the spreadsheet. Arjun reorganized my entire pantry the second time he came here. Alphabetical. I found the cumin under C. I’ve lived here twenty years; the cumin was never under anything.”

“It’s a system,” Arjun says.

“It’s a sickness,” says Graham, happy.

“Twenty years,” Kelvin says, “I knew where everything was by not knowing where anything was. I could find the cumin in the dark. Now I have to read.“

“It’s a system that means you can find the cumin.” Arjun says it to the room, but he says the next part to me, lower, while Graham, refilling glasses, is already turning Kelvin’s accusation into a story of his own. “Kelvin moved it all back the week after. He just wanted me to know he’d noticed it was done.” He says it, and goes back to his plate.

The room laughs, and the laugh takes it away, and I laugh too, a moment late, because for that moment I was somewhere else. In a room of the only men in the city who would have taken the true sentence without a flicker, I had reached for the smoother thing instead, and done it fast, and it was the speed I hated most. His squash racquet will be standing in my entryway before the lease renews.

I look, once, at the second pair of reading glasses on the shelf, and at Kelvin moving between the kitchen and a table in a flat he has never once called anything but his. He has been not-saying a thing since long before me. I let it stay where it is, and I eat Kelvin’s food, and Graham, satisfied, starts in on someone else.

Some weeks later my phone lights in the back of a cab on the way home from a shoot. Arjun wants to know when I am done and whether I feel like dosa at the usual place. There is a usual place now. I cannot point to the week it became one; no one proposed it, and nothing was agreed. I have spent the day building the man other people are meant to see, out of takes and release forms and a brand’s quiet preferences about which words may be said on camera, and somewhere across the city there is a dosa, and a person who wants nothing from me but my arrival, and I want to be there so badly my knee is going in the footwell. I write back that I will be done by seven. I do not write the rest of it down anywhere, including in my own head, where it would have needed a name.

Bugis Street, where the sailors came for the trans women and the drag shows, is gone — cleaned up and built over into a mall and a covered market, with that part left out. New tile, bright signage, a cool mall breathing air-conditioning out onto the street where the bars used to be; walking it, you would never know a thing had been taken. I walk it and admire the work and do not think, once, that it is my work too. What happens to me there is a night in February.

The underpass lets us out onto Victoria Street at ten.

Twenty-eight degrees, eighty-two Fahrenheit; I do the conversion, because I need somewhere to put my eyes. But the air outside Bugis Junction is not a number. It is a texture. It reaches the skin before anything else: a warmth with weight in it, a wet that does not bead but settles, carrying drain water and frangipani and the diesel of the idling buses.

Arjun is beside me, close enough that I hear his breathing change when he laughs at something I said about the underpass, and I would say it again, whatever it was, to hear that.

An hour earlier, then. We are at Albert Centre, the hawker stalls off Queen Street, the food center at the loud middle of its evening. Under the low ceiling the heat from forty woks has nowhere to go; it sits on the tables and the heads of the people at them. Each stall runs its own light — white tube, warmer bulb, the orange of the satay coals being fanned at the front — and the smells layer beneath it: charred chili, and the deep animal note of a soup that has been on since morning.

Arjun walks the stalls slower than I would, reading the queues. He stops at the Indian-Muslim stall at the back, the one he says makes the best murtabak in Singapore, a claim I have no standing to evaluate and no desire to contest. The uncle at the griddle has a stack of dough balls under a damp cloth and a paddle worn smooth at the grip. Arjun orders for both of us in Tamil, switching to English to ask me whether I want it spicy, then switching back before I finish answering, so that what I want and what he orders are not necessarily the same thing. The uncle says something short, no smile, a sentence that ends in a word I will later learn is the way you ask after someone’s mother. Arjun answers with a sentence that ends the same way. While the man works — flattening the dough with the heel of one hand, slapping in the egg and the mince and folding the edges into a square that hisses when it meets the oil — the two of them keep talking, and I stand a half-step out of it, holding a tray, watching Arjun be a person in a place he is from in a city he came to, both at once.

“He asked if you eat chili,” Arjun says, not turning. “I told him you’ve been practicing.”

“Have I.”

“You will have, by the time he asks again. I’ve committed you. Don’t make me a liar in front of this man.” The uncle says something else, longer, flipping the murtabak with a flat turn of the paddle and not looking up. “He says the last ang moh I brought asked for it without chili and he made it anyway, out of respect, and then watched the man drink three teh tariks.”

“There was a last one.”

The murtabak goes over on the griddle. “Don’t drink three teh tariks. He keeps count.”

The murtabak comes onto two plates, cut into squares with the side of the paddle, and a small steel dish of dark curry beside it, and a wedge of cucumber. Arjun carries both plates. “You carry the drinks,” he says, and tips his head at the chai-wallah two stalls over, and I understand that the division of the tray has already been settled between us without a sentence, the way it gets settled between people who have eaten together enough times.

We find a table a young family is leaving, the parents stacking the kids’ bowls with the speed of people who have done it a thousand times, and Arjun puts the plates down and wipes the laminate with a paper napkin out of the steel cup before either of us sits, a small automatic courtesy I have started to anticipate. The stool is bolted to the table and too low, and we sit at the angle that bolted stools make you sit, knees turned out, leaning in.

I tear the murtabak with my right hand. I get it almost right. Four months of the dosa stall in Holland Village live in the motion now — the thumb and the first two fingers, the left hand on holiday — and most nights it is no longer a thing I watch myself do. Tonight a corner of the square is too thick to tear clean and folds instead of separating, and curry runs down the side of my hand toward the wrist.

Arjun does not look up from his own plate. “You’re still attacking it.”

“It folded.”

“It surrendered.” He tears his own cleanly, scoops, eats, and only then looks at the mess at my wrist, and the look is the one from the first morning at the dosa stall, except slower now, the eyes going from my hand to my face and staying on the face. There is a thing under it that was not there in October, a fondness he is not bothering to keep off his face because the food center is loud and nobody is watching. He hands me a napkin without my asking. “You’ll get there.”

The murtabak is dense and rich and faintly sweet under the heat, the egg setting the dough into something between a pancake and a pie, the mince spiced in a way that arrives a full second after the first bite and then keeps arriving. I drag a corner through the dark curry. The chili lands at the back of the throat and stays, and my eyes go, and the sweat comes up across the top of my lip all at once, a row of it, and I drink water. Arjun watches this without comment, and I understand that he ordered it spicy on purpose, and that the asking-me-about-the-chili had been a formality, and that I do not mind. I like being ordered for. I like it somewhere well below the head.

“Good?” he says.

“Better than yours.”

“Mine is fine.” He tears another corner. “You always order the loud one. The flat white when everyone has a flat white. The spicy one because the uncle dared you with his face.” But he is pleased, and he eats the rest of his looking out at the food center, at the satay coals being fanned, at a table of taxi drivers on their break, at the gray-rag man working the returns, and once, for no reason I can find, at me, and then back out at the room.

The teh tarik comes once the plates are mostly gone, in two thick glasses already sweating in the air. The chai-wallah pulls it for us, kettle held at chest height, the tea falling a foot and a half into a metal jug at his knee, a long ribbon of pale-brown liquid that aerates as it falls, then catches and re-pours, twice, three times, until the jug holds a half-inch of foam he scrapes into our glasses with the side of a spoon. The drink is sweeter than I expect and milkier and the temperature of the inside of someone’s mouth.

Arjun watches me try it. “My father drinks it hotter than this.”

“How much hotter?”

“Throat-cancer hotter.” He says it in the same tone he used to order the murtabak. “It’s a thing among the men of his generation. Tea so hot you can’t sip it for ten minutes, and they don’t wait, and the rates of esophageal cancer for Indian men over sixty are higher than for any other men their age in the country. Nobody talks about it.”

“And the women?”

“The women drink it warm.”

He takes a sip of his own and makes a small face I cannot read.

“That’s encouraging,” I say.

“What is.”

“The Indian Men of His Generation. The esophageal cancer rates. The whole received tradition rendered as table chat.”

He sets his glass down. “Daniel. One doesn’t just say these things out loud. And you, here, with a fifty-cent tea —”

“You just said them.”

“Aiyo, but I’m allowed. My father, my generation, my problem. You — you’re a guest. Guests don’t get to name the cancers.”

“Can lah,” I say. The particle comes out half-laughing, with the wrong stress; the can is longer than the lah. He looks at me.

“You’re learning.”

“Slowly.”

“No, that was bad. That was a man who has heard the word said by other men.” He says it again, fast, throwaway, the stress landing somewhere I cannot find. “Short. You’re putting a whole sentence into two words you don’t need a sentence for.” He drinks. “But you said it.”

The stall uncle is already wiping down the next table. Across the aisle a hand-painted board reads VEG & NON-VEG CUISINE.

When the food is gone Arjun is up before I am, taking both plates and the empty glasses to the return point without being asked. I reach for my wallet and he says, over his shoulder, “You got the underpass coffee. I’ve got this.” There was no underpass coffee. It is the form the thing takes between us; no one is keeping track, and no one has ever come up short. He pays the drinks man in a folded note and waves off the change with two fingers, and the man nods, and we go.

Outside the food center the night reaches us all at once. The covered hall had its own weather, the trapped heat of the woks; the open street has the other weather, the wet-season air that sits on Singapore in February like a held breath, warm and thick and faintly green with rain that has not started and may not, the sky a low orange where the city light hits the cloud. A drain along the curb is running with the afternoon’s water still, and somewhere close a frog is going, one note, patient, and stops when we pass and starts again behind us. Arjun loosens the second turn of his sleeve and turns it up once more against the heat, and I watch him do it, and I want to put my mouth on the inside of it, at the elbow, where it would taste of salt, and he catches me watching and does not say anything, and we step off the curb together.

We walk south along Victoria Street toward the river. He walks at a pace that seems slower than mine until I see I am the one adjusting. His shoulder is close to mine, not touching, close enough that I feel the warmth of it through the air between his sleeve and my sleeve. A space of about an inch. I have not paid attention to an inch of air since my first weeks here, when every proximity was a question and every question had a body attached to it. I am paying attention now.

Halfway down the block he steps without warning into a mama shop, the kind of narrow corner shop that sells everything and admits it in no order — newspapers stacked on the step, a cooler of drinks humming against the back wall, packets of nuts and biscuits and dried cuttlefish clipped to a grid that runs floor to ceiling, a row of plastic toys faded to the same dust color by the doorway sun they get all day. He buys a small bag of sour plums and pays the auntie behind the counter in coins he counts out exactly so she does not have to make change, and she says something to him and he laughs. Outside he opens the plums and holds the bag out to me. The plum is salted and sour enough to pull the whole inside of my mouth toward one point, and dusted with something — licorice, maybe, or five-spice — that arrives after the sour and stays, and I make a face, the whole face, nothing like the one I use for clients, and he watches me make it with open enjoyment.

“You don’t have to like it.”

“I don’t dislike it. My face is making a decision without me.”

“That happens to you a lot. Your face is the only honest thing about you. The rest of you is in PR. It’s an acquired thing. Everyone’s auntie has a jar of these. You suck on it for an hour, you ruin one tooth, and somewhere around the second one you stop being able to remember not liking it. Everything here works like that. It’s awful, it’s awful, it’s still awful, and then one day, don’t ask me how, it’s yours, and you’ll fight a stranger over it.”

“That’s a sales pitch.”

“It’s the only one that works here.” He takes one himself, unbothered, finds no bin, and pockets the stone, and I like him for it out of all proportion.

We pass a five-foot way where an old man has set up a folding stool and a transistor radio and is listening to what sounds like a Rediffusion opera, his eyes closed, one slipper off; a temple’s side wall, the incense from inside reaching the street in a thread that crosses the diesel and the drains and is gone. Arjun nods at the temple — not a bow, just an acknowledgment, a thing his hand does at chest height almost too small to see — and keeps walking, and does not explain it, and I do not ask.

“So tell me about Brooklyn,” he says.

I tell him about Brooklyn selectively. The apartment I gave up. The job at the firm’s New York office. The transfer, which I describe as a career move because that is one of the things it was. I say, “I needed a change.”

“Everyone says that.”

I am quiet.

“It got tiring,” I say. “Holding it up. The version of me I was being there.”

He looks at me a moment longer than he has at any earlier point in the evening.

“Performing,” he says, in a tone that is not a guess. “Yeah. I know it. I knew it longer in India than I’ve known it here. But I know it here too, some weeks.”

“It’s the same thing?”

“It’s the same shape. You just do it in a different room.”

“What did you say, when you came?”

“I said I came for a job. Which was true. And then I stayed because —” He pauses. We are at a corner, waiting for the signal, and the pause is long enough that I look at him. His face in the streetlight is the specific yellow-sodium color the older lamps make, a color I have not noticed until now because I have not looked at anyone’s face in that light from this angle: the jaw, the line of the neck where the collar meets skin, a bead of sweat sitting in the hollow there and going nowhere. I am staring. I want my thumb on that bead. I keep my hands where they are.

“Because what?”

“Because I hadn’t finished what I came to do. And then because I had, and by then it was too late to leave without it being a statement.”

The signal changes. We cross.

Bugis, the neighborhood and not the mall, has by 2013 been demolished and rebuilt. The nearest the old street comes to surviving is on television: the year after I arrived, a drama called Serangoon Road, set in the Singapore of fifty years before, rebuilt the old Bugis on a soundstage and ran on Sunday nights — the street lit and dressed and walked through again. Arjun had watched two episodes when it aired and given up. He watched a third because I said I should see it, on the couch at Fortuna Court, and gave it ten minutes of silence. Then: “That sign is right. The sign painter they found, whoever he is, he’s right.” A while later: “They built the whole street and forgot to put anyone on it who’d actually lived there. It’s very clean. Everybody’s teeth are wrong.” He watched to the end anyway, angled forward, reading the set the way he reads a queue. What he was looking for on that street I could not have told you. The credits ran and he said, “They’ll do the whole country like this eventually. Beautifully. You’ll visit,” and turned it off.

The back of Parkview Square cuts a different shape against the night, bronze Art Deco dropped without apology into a part of Bugis that is otherwise post-war shophouses and the heritage market. Arjun glances up at it as we pass. “Batman building,” he says, not slowing. “They wanted Gotham.” He does not look up again.

We walk past the glass-roof market without going in. The street market that runs along its flank is still open, the late stalls under their own bare bulbs, racks of phone cases and printed scarves and rubber sandals graded by size, a man frying you tiao in a wok at the curb, a woman selling cut fruit from a cart of crushed ice — pineapple in long spears, watermelon, green guava with sour-plum powder in a paper twist beside it. The aisle between the stalls is barely two people wide and the crowd moves through it at the crowd’s own speed, slower than either of us would walk alone, and Arjun lets a family with a stroller go first, and a teenager pushing past with a phone held flat in front of his face, and a woman carrying a rice cooker still in its box. For a second the crowd closes between us and I lose him, and then the aisle opens and he is there again, a half-step ahead, glancing back to find me without making a thing of finding me. His hand comes up at my elbow — not a hold, a touch, a steering, there and gone — to bring me around a puddle I have not seen, and I feel the place it was for longer than it was there.

A vendor calls something at Arjun in Hokkien and Arjun answers in the few words of it he has, badly, and the vendor laughs, delighted, and says it again slower, and Arjun says one more word and the vendor throws up both hands and waves us off. “I have about eight words,” Arjun says, low, only to me. “Six of them are food. One of them I just used wrong. All these years and I have eight words.” We pass the fruit cart and he buys nothing and looks at all of it.

Arjun says, “My mother would have loved this. Not the new part. The older one, before they put the roof on.” He says it without nostalgia.

I say, “Did you grow up here?”

He says, “No, I grew up in Chennai. Mylapore: three streets, a temple, and a market my mother knew better than her own kitchen. She bought our fabric from a man who swore every bolt was the last of its kind. It never was. I came over at twenty-three.”

A small dry-goods shop on the corner has a sign over the door in Tamil. Arjun points at it.

He says, “Read it.”

I say, “I don’t know any of those.”

He says, “Try.”

I make a sound that approximates what I think it is. It is wrong. He laughs once, smaller than the laugh he gives when I have said something he could have said better. He says, “That’s po, not pa. Your mouth is open too wide. Close it about half a millimeter. The vowel is rounder.”

I try again. Wrong again, in a different way.

“Half a millimeter,” I say.

“Half.”

It is the smallest instruction anyone has ever given me, and I laugh at it, at the idea of a mouth with tolerances, at a word that fails by the width of a pen line. He holds up his thumb and finger with nothing between them, to show me the distance, and keeps them there until I am done.

He says, “It will take you longer than you think. The pa in your mouth is not the po in Tamil. The tongue is in a place yours doesn’t go without help.”

He is watching my mouth while he says it. I am aware of the watching, a heat with a location and no contact, and aware, separately, of being a man on a dark street having the shape of his own mouth corrected by another man standing close enough to correct it, close enough that I want him to correct it with his own, and the two awarenesses will not sit in the same part of me, and I change the subject.

I say, “What does the sign say?”

He says, “Pongal-um Kadai. The Pongal Shop. They sell the rice and the lentils and the jaggery for the Tamil New Year.” He points at one of the characters. “This one is po. This word is kadai, which is shop. You’ll see kadai on a hundred signs in Little India. By the time you can read kadai, you can read about a tenth of what Little India is telling you.”

I try po one more time. He doesn’t correct me.

We turn down a side street. A row of shophouses with painted shutters, a cat on a concrete post, three motorcycles in sequence on the five-foot way. The street has the particular hush of a Singapore back lane after ten, which is not silence — there is the drip of an aircon unit somewhere above, the tick of a cooling engine, a television two floors up running a Korean drama dubbed into Mandarin at the volume of a household that has stopped hearing it — but a hush all the same, the day’s traffic gone out of it, the shophouses leaning their stored heat back into the lane. The five-foot way is uneven underfoot, the tiles laid by a dozen different owners across a hundred years, a lip here, a patch of newer cement there, and I watch my feet without meaning to and then stop watching them because Arjun does not watch his; he knows the lane the way he knows the queue and the cup and the order of a meal, in his feet, without looking. The air between his shoulder and mine has gone to less than the inch. A woman on the second floor is hanging laundry on a bamboo pole extended from her window, and the pole wobbles with each shirt she clips, and I watch it wobble. Six months earlier I would not have seen her at all.

I say the heat doesn’t let up, even now. Arjun shrugs. “In New York hot is an event,” he says. “Here it’s infrastructure.” The way he says infrastructure, softer at the second syllable than I would, makes me look at him longer than I have been. He notices. He keeps walking.

The side street takes the inch between us and makes it the only thing there is. On the main road I had been measuring it. Here I stop measuring.

I can feel the warmth coming off the side of him, the heat of a body that has been walking in the night air for an hour, and under the warmth something that was, this morning, whatever he put on, and is now mostly just him, and it is doing to me what a shirt has no business doing on a public lane. My own body has gone somewhere ahead of me.

Arjun takes my hand.

He does it without preamble. We are on the side street, between two rows of shophouses, under a lamp that is not the sodium-yellow of the main road but a warmer, weaker bulb that makes the shadows longer, and he reaches across the inch of air and takes my hand the way a person takes a glass from a table — with no announcement, and no permission needed. There is a version of this gesture I already know. It lives in doorways: a grip that begins as a handshake and then declines to finish as one, that holds a beat past anything a handshake needs and stops short of anything that would ask to be named, indoors, deniable, between just the two of us. This is that gesture with the door taken away. It does not stop short. It does not let go. We are outside, and the street is not empty, and his hand stays.

His palm is dry and warm, and the squash-racquet callus I learned at the dosa stall is there under my fingers, and I find that I have been waiting to feel it in this register and not the other, the hand as a hand and not a lesson. It is a hand holding a hand on a public street; it is not, by any measure I could write down, more than that. It goes through me as though it were more than that. Four inches of skin, and I feel it in the wrist, up the forearm, and in places a hand cannot reach and has no business reaching. What it tells me, once it gets there, is the plain fact that I have wanted exactly this, the specific weight of his specific hand, for longer than I have let myself know it.

His thumb moves once along the inside of my wrist where the skin is thinnest, and finds the place where the pulse is, and stays on it, and my pulse is not a thing I have ever been able to produce a version of.

At the same time my chest draws in, not quite a flinch, the shoulders squaring a degree.

And I do not pull my hand away.

I could. The side street is not empty — a man locking up a shop three doors down, across the road a couple walking a small dog, behind us a motorbike idling. Any of them could look. The couple does. Their walk slows a half-step, the dog taking the slack, and something passes over the two of them that I am too far away to read, a conference or a nothing, and their pace comes back before I can rule which. My hand stays where it is. That is the entire event. I never find out what they saw, and it stays open in me for three streets. I do not know what they would see — two men holding hands, one South Asian, one white, both in the collared shirts and leather shoes of a certain kind of Singapore professional — and I do not know what that picture means in this neighborhood, on this street, under that lamp. I have seen men hold hands in Little India dozens of times, easily, without code. This is not that. I know what it means to me. For a second the word for it is right there, behind my teeth, easy. Then the old habit swallows it and hands me the wide, safe question in its place: what it means to the city, which I can’t say.

Arjun’s hand is still in mine. He has not tightened his grip, has not looked at me, is walking at the same pace and looking ahead as if the hand-holding were the least remarkable thing about the evening, an ordinary fact about two people on a side street who happen to be walking in the same direction. His breath is at the same rate as mine, or mine has adjusted; I could not say which.

He does not look like a man being brave. He is being himself in a space he has decided is safe enough. He has lived here for years. He knows the scan. He has run it longer than I have. He has, at some point in those years, arrived at a place in the calculation where the answer is: here, now, on this street, yes.

I have not arrived there. I am arriving.

And so we walk, and I hold his hand, and it is not enough, and it is all there is going to be tonight, and I know both of those at once and the knowing does not help. I want him. I want him against the shophouse wall in the dark part past the lamp, his back on the plaster, the whole weight of his chest coming through two shirts gone to cling, his sweat and mine already the same sweat on the one hand of his I have, his hips in my hands, his mouth open, the sound he would make. I want to find out what he sounds like. I want his hand somewhere other than my hand. I want to be the one who says stop, once, and mean the opposite. I have all of this in me on a side street in Bugis at ten past ten on a Tuesday with a couple and a small dog across the road, and I am hard, in office trousers, walking at a pace we have agreed on, and nothing about my face, I hope, is saying any of it. He knows. Of course he knows. Neither of us slows down. The wanting stays exactly where it is, in the hand and the arm, and I carry it up the street like a bag I’ve been handed, and it is heavy, and I would not put it down for anything.

We walk like that for about four minutes, which is the length of the side street and the turn onto the next one and about half a block past a kopitiam closing up for the night, the chairs being stacked inside with the clatter of metal on tile. I hold it anyway. Then the street opens onto a larger road, and the light changes, and there are more people, and the space between our hands opens by an inch, and then by another, and then we are walking side by side again, not touching, at a distance that is ordinary and that feels, for the first time tonight, like a decision rather than a default.

We do not go straight to a taxi. There is a coffeeshop on the corner where the road meets the canal, one of the ones that does not close. A corner unit with its shutters up and half its lights off and a man behind the drinks counter counting a night with three customers in it. Arjun goes in without asking whether I want to, and I follow him.

He orders two kopi siew-dais. The man sets them down in the cups that are the same cups in every coffeeshop in Singapore, thick-walled, a green band around the rim, and we take them to a marble-topped table near the open side, the marble the first cool thing my forearms have touched all night, where the canal is a darker stripe under the dark, and the air off the water is the air it was on the side street.

“You drink it local now,” Arjun says.

“I’m trying it.”

“You’re trying everything. The flat white. The murtabak. The —” He tips his head at the kopi.

“You’re keeping a list.”

“I notice things. It’s a condition. You’d know.”

I would. I drink the kopi, bitter at the top and sweet at the bottom where the condensed milk has not been stirred all the way.

“On the street, back there,” I say, and do not finish it.

“I know. You did the thing your face does, the checking. It’s fair. Technically the two of us are a crime, no? Three seventy-seven A. Nobody uses it. They keep it in a drawer, and everybody knows the drawer is there.” He turns the cup a quarter-turn. “India has the same one, and ours is older. Almost the same number, three seventy-seven with no letter on the end. The same empire wrote both of them. We struck ours down a few years back, in the Delhi court. Now the big court’s sitting on whether to put it back.” He drinks. “They’ll surprise you. They like to.”

He makes it sound like housekeeping. It is the best copy I have heard in this country. The checking my face does on its own, the thing he was kind about a minute ago, is the drawer, working.

The man behind the counter has begun the long closing of a place that does not exactly close: he lifts the steel trays out of the display and stacks them, runs water over the kopi sock and hangs it on its hook, turns off the bank of lights over the cold drinks so that the corner where we sit is the brightest thing left. He does not look at us. We are not in his way and we have paid. A cat comes in off the five-foot way, sits in the doorway with the proprietary calm of a cat who is fed here, looks at the two of us, and decides we are not the development of the evening it had hoped for. Arjun watches it go.

“That’s the boss,” he says.

“The cat.”

“The cat runs four of these. He franchises.” He says it flatly, and waits, and I laugh.

“Four of them,” I say. “All this one cat.”

“He’s got a guy at each.” He drinks the last of the kopi and tips the cup to get the sweet sludge of condensed milk off the bottom, unhurried. “My father did the markets like this — not the coffee, he never sat for that, but the walk. He’d take me through the whole row and buy nothing, and the men would tell him things, and we’d come home and my mother would say what did you get and he’d say news.” He sets the cup down. “He thought he was very funny.”

“You’re a little funny.”

“Give it a year. You’ll meet funnier people and I’ll have to start trying, and I don’t want to start trying. I’ve got it very easy with you right now.”

He says it into the cup. He looks out at the man closing up the trays, the cat gone, the canal a dark stripe, and for a moment he is not here, and then he is.

Outside, a delivery truck idles at the lights with no one behind it, and a man on a bicycle goes past with two crates of empty bottles strapped over the back wheel, the bottles ringing softly against each other at every seam in the road, a sound that arrives, crosses, and does not come back. Arjun says nothing, and the saying nothing is comfortable. Six months of rooms with nobody else in them, and this is the first silence with someone in it.

His hand is flat on the marble, the same hand, and I am aware of it the way I was aware of his hands at the coffee place on the first Saturday, except that the awareness has a fact under it now that it did not have then. The marble is not wide. His hand, the cup, my hand make a short triangle on it. He does not move his hand toward mine. I do not move mine toward his. I want to; I can feel the one tendon at the back of my hand that would do it. One of us could close the small distance; we both know it is available; and we both leave it on the table. A fluorescent tube buzzes above the counter and a radio somewhere plays a Chinese ballad below conversation, and we sit in it, and the not-moving is not a refusal. It is the four minutes on the side street again, in a different room. He knows the room. I am learning it.

He says, “You’re quiet.”

“I’m tired.”

“You’re not tired. You’re —” He stops. Declines to finish a sentence whose ending he has already shown me he has. He drinks his kopi. “It’s fine, Daniel. You don’t have to have a face for it. That’s the —” He gestures at the table, the two cups, the canal, the hour. “That’s what the late one is for. Nobody’s awake to need a face.”

“Is that why you come to these.”

“I come to these because the kopi is a dollar.” But he is smiling.

We finish the cups. Outside, the main road has the specific emptiness of Singapore after eleven, swept and lit. He is walking to the train. I am going to find a taxi. We stand for a second at the point where the standing has to become two directions.

“Saturday,” he says, meaning the dosa, the stall near his flat. I say Saturday. He goes, at the pace that only looks slower, and I watch him to the corner before I go to find the cab.

I take a taxi home because the MRT is down to its last trains and because the taxi is seven SGD and because I am not, at eleven-forty on a Tuesday night in February, in a condition to negotiate the night bus. The driver pronounces the destination, Moulmein Rise, before I finish saying it, picking the words up where I started them. “Moulmein Rise,” he says again, satisfied — a man somewhere past sixty, a small brass deity zip-tied to the dash and a thermos wedged in the gap beside it, the radio turned low to a call-in show he answers under his breath. Some caller has said something that does not sit right with him; he tells the windshield cannot like that one, mild, final, and settles it. The host says something back to the caller, the caller protests, and the driver shakes his head at both of them. “Two of them also wrong,” he says. He pulls out without checking the empty road, because the road is his at this hour, and he knows which lights will hold for him all the way to the river. I sit in the back with my hands in my lap, the cab’s aircon turning the walk cold on me inside a block.

My left hand is warm. My right hand is cool. It is February; both hands are the same temperature. I turn my left hand over in my lap anyway, palm up, and look at it under the passing streetlights. The same hand it was all evening, the same lines, the same knuckles. It does not look different. I put it against my mouth anyway, in the back of a taxi, at midnight, like a teenager, and I keep it there.

The taxi passes Balestier Market, shuttered at this hour, the metal pulled down over the curry-puff station where the auntie knows my order. The taxi passes a minimart on the corner, its fluorescent light still on, and I cannot pass a lit minimart at this hour now without the mochi and the condensation and his hand. The taxi pulls into the covered drop-off at Fortuna Court, and the security guard looks up from his small television, and I pay the driver and walk through the lobby. The building asleep around me, Aunty Lim’s downstairs unit gone dark by ten as it always is, the mailbox alcove’s small fluorescent humming alone. I take the elevator and press the button for the fourth floor and stand in the corridor that smells of laundry from one end and something indeterminate from the other.

I let myself into the apartment. The aircon is running on the night setting it always runs on, the hum the apartment’s resting pulse. By February I have stopped registering it as a sound. The compressor catches once with a small shudder and steadies, and the cold comes back to where it was.

I stand in the living room with the lights off and the shirt off. I have not been to the gym in eight days, and I can feel my body in the dark, and I am hard again, in my own flat, with nobody in the country to tell, and I stand there and let it be what it is. This is what the staying looks like.

The racquet has been by my door since March.

It is Arjun’s. It leans in the corner of the entryway where a person would keep an umbrella, head down, the grip wrapped in a tape gone gray along the edges, and it has been there long enough that I have stopped seeing it. Arjun does not live in the apartment. He has a flat in Holland Village with his own bed and his own kitchen and a closet of the suits he wears to an office I have been inside exactly once. Lately the office has been circling him: a bigger job, a floor higher and a continent over, that he brings up with his mouth full and his eyes on the television and then does not bring up again. I have not asked, and he has not said. On the nights he stays at Fortuna Court he brings a bag, and the bag goes home with him, and the racquet does not. Neither of us has ever said anything about the racquet. It arrived one Saturday and then it was a fact of the apartment, the way the aircon is a fact of the apartment, and by this summer I have learned not to ask a question whose answer I already have and do not want said aloud.

It rained in the night. I know it before I am properly awake, from the smell that comes in under the door of the balcony, wet concrete and soaked leaf, green, with the iron tang of the drains running somewhere below and the rain trees letting go of what they caught all at once when the wind moves them. The light is not yet light, only the gray that comes before it, and the aircon has been off all night because Arjun cannot sleep in it and I have learned to sleep without it, in the thick warm dark with the window open a hand’s width and the city breathing in. He is on his back, one arm off the side of the bed, all the way under, breathing in the slow way I read before I learned anything else about how he sleeps. The sheet is pushed down. The heat of him is along one whole side of me, hip to ankle, and the cooler air from the window is along the other, and I lie there half a body warm and do nothing about either, because there is nowhere I have to be and no version of the morning I have to produce. I could put my mouth on the shoulder nearest me. I want to, and I don’t, because I would rather have him asleep against me for twenty more minutes than awake and looking for his shoes. The balcony rail outside is beaded with rain going slowly to nothing in the first of the warmth. I count the twenty minutes in his breathing and get to eleven, which for me is a personal best.

Before he goes, there is the milk. He cannot start a Sunday without it warm — not heated in the microwave, which he holds in something close to moral contempt, but stood over in the small steel pan he brought up from his own kitchen one weekend and never took home, the heat low, the surface watched for the first crawl of a skin. He drinks it plain, no coffee in it, no sugar, a thing his grandmother did and her mother before her, he told me once and only once, in the offhand way he gives me the load-bearing facts so I will not make an occasion of them. I have learned to leave the pan to him. The one time I tried to do it for him I let it climb too fast and a skin came up gray and wrinkled at the edge.

“It’s fine,” I said. “It’s milk.”

He looked into the pan, and then at me, with enormous gentleness. “It is not fine. You boiled it. One doesn’t boil it, one stands with it.” He took the spoon out of my hand without any drama at all. “You can do many things, Daniel. You will not be doing this.” And he scraped the skin off the edge and started again, patient with the milk in a way he was patient with almost nothing else, and let me keep the toast.

This morning he has it going at quarter to eight, one hand on the pan handle, the other turning the spoon in slow figure-eights, while the radio he keeps tuned to a station I cannot find again when he is gone plays something with a flute in it under a man reading the shipping notices. The kaya toast is already gone from me and I am hungry again, and I sit at the table and watch the back of his neck and want two things at once and will get neither before eight. There is a steel bowl on the counter under a plate; he soaked rice and dal in it the night before last and ground it yesterday, and the batter is fermenting toward the middle of the week, when he says the sourness will be right, and the bowl sitting there working, a thing started days before the meal it is for, is the most domestic object in the apartment, more than the racquet, more than the key. It smells, already, faintly sour under the plate when I walk past it, and I walk past it more than I need to.

He has set a second bowl to soak before he leaves, smaller, the rice and dal for next Sunday’s, a week ahead of when anyone could need it. The dal settles and ticks against the steel as the water takes it; the counter still smells of the ghee and the cardamom he cracked under the flat of the knife. He does it without telling me he is doing it, and when I ask him why two he says, “So there is always one going,” and does not look up from the soak, and I understand that there is a version of this kitchen, running on a clock I cannot see, that he is keeping for a reason that is his, and that I am not going to be given this morning. He wants the batter working on my counter and not on his own. That much I can read, at quarter to eight, with the pan going and the aircon still off. The why he keeps. He pours the milk into the one glass he uses, rinses the pan, sets it upside down on the rack, and drinks standing at the window in the flat early light. His throat moves. There is a white line on his upper lip after, and he takes it with his tongue without knowing he has done it, and I say nothing, because there is nothing in the moment that wants saying, and because I would have liked to do that for him.

Before he leaves he lifts the upturned plate off the bowl and looks at the batter underneath and stirs it once with the back of a spoon, down to the bottom and up, reading it the way he reads the milk and the price and the cricket, for whether it is doing what it should. “It’s coming,” he says, to the bowl, not to me. “Slowly. Your apartment is cold for it. In Chennai this would be done by lunch.” He bends and smells it, frowns, not displeased, the frown of a man taking a reading he trusts more than the calendar. “Another day. Maybe two. It tells you. You don’t tell it.” He settles the plate back. Under his breath, once, a word in Tamil, to the batter, not offered for translation. “My mother would have it on the stone by now. She did not measure anything. You ask her how much and she shows you the hand.” He cups his palm, holds it out, a measure that is no measure. “This much. And it was always right, and if you used the same hand it came out wrong.” He says it lightly, the grandmother and now the mother handed across in one Sunday, and I want the whole of it, the stone and the hand, and I know better than to ask, and then he washes the spoon and sets it on the rack and goes to find his shoes, and the bowl sits on the counter working at its own speed, slow, in the cold of my apartment.

He has gone to the courts at eight. He plays squash on Sunday mornings at a club near his flat, with a rotation of men he has been playing with for years: a corporate lawyer, an Australian whose name I never retain, a Tamil uncle. He comes back from it unhurried, his hair wet from the club shower and combed with his fingers, smelling of a soap that is not mine, and I like the wrong soap on him. I like that he comes back smelling of somewhere I wasn’t and gets into my shower anyway.

In the kitchen, in the slow hour I have to myself after he leaves, I notice the salt-and-pepper grinder is still where I set it down after dinner, the width of its own base to the right of where it lives. Yesterday afternoon, while I was in the shower, he had moved it home; I never saw him do it, but I have reached for that grinder often enough to feel when it is off. Counter, stove, grinder, press. He moved it without saying he had moved it, the way he moves a book on the nightstand back into the order of the books beside it. The kitchen has a settled grammar I did not put there. I pick up the grinder and set it down on the side the kitchen has assigned it, and stand there with my hand still on it.

There are other corrections. The dish towel hangs folded in thirds over the oven handle, which is not how I hang a dish towel; I bunch it through the handle, and at some point in the spring it stopped being bunched and started being folded in thirds, and I have never once seen him do it, only the result, every morning, the towel in thirds. The mugs face the same way on the shelf now, handles all to the right. The bag of his particular tea, the one with the cardamom already in it that he buys at the Mustafa Centre at one in the morning because that is when he ends up at the Mustafa Centre, lives in the cupboard behind my coffee, where I would never have put it and where it is, I have come to understand, exactly correct.

None of it was negotiated. The apartment was mine and then, by a slow accretion of small things done right and never mentioned, it became a place that a second person also lived inside the logic of, and I find I can stand in my own kitchen and read, in the position of every object, the hand of a man who is at this hour on a squash court across the city, missing a ball on purpose to keep a point alive. This is the one hour of the week I am alone in this country and not lonely in it, and I know the difference. Lonely is the balcony at eleven at night, the lights on across the canal, a whole city that needs nothing from me on a Sunday. I can still walk that kitchen from here, object by object, the grinder on its side, the towel in its thirds.

“You moved the grinder,” I said.

“I moved the grinder.”

“There’s a right side.”

“There is a right side. You keep putting it on the side where the cloth gets caught when you wipe down. I am not saying anything about it. I am only correcting it.” He set it down, an inch to the left, where it has lived ever since. “When you are not here I leave it on your side. So it doesn’t get used to me.”

He went to find his shoes, and I never moved the grinder back.

There had been a first morning, a year and some months back, before the racquet had a corner and the milk had a pan, when he stayed over for the first time.

We had come up late, and it had been the good kind of late, and then he was asleep, on his back, one arm off the side of the bed, all the way under, and I was awake, all the way awake, because of the plain animal fact of another body in the bed, taking up room I had gotten used to having, breathing on a rhythm that was not mine and would not adjust. The window was open a hand’s width and the night came in at intervals, the drains, the frangipani, once a motorbike going up through its gears somewhere past the canal. At some point he had shifted in his sleep, and where his arm had been against mine there was now a gap the width of a hand, and the skin of my forearm was keeping the warmth of him and losing it, and I wanted the arm back. That arm, at that stretch of skin. I was hard, on and off, the whole gray hour, and it was not even about that; it was the arm. I lay in the gray-before-light wanting it and not moving, because a man you have slept beside once is not yet a man you can reach for in his sleep.

I could have slept. I did not want the morning to start, because starting it would spend it, and I did not yet know there would be another one.

When he woke he did not reach for his phone. He turned his head, found me already awake, and looked at me for a while, unhurried, wanting nothing I could detect except to be looking. “You’ve been up,” he said. “A while,” I said. “You watch people sleep. Of course you do.” He did not seem to mind being watched. He did not offer a sentence about the night or ask for mine. He pulled me in by the back of the neck, dry-palmed, the callus at the base of the thumb, and we did not get up for the milk for a long time, and I was loud, for me, which is not very, and neither of us made anything of it.

We go for the dosa.

The stall is in Holland Village, near his flat, the same stall he took me to on the second Saturday, when my fingers were too slow and the dosa tore wrong and the sambar ran down my wrist and he reached over and adjusted my grip, without looking up, the way you adjust someone’s hold on a racquet. I understand it now as the first lesson in a curriculum neither of us ever called one. It went on, Saturdays and Sundays, at this stall and at others, the places off Serangoon Road where he orders in the script I cannot read, and the proof of it is that my hands have learned.

This is a small thing to be proud of. I am proud of it anyway. I tear the dosa with the right hand, thumb and the first two fingers, and scoop the sambar with the torn edge, and I do it without watching my own hand do it, and I am hungry enough that the first piece goes in too hot and I take it anyway, the roof of my mouth paying for it, and a run of sambar goes down my thumb and I get it with my tongue before the wrist. “Because the rest of us are running,” he says, delighted with it. “Because he has made a study of the one ball he cannot lose to, which is the ball he does not play.” Arjun points the torn dosa at me like a man making a closing argument. “You would love him, Daniel. The griddle gives off a smell I could not have named two years ago and could now find blindfolded across a city — the toasted-rice smell of the batter hitting the hot iron, the ghee going at the edges, the curry-leaf and mustard-seed crackle from the pot of sambar kept at a low boil all morning. The tin holds the heat down on us. Arjun sits with his back very straight, which he does everywhere, and eats fast and neatly, and between mouthfuls he rolls his right thumb absently against the side of his first finger — the squash hand. I have stopped pointing it out to him. I just watch the thumb work, and I want it, that thumb, on the inside of my wrist where it goes when he is reading me, and I eat instead. The uncle who runs the stall knows Arjun’s order and has come, over the year, to know mine, which is Arjun’s order, because at some point I stopped ordering and started arriving. He says something to Arjun in Tamil that ends in the word I now know is the way you ask after a person’s mother, and Arjun answers, and then the uncle says something else, shorter, with a tip of his head toward me, and Arjun’s face does a thing, and he does not translate it.

“He says you eat properly now.”

“That’s the whole sentence.”

The trouble, when it comes, comes in the lift.

“I read part of the notice,” I say.

“Two,” Arjun says.

“Two.” She lets that sit with the vegetables.

“Don’t.”

The pool is not closed on Sunday. We have it nearly to ourselves.

“We could.” He does not move toward doing a few lengths.

“There’s an order. You have done the pool in your head already. You’re at the gallery right now, checking the light. That’s the trouble with you, Daniel. You’ve already had this day. I’m still in it.”

“The show closes today,” I say.

“It is good,” he says, delighted, settling it. “The light in your head is very good. You have lit the gallery.” He pushes off the wall and floats onto his back, arms out, addressing this to the sky. “I am in love with a man who has lit a gallery he is not standing in.”

He says it to the sky, and I say nothing back.

The short laugh first. And the worst of it is that he is right, exactly and without margin: I have a clear, false memory of a swim I have not had.

“I’ll do the lengths now,” I say, with what I believe is dignity, and push off the wall, and get through perhaps four strokes of a front crawl before I understand I am swimming lengths to prove a point about not having a plan, which is its own kind of plan, and I stop in the middle of the pool, standing, the water at my chest, and look back at him floating there reading the sky, and that is when the wide one comes, the whole face, because he has watched me work it out in real time and waited for me to arrive at it.

“There he is,” Arjun says, to the sky, not to me. “He’s found it.”

A family at the shallow end, the parents under the one umbrella, and otherwise the long blue rectangle and the heat coming straight down and the towers of the building across the canal standing in the water upside down — the nearer tower holding its shape, the farther one already coming apart, kept from being a building by the smallest movement of the surface.

Arjun swims a slow front crawl to the far wall and back, and I watch the shoulders do it, the water going off the back of him in a sheet at the turn, and I want him, here, in the chlorine, with the family at the shallow end, and I put the thought back where it came from and take it out again. I go looking, the way I always end up looking with him, for the seam, the place where the ease is being managed, and I cannot find it. It is a face that stays itself when it is looked at. I spend my days with faces that arrange themselves toward whoever is watching, and his does not arrange. He stops and stands with his forearms on the tiled edge and his back to the sun, and I come and stand next to him, our forearms on the edge, and we do not say anything for a while. The family at the shallow end packs up and leaves — the father carrying the smaller child upside down and shrieking under one arm, the wet footprints going out the gate and drying behind them before they reach it — and then it is the two of us. The afternoon does not pass so much as deepen, the light getting heavier rather than lower.

Somewhere in there I notice that my breathing and his breathing have arrived at the same rate, the rise and the settle of two ribcages against the same tiled edge, and I do not know which of us matched the other and I do not test it by changing mine. He turns his head and rests his cheek on his own forearm, facing me, eyes shut against the sun, and says nothing, and the wet at his hairline catches the light, and a drop runs down past his ear and off his jaw and back into the pool without his moving to stop it. We breathe at one rate and watch the broken towers stand on their heads. It is the most married I have ever felt.

His phone goes, on the towel, at the pool’s edge.

He climbs out. I watch him do it, the water coming off him, down the groove of his back, the shorts gone heavy and low on his hips, and I look, because there is no one left at the pool to see me look. He stands dripping on the tile and reads the screen and then takes the call, two fingers pressed to his free ear, and speaks for the length of a short call, and every second of it is in the other Arjun, the office one, the voice with the corners squared, and I catch some of it. A name, a number, the word Tuesday, the word London said once, flatly. Then he says a thing that makes whoever it is laugh across the connection, and he hangs up and comes back to the edge and lowers himself into the water and his breathing takes a few seconds to find the rate again, and I let it.

“Work,” he says. “On a Sunday.”

“What did they want.”

“Nothing. They want me to know they’re thinking of me. It’s a whole register, Daniel. Being thought of. They make you feel it’s a gift.” He wipes water from his eyes with the heel of his hand. “A man rings on a Sunday to tell you that you were in a room you were not in, that your name came up and was set down again gently. And you are meant to spend the rest of the day a little warmer for it. Which I will. That’s the annoying part, I will.”

“So what did he give you,” I say.

“Nothing. He gave me nothing, Daniel. There was no work in it, no decision, not even a question I could go away and answer. Ten minutes of a partner’s Sunday, and partners cost, they cost by the quarter-hour even when they are being warm. All that money, spent, to hand a man nothing.”

“Is it.”

“It’s a gift the way a list is a list.” He bumps his shoulder against mine, once, under the surface, where it doesn’t count, where no one can see it. “Don’t worry about London. It’s just where the men on the phone like to imagine themselves.”

“You said London like it was a meeting.”

“It is a meeting. There’s a panel. They fly you somewhere, they look at you, they decide whether to look at you more.” A cloud crosses the sun; the water cools half a degree against my arms, and I let the word panel sink down into it. He floats it off the way he floats everything, on its back, arms out. “It’s nothing, Daniel. It’s a thing firms do. You make a man feel chosen once, when he is thirty and hungry, and after that he does the rest himself. He keeps himself worth choosing. You never have to do anything again, maybe ring him on a Sunday. I’ve watched it done to better men than me.”

And he ducks under the surface and comes up with his hair flat, and that is the end of it. I go back to watching the towers in the water.

We come in from the pool in the early afternoon and the apartment is cool and dim with the blinds half down against the west light, the aircon cold on the chlorine still drying on my arms, and for a while nothing happens. He showers first and comes out in a pair of my drawstring shorts that have become, without discussion, his, and that sit lower on him than they ever did on me, and lies down on the sofa with one of the books that have started living on my shelves at the end nearest the door — paperbacks with cracked spines, a history of the partition, a novel in a language I cannot read that he is rereading for the third time — and he reads with the stillness of a man who can disappear into a page. I make coffee for myself, two scoops, the machine he tolerates and rarely uses, and I bring the weekend paper to the armchair, and I read the business pages and then the obituaries, which I read everywhere I live as a way of learning what a place considers a life, and the aircon runs its even hum and a delivery bike whines past on the road below and somewhere down the corridor a child is being told, at length and without effect, to put on shoes.

He reads with his knees up and the book against them, and twice he laughs at something on the page, the short laugh, to himself, not for me — and the second time, he reads me the line without lifting his eyes from the page — low and unhurried, the words handed over rather than performed, his voice dropping the half-step it only drops for me. It is a sentence about a railway minister in 1955 and a bridge that was opened a year before it was finished. “They cut the ribbon,” he says, “on a bridge you could not yet cross. And everyone clapped. And then they all went home the long way.” He lets it sit. He looks up to see if it landed, and finding that it did, lets me have the small unguarded smile he gives no one in an office.

A few pages on he goes still in a different way, the way he goes still over the milk. “Here,” he says. “Listen. The minister built a guest house at the bridge for the dignitaries to stay in for the opening. Forty rooms. And then the bridge was late, so the opening was late, so the guest house sat empty two years with a cook kept on, full salary, in case the dignitaries came early.” He shakes his head, delighted and scandalized in equal measure. “Two years a man cooked, every day, for guests who were not coming, so that the day they came it would look as though he had always been cooking.” He looks at the blinds a moment, and I would bet the flat he is watching the cook lay dinner for nobody. “You should put the cook in one of your decks.” Then he goes back into the page and is gone again, and I do not say anything, because the thing did not want a reply, only a witness, and I am the witness, in the chair, with the coffee going to the temperature of the room.

Earlier, still reading, he had reached a foot across the small distance between the sofa and the chair and set it against my ankle, the cool sole of his foot on the side of my foot, and left it there, and gone on reading. He turns a page. I turn a page. The foot stays. Twenty minutes go by like that, two men reading in separate languages with one point of contact at the ankle, and for those twenty minutes I do not want anything I do not already have, and nothing in me is grading anything against the sole of his foot.

Later, when he comes up out of the book, I tell him a deck is the wrong word for what had been on my desk all week. I had a monk. A real one, a Buddhist monk with a condition in his hip that the device people wanted as their case, and so we had built him a courtyard that photographed better than the one he prayed in, and lit it, and staged a doctor’s appointment he had already had, twice, so a camera could have it a third time. I tell him about the consent forms the monk signed with great patience, and the day we lost to the legal team over whether the man could say, on camera, the actual name of the thing in his spine.

Arjun puts the book face-down on his chest to give it his whole attention. “They made a monk audition his own pain,” he says, pleased and appalled in the exact proportions he keeps for things from my world. “For a word he was not allowed to say.” He thinks about it. “Your job is worse than mine, Daniel. I only move the cumin.”

He does not pick the book back up. He comes across the small distance between the sofa and the chair and takes the coffee out of my hand and puts it on the floor and gets me up out of the chair by the wrist, not gently, the way you take a plate to the table, no announcement, because that is where plates go. “The page can wait,” he says, and that is all the explanation I get, and I don’t need one, because I am already standing and already hard and already, God help me, a little embarrassed about how fast, in the middle of a Sunday afternoon with the blinds half down and a child down the corridor still being told about shoes. He wants me. Not the map, not the grip, not the side the grinder goes on. Me, plainly, now, and I am the man who sees everything coming, and I did not see this. The blinds put their stripes on us and move them. Nobody is the dry one; the shorts that are mine and are his come down under a hand that does not ask, and at some point I hear myself, a sound I had not planned and could not have cleared with anyone, and I do not care, and I make it again, louder, on purpose, to see what he does, and what he does is not stop. Nothing from that hour got filed. I have looked. There is no tell to find, no shift in the breath. He wanted me, and it was the best hour of that year, and I spent the next one rearranging the cushions, and if you had asked me that evening what happened on Sunday I would have said we did the market.

We do go to the gallery, in the middle of the afternoon, because it is the one on the list with a clock on it. Gillman Barracks is the old army camp, low colonial blocks the color of weak tea set among rain trees with their canopies grown into one another, the whole place quiet. We walk in from the road under the trees, and the heat sits in the shade differently than it sits in the sun, heavier and stiller. A security guard in a plastic chair nods us through without getting up.

The closing show is in one of the back blocks: large photographs, a Malaysian artist, palm plantations shot so close and so flat that the rows of trees stop being trees and become a pattern, a green going to black at the edges of the frame. The room is cold with the gallery aircon turned down to the temperature art is kept at, and we are the only two in the room but for a girl at a desk by the door. Arjun goes slowly. He stands a long time in front of one print, hands clasped behind him, and I stand a little behind him and watch him look, which I would rather do than look at the photograph: the back of his neck where the collar sits, the two tendons, the place I had my mouth an hour ago and would put it again now, in the cold, with the girl at the desk six feet off, and he is looking at a palm plantation. After a while he says, not turning, “My grandfather managed an estate like this. Not this one. One like it.” He does not say more, and I do not ask, and we move to the next print, and the fact of the grandfather hangs in the cold room with the green going to black, given to me and not explained, and I carry it out into the heat with the rest of the things he has handed me sideways today.

In the gift area by the exit there is a rack of postcards of the show, and I watch him almost buy one and then not — pick it up, turn it over, read the price on the back, set it down with the small economy of a man who grew up watching every rupee and has more than enough money now and will still never quite spend it on a postcard. Outside, the four-o’clock gold has come in low across the lawns. The rain trees fold their leaves. We walk back through it without talking, and it is exactly what I had wanted, exactly what I had lit a gallery for in my head at the pool, and I have it, and Arjun is in it, and neither of us says a word about the light.

On the way back there is a provision shop he likes, a narrow one off the main road with the goods stacked to the ceiling and a fan turning over the till, and he stops without asking whether I want to stop, because he needs curry leaf and the small green chilies and one particular tin he never has to read the label of — a sharp yellow powder whose name I keep failing to retain — that the bigger places stock and then stop stocking the moment he relies on them. The shop smells of dried fish and jute and something at the back gone past ripe, and the fan only moves the smell around, and I stand in the one aisle with my hands in my pockets, because there is nowhere in it to put a man my size. The man knows him. Of course the man knows him. He weighs the chilies by feel, pours them off the pan into a thin pink bag, twists it, and says a number. Arjun says a smaller number. The man says the first number again, gently, and Arjun puts two of the chilies back as a matter of principle, and they settle a cent below where they started, both satisfied.

“You talked him down one cent,” I say.

“I talked him down nothing. I gave him back two chilies he did not want returned and we both got to feel like men.” He lifts the tin of yellow powder, checks the seal with his thumb, sets it in the basket. “My father did this every Sunday of my childhood. The same shop, the same man, the same forty paise that changed nothing. I thought it was about the forty paise. I was twelve before I understood it was the only conversation the two of them had ever had, and they had been having it, happily, for thirty years.” He hands the basket to the man.

“You don’t need all that,” I say, of the curry leaf, which is a whole branch, far more than a man cooks with in a week.

“You always need all that. You take the leaves off the stem and you keep them in the cold and they last. The stem you throw.” He hands me the bag to hold, the bag passed to me without a word, my hands enlisted. “My grandmother kept a plant. On the back step. You took what you needed in the morning and the plant did not mind.” He pays, and pockets the coins the man gives back without counting them, and we go out into the heat with the curry leaf giving off its sharp green smell from the bag in my hand, and the smell is on my fingers for the rest of the night. I keep finding it there.

The fight is about the table.

It starts at five, in the apartment, the two of us showered and the aircon on and the light going amber on the canal, when I say we should think about leaving by six-thirty, and Arjun, on the sofa with the squash racquet across his knees, rewinding the grip tape, says, “Leaving for what.”

“The table. Seven.”

“I thought the table was an option.”

“It was on the list.”

“Everything circled is an option,” he says. “You told me that. In the kitchen. The pen rested itself.”

“I’d like to go to the dinner.”

“Then call them and move it to a Saturday. I want —” He sets the racquet down, flat, on the cushion, which is a thing he does when he is about to be more direct than the racquet permits. “I’ve had a good day. I had the dosa and I had the pool and I got teased in a lift. I would rather not put on a collared shirt and sit in a restaurant being served by a person who has to perform that he’s glad to see us. I would like the day to stay the shape it is.”

“It’s a forty-minute dinner.”

“It’s not the dinner. It’s the getting ready for the dinner. It’s the —” He moves a hand. “It’s that the day has been a day, and you want to put a frame around it before it’s over, no? And I want to find out what the day does on its own.”

“So I’ll cancel it.”

“Cancel it, then.”

“I can’t cancel it at five-thirty. It’s a twelve-table place. If I cancel now they can’t fill it.”

He looks at me.

“They can’t fill it,” he says.

“It’s a small restaurant. They hold a table from the time you book it. If I call at five-thirty and say I’m not coming, that table sits empty at seven, and they’ve turned away —”

“Daniel. You are now defending the restaurant.”

“I’m not defending the restaurant. I’m describing how a reservation works.”

“You’re describing how a reservation works to me.” He is not raising his voice; that is not where Arjun keeps the pressure. The pressure is in the evenness, in the way he can make a sentence completely flat and let the flatness do the work a louder man’s volume does. “We are sitting in your apartment, on a Sunday, and we are having an argument about the moral standing of a restaurant’s empty table. Do you hear it? Do you hear what we’re —”

“I hear it.”

“Then why are we —”

“Because I made a plan,” I say, “and you’ve spent the last hour treating the plan like a symptom, and it’s a table, Arjun, it’s a table at a restaurant, people book them, it is the most ordinary thing a person can —”

“It is the most ordinary thing a person can do,” he says. “Yes. I agree.” And then the edge comes up under the flat, for the first time all afternoon. “You don’t even like these places. You like that you can afford them. Except you can’t, quite, and I am the only one at this table who can do the arithmetic.”

Under the table my stomach says something low, and long, the kind of sound you cannot pass off as the furniture. Kaya toast at seven, one coffee, a dosa at noon I ate too fast to count, and nothing since; the arithmetic he means has been done on the stomach too, since Thursday, and the stomach has just said so, out loud, in the middle of his sentence, and neither of us laughs.

I go looking for what is under it — contempt, or worry, or some piece of my own week he has reached across and picked up — and I find the words and nothing beneath them.

“So why does it feel,” he says, “in this room, right now, like it is not about the table.”

And there it is, set down on the cushion next to the racquet, the sentence neither of us is going to pick up, and we both look at it, and then we both, by a kind of silent treaty, look away from it, because picking it up would require a vocabulary I do not own and Arjun, generously, catastrophically, will not use ahead of me.

We do not talk for a while. He goes back to the grip tape. I sit in the chair by the window and look at the canal, and the aircon runs, and the apartment sits at the one temperature it has ever had, and the silence is the bad kind, the kind with a meter running. Somewhere in it a sentence assembles itself, whole, the way they do when I am paid to make them. It is about the arithmetic. It is accurate, which is the reason it would work, and it would take four seconds to say, and I know what it would do to him at the exact resolution I know what a headline does. I keep it where I can reach it, priced, and I do not spend it. The silence goes on long enough that the light changes.

I take my phone out to the corridor.

The corridor smells of someone’s dinner from the far end and laundry from the near end, and is warm after the apartment, and the phone is slick in my hand before anyone picks up. I call the restaurant. A young man answers, and I say the name the booking is under and that something has come up and we won’t make it tonight, and I brace for I don’t know what; for the table, for the cost of the table, and the young man says, no problem sir, thank you for letting us know, we hope to see you another time, warmly, with no weight on it at all, and hangs up, and I stand in the corridor with the phone in my hand having been forgiven, instantly and completely, for a thing the man on the other end did not even register as a thing. I stay where the call left me. The table was a hundred and sixty Singapore dollars a head before wine. I paid for it on Thursday and Friday with two hawker lunches I did not buy, and I am paying for it now, in the stomach. The skipping has been a quiet routine all spring. $2,850 in rent and a credit-card minimum I am meeting the way a person meets a deadline at five p.m. on a Friday, not the way a person clears a thing. Someone at the far end of the corridor is frying garlic. I stand there and breathe it in as if that counted as dinner.

When I come back in, Arjun does not ask. He heard the shape of it through the door. Nothing in this building is soundproof. He knows I called and knows what I said, and he does not make me narrate it, and he does not say thank you, because thank you would make it a transaction and it is not a transaction, it is a man putting down a thing he has been holding too hard. He looks up from the racquet. He says, “We could get the dosa again.”

“We had the dosa.”

“We could have it again. The uncle will think we have a condition.”

“We do have a condition.”

“We do,” he says, and that is the dissolve, not an apology from either of us, not a single word of repair, just the two of us agreeing, out loud, that there is a condition, and that the condition can be fed dosa twice in a day.

On the second floor Aunty Lim’s door is open, as it is every evening — the grille drawn across and latched, the wooden door behind it swung flat to the wall, and she is at her table with the Chinese paper and a small dish of something, and the canary is covered for the night under a cloth on its cage. She does not get up. She looks at the two of us going down past her grille with the look she gives everything, and then she says, to Arjun, through the bars, “You ate already.” Not a question. He stops. “We’re going for dosa,” he says. “The place in Holland Village, the one with the good sambar. We go most weeks now.” “Again,” she says, as if the frequency were the point. She takes this in. “Good. He is too thin.” The he is me; she does not look at me when she says it; she has decided my nutrition is a matter she will conduct through Arjun, the way she conducts the pool notices. “Last week the dustbin outside his door. Three days. Same bag.” She says it to Arjun, delivering a household report to the one party in it who will act on it. “Inside, what. One box. Cereal.” The word arrives with quiet contempt. “A grown man, cereal for dinner. Aiyoh.” A small shake of the head. “Bring back, don’t bring back, but he should eat.” And she goes back to her paper, the audience over, and we go on down, and Arjun says nothing until the landing, where he says, quietly, “She likes you,” and I say, “She has never once said my name,” and he says, “That is how you know.”

We ride over for it after dark and walk the last stretch from the taxi. The day’s heat has come off the pavement and what is left is the night air, the drains breathing their faint green smell, the frangipani in the planters along the road letting go of the day’s perfume. The shophouses have their grilles half drawn; a man is hosing the apron in front of a closed bakery; a cat the color of dust crosses ahead of us, unhurried, and does not look at us. Arjun walks with his hands in his pockets and his head a little back, looking at the upper stories as he always does, the old plasterwork and the bottle-green louvers and the one window with a birdcage hung outside it, and he says, of no building in particular, “I would have liked to live up there. When I first came. Before I could afford to want things sensibly.” He says it without weight, an offcut of a man. “Up there,” he says again, of the window with the birdcage, “with the bird and the window propped open on a chopstick. I had a room like it when I came. One room, off Serangoon, that gave onto an airwell, and the airwell carried everything — the couple on three who fought about money every Sunday and made up loudly every Sunday night, a boy somewhere practicing the same four bars of piano for a year and never the fifth, a grandmother who shouted the wrong answers at her game shows, somebody’s rooster that nobody on the block would admit to owning.” He is smiling at the upper story, not at me. “I hated it. I tell people now I loved it. In ten more years I will believe I loved it.” He puts his hands back in his pockets. “Even the rooster is halfway to a fond memory, and I hated that rooster personally.” And he walks on, and I take the line and put it away and do not turn to look at him while I do.

We take the dosa at the wrong hour, half past eight, the stall nearly empty, the griddle scraped down and cooling at one end while the man keeps the other end going for the stragglers, and the uncle does look at us, and does say something Arjun lets go by untranslated, and we eat with the right hand, both of us competent now, the second dosa of the day, thinner and more brittle than the morning’s because the batter has been sitting, the edges shattering like a wafer when I tear them, and I eat it fast and with my head down, and I do not stop to be embarrassed about the speed until it is gone and Arjun is looking at me. Somewhere across the city a table for two goes to seven o’clock and then past it with no one in it, a small empty fact in a room I will never see, and is, by the kindness of a young man on a telephone, no one’s fault. The dosa comes to eleven dollars for the two of us. I pay for it because Arjun paid for the morning’s, and I do the small arithmetic without writing it down, and for once it comes out in my favor.

He stays late. When we come up from the dosa he does not pick up his bag, and that is its own small message, the bag left where it is, and I read it and say nothing.

In the dark the aircon is off for him and the racquet keeps its corner and I reach for him, the ordinary reach. He takes my hand where it lands and holds it, flat, against his chest, which is not a no and is not a yes, and for a moment I think it is the prelude to the yes. Then he says, quietly, into the dark, “Not tonight.”

That is all. He does not give a reason. Arjun keeps reasons the way he keeps the other things, in the room he keeps even here, even with me, the door shut without sound. He is not turning away. His hand stays on mine on his chest, and his breathing does not change, and the refusal is not a wall so much as a held line, a thing he has decided in some room I am not in and brought out whole.

I wait for the part of me that should mind. A year ago it would have minded. There would have been a reading run on the held hand, the flat voice, the not tonight — the cost calculated, the body reporting in. The body reports nothing. I lie there with my hand under his and find that I feel less than I expect to feel, and that the not-minding is not peace. It is just quiet, the same quiet the chest went into in the lift. I say, “Okay.” He squeezes my hand once and lets it go. We lie there. The line holds and I do not test it. After a while he sleeps, and I stay awake a while in the dark on my own side of a bed we are both in, keeping the line for him because he asked, and feeling, about the keeping, almost nothing at all.

At some point he woke, and I did not hear him do it. He went home, in the end, the way he went home most Sunday nights. His life was kept in Holland Village — the suits in the wardrobe there, the Monday that started there — and a man could not run a Monday from a borrowed key and a racquet left in someone else’s entryway, and so on Sunday nights, late, he gathered the bag he had brought and not the racquet, never the racquet, and stood at my door, said something ordinary, and went.

That night he stood at the door and looked at the racquet in its corner. Head down, the grip tape freshly wound, ready for a Sunday I could already feel arriving behind this one, the whole rest of them lined up like that, identical, a year of them, more. He said, “Tuesday the pool’s closed. They put the thing in the water.”

“Aunty Lim told me.”

“Aunty Lim told me,” he said. “I’m telling you. There’s a chain of command.” And he laughed the short one, and he left, and I heard the lift come and take him, and the apartment was one man’s again. The aircon ran its even hum. The racquet kept its corner. I stood in the kitchen in the shorts he had worn that afternoon, which were mine again and smelled of him anyway.

I poured a second glass of wine and drank half of it at the counter, and looked at the other half, and wanted it, plainly, the way I had wanted the arm back that first morning, and poured it out. That was the night I made the rule, though it did not need stating for a while: one glass on the nights he goes, because the glass on those nights wants to be two, and the second wants to become a sentence said down a phone, and the morning has to pay for that.

She was right that I never read the notice. There would be a great many more Sundays. I knew that, in the bed afterward — knew it the way you know the aircon will still be running when you wake. What I did not know, and could not have been made to believe by anyone, including Arjun, including the uncle, including Aunty Lim with all her information, was that they were a thing with a number on them. I thought they were weather. I thought they were the climate I had moved to.
